## The Heart of Legacy Coaching

### What You're Really Helping Families Pass On

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

**Brett:** Welcome to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, and I'm here with Tom Conway, who has come alongside more families in this space than probably anyone over the last thirty years. Tom, I'm grateful for your life, your leadership, and your legacy, and I'm glad to be starting this series with you.

If you sat down with most families and asked what they want to pass on, you would hear some version of the same answers. We want to take care of our kids. We want to be wise with what we've built. Those are good answers, and there's usually something underneath them. So Tom, let me start you here. When people hear the word legacy, what do they normally mean, and what are they missing?

**Tom:** When they hear that word, they almost always think about something that happens at the end of life. What they leave behind.

That's part of it, but it's a very small part. Legacy isn't something that happens when you die. It's something being formed right now, every day, whether you're thinking about it or not. It's something you've received from the people who came before you, and it's something you're passing on to the people who come after you.

So the question changes. It's not just what am I going to leave. It becomes what am I building into the lives of the people around me right now.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** That's such a shift, because most people in the advisor world are used to thinking about outcomes. What's the plan, what gets transferred, how do we structure it. All of that matters. But what you're saying is that by the time you get there, a lot has already been set in motion. The relationships, the patterns, the way people think, those are forming long before anything gets transferred.

**Tom:** That's exactly right, and once people see it, it changes how they think.

One of the biggest misunderstandings is that legacy is mainly about money. Money matters, but it's a very small part of it. The most significant legacy you're going to pass on has very little to do with money. It has to do with your faith, your character, your values, and what you model over time.

Because at the end of the day, people don't replicate what you say. They replicate what you live.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And you see that play out. You can sit with two families that look very similar on the surface, similar success, similar resources, and the experience inside those families is completely different. In one there's connection and clarity and purpose. In another there's confusion, maybe tension, maybe distance. And it becomes clear that what's being passed on isn't just financial.

**Tom:** That's right. And what I've found is that when you spend enough time with families, what they're most concerned about usually isn't money. It's the spiritual condition of their kids and their grandkids.

They're thinking about whether their children are going to be grounded, whether they'll make wise decisions, whether they'll have a foundation that holds over time. And that's not just a personal concern. It's a biblical idea.

You see it in Deuteronomy 6, where it talks about loving God with all your heart and intentionally passing that on to your children in the normal rhythms of life. And then in Psalm 78 it extends across generations, one generation telling the next so they would trust God, and then they would teach their children.

So this has always been bigger than one generation.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** That reframes it, because now you're not just helping someone manage money. You're stepping into something with a ripple effect across generations.

**Tom:** That's right. And one way I try to simplify it is to say that every family is passing on two things, whether they realize it or not. They're passing on wealth, and they're passing on wisdom.

The question is not whether that's happening. It is. The question is how. Because if wealth is transferred without wisdom, it can create more problems than it solves.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that's where you see the gap. Most systems are built to move assets. They're not built to prepare people.

**Tom:** That's right. And there are really three parts to this. There's the legacy you've received, the legacy you're going to pass on, and the legacy you're living right now. That last one is the most important, because it's the one you're shaping every day through how you live, how you decide, and how you treat people.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So it's not something we define later. It's something we're already living into.

**Tom:** Exactly. And whether people realize it or not, they're teaching all the time. They're teaching by how they live, how they respond, what they prioritize. Your kids are watching, your grandkids are watching, the people you work with are watching. What they see is what gets passed on. That's why modeling matters so much, because your life becomes the message.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that's where this moves out of theory and into real life. It's happening in everyday decisions and conversations, and over time those moments form patterns, and those patterns become the legacy.

**Tom:** That's right. And when you look at families who do this well, it's not that they're perfect. But you start to notice certain things. There's usually a foundation, often faith. There's a sense of shared direction, even if it's not perfectly defined. You can see it in how they live rather than just what they say. There's some level of communication, a willingness to talk about things. And over time they're intentional about helping the next generation grow into it.

Those things don't just happen. They're developed.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** They're built intentionally. So when you're sitting with a family, what are you really trying to help them move toward?

**Tom:** I try to keep it simple. There are two things I'm hoping for. One is that the family stays connected, that there's harmony there, because if you lose the relationships it really doesn't matter what you did with the money. The other is that what's been built is transferred in a way that actually helps the next generation, that they're prepared and they have some wisdom around it.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So it's not just about success. It's about what that success produces over time.

**Tom:** That's right. And to guide that, we come back to three simple ideas. Helping families get clear on what matters, helping them align their lives with that, and helping them communicate in a way that builds trust.

And at the end of the day this comes back to stewardship. It's not just about what you build. It's about what you do with what you've been given. What a lot of families really want is to be able to look back and know they did this well, to hear, well done, good and faithful servant.

[CUE] CASE STUDY

**Tom:** The financial side stays strong and the relational side drifts. And when the next generation steps in, they're receiving something they don't fully understand. **Brett:** Give me the other version.

**Tom:** Second family has a strong sense of connection. They spend time together, there's communication, there's real care for each other. And because of that, something else takes shape over time. **Tom:** Then he starts smaller than a family meeting. Maybe it's one letter dad writes. Maybe it's one conversation with one child. [CUE] BOTH CENTER

**Tom:** Legacy isn't decided at the end. It's built day by day, over time.

**Brett:** Tom, that's a powerful place to land. And before we ever help another family think about this, it's worth asking personally. Are we being intentional about what we're passing on? If the people closest to us described what's being passed on right now, would it feel intentional, or would it feel more like default?

# SESSION 2

## A Different Way to Lead Families

### Moving from Advice to Guidance

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

**Brett:** Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, I'm with Tom, and we're glad you're here.

Last session we talked about what families are really passing on. Once you realize legacy is more than money, the next question is how you actually help a family navigate it. So Tom, when someone recognizes this is more than a technical conversation, what has to change in how they lead?

**Tom:** One of the first things that changes is how you see your role.

Most advisors are trained to give advice. You look at a situation, you analyze it, you recommend a solution. That works well with technical issues. But when you begin working with families, especially across generations, you're dealing with something more layered. People, relationships, perspectives, history. Those don't respond to answers the same way.

So over time the role shifts. You're not just giving advice. You're guiding a process.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that's where things really shift. Most advisors don't struggle with knowing what to do. They struggle with what to do when the room doesn't respond. You can explain something clearly, even correctly, and still feel like nothing changed.

And it's easy in that moment to think, I need to say this better. But what's really happening is the family hasn't processed it together yet. So the solution isn't better answers. It's a different kind of conversation.

**Tom:** That's right. And one of the first shifts is moving from answers to questions. You're still bringing insight, but instead of leading with answers you're helping draw things out.

There's a verse in Proverbs that talks about the purposes of a person's heart being like deep waters, and someone with understanding draws them out. That's a helpful picture, because you're not putting information into the conversation. You're helping surface what's already there. And a lot of times, what's already there is what matters most.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that changes the posture completely, because instead of filling the space you're creating space. When you slow things down like that, people process differently. They start saying what they're actually thinking, sometimes for the first time. You hear what they're unsure about, what they care about, what hasn't been clear.

**Tom:** Another important shift is how families think about themselves.

The modern view of family leans toward independence. You raise your kids, you prepare them, you send them out. There's a place for that, but it's not the full picture. When you look at it more broadly, especially from a biblical perspective, there's a different emphasis. It's more about interdependence. Multiple generations staying connected, continuing to influence one another, living with a shared sense of purpose over time.

When you see that, it changes how you think about legacy. It's not just something that gets handed off. It's something that continues.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that reframes success. If independence is the goal, then success becomes, they're on their own. If interdependence is the goal, success looks like ongoing connection and influence over time.

**Tom:** And when you look at Scripture there's a promise connected to that. God's design for family was never just one generation. It was always meant to extend beyond, to children and grandchildren and beyond. So what you do today doesn't just affect the present. It carries forward, and that stretches how you think about time.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So now you're not just asking what's the right decision. You're asking what kind of family this is shaping over the next generation or two.

**Tom:** And when you step back, there are four primary reasons families struggle across generations. Most of the time it comes down to communication and trust. That's usually the biggest factor. Then the next generation not being prepared for what they're receiving. Then a lack of clarity about what the family is trying to do together.

And when you look at it honestly, very little of it has to do with the financial plan itself.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** Which is surprising, because that's where most of the focus goes. So you can have a well-built plan and still have a family that isn't aligned.

**Tom:** And underneath those four, you start to see a pattern of where things break down. There's often not much of a foundation, sometimes not much faith. There isn't strong involvement across generations. There's a lack of clarity about what matters. Communication fades and people stop being intentional. The next generation isn't being prepared. And over time there's not a strong sense of stewardship or generosity.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And none of those feel like major issues on their own. But you can see how they build. It's not one big failure. It's a pattern of smaller gaps that never get addressed, and over time those gaps define the direction of the family.

**Tom:** And when you look at families moving in a healthier direction, you see a different pattern. There's usually a foundation, often rooted in faith. There's a sense of shared values, and you can see them lived out rather than just talked about. There's communication, even if it's not perfect. There's intentional preparation of the next generation. And over time, a mindset of stewardship and generosity.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And what stands out is that those things are developed. They don't just happen. They're modeled, reinforced, and repeated over time. That's where leadership plays a role, because somebody has to guide the process.

**Tom:** So when you're helping a family move that direction, a few shifts make the difference. It starts with a foundation, often faith. Then helping them communicate well. Preparing the next generation, not just financially but in how they think and make decisions. Helping them get clear on what matters. Creating space for real conversations. And over time, helping them live with a sense of stewardship and generosity.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So instead of trying to fix one issue, you're helping build a different pattern altogether. And that's what changes the trajectory.

**Tom:** And that's where legacy planning really has two sides. There's the technical side, the structures and strategies and planning. And there's the relational side, how the family communicates, how they make decisions, how they stay connected. Both matter.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And if you ignore one, it shows up in the other. You can have a great plan with a disconnected family, or a connected family without structure. The goal is bringing both together.

**Tom:** And as you grow into this role, you notice a shift in how you lead. At first it shows up in simple ways. You listen more carefully. You ask better questions. You don't feel the need to rush to an answer.

Over time that deepens. You start to recognize patterns in families, how they think, how they decide, how they relate to one another. You begin helping them think together rather than individually.

And as that develops, your perspective widens. You're not just focused on the conversation in front of you. You're seeing how decisions affect relationships and how those patterns carry forward.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So it's not just a skill shift. It's a perspective shift.

**Tom:** And eventually that leads to a multigenerational mindset. You're not just thinking about the person in front of you. You're thinking about their kids and their grandkids. And that changes how you lead.

[CUE] CASE STUDY

**Brett:** Tom, give me a picture of an advisor making that shift, because I think it's easier to describe than to do.

**Tom:** I know an advisor who had a client relationship of about fifteen years. Good relationship, well managed, no complaints anywhere.

He came to a workshop and afterward he told me he had realized something uncomfortable. In fifteen years he had never asked that client a single question that wasn't about the portfolio.

**Brett:** Not because he didn't care.

**Tom:** Not at all. It just was never his job as he understood it. So he went back and did one thing differently. In the next review meeting, before he opened the file, he asked how the family was doing.

**Brett:** What happened?

**Tom:** The meeting ran an hour longer than it was supposed to. And most of it was about a son who was struggling, which the advisor had never heard a word about in fifteen years. Nothing in the plan changed that day. The relationship changed that day.

[CUE] SCENARIOS

**Brett:** Two scenarios, Tom.

An advisor tries this and the client shuts it down. Says something like, let's just stick to the numbers.

**Tom:** He sticks to the numbers. And then he asks again in six months, in a different way. Some clients need to see that you'll respect the boundary before they'll let you past it. Pushing on the first no is how you get a permanent no.

**Brett:** Second one. An advisor works in a firm where none of this is billable and his manager is watching his production numbers.

**Tom:** Then he starts with the clients where it's easiest and lets the results make the argument for him. He doesn't need permission to ask a client how his children are doing. And when a family deepens the relationship and the next generation stays, nobody in that firm is going to ask him to stop.

[CUE] BOTH CENTER

**Tom:** You're not just thinking about the person in front of you. You're thinking about their kids, and their grandkids.

**Brett:** Tom, fifteen years and one question. That stays with you.

Here's the question to sit with. Are you primarily giving answers, or are you helping families think and move forward together? Because the way you lead that conversation shapes what happens next.

In the next session we're going to talk about how families begin to define what matters most, their values, their priorities, and how those take shape over time.

## What Matters Most to Families

### Helping Clients Clarify Values and Priorities

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

**Brett:** Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we're grateful you're with us.

Last session we talked about moving from advice to guidance. Every family operates from a set of values whether they've defined them or not. Those values shape decisions, relationships, and direction over time. But when they aren't clear, decisions start to feel disconnected. One decision makes sense on its own and doesn't line up with the next one.

So part of what you're doing is bringing clarity to something already there that hasn't been expressed. And when that happens, it changes how a family thinks and how they move forward.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So it's less about creating something new and more about uncovering what's already driving things underneath the surface. Without that clarity, a family can feel like they're making progress without moving in a consistent direction. Decisions get made, but they don't build on each other. And over time you see confusion, or even tension, not because people disagree but because they're not anchored to the same things.

**Tom:** That's right. And when you step back, there are two core goals sitting underneath most family conversations, whether they've named them or not.

One is maintaining family harmony. Relationships, unity, how people stay connected over time. The other is wise stewardship and transfer of wealth, how resources are handled and passed on.

And those two don't always move together automatically. Without clarity, they can pull in different directions.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** That's a helpful way to see it, because most families are trying to do both and haven't named it. So they lean one direction without realizing it. Sometimes everything gets centered on preserving harmony, even when that creates ambiguity or avoids hard conversations. Other times everything gets centered on the financial side, structure and efficiency and outcomes, even when it creates tension relationally.

So what looks like disagreement on the surface is often deeper misalignment underneath.

**Tom:** Exactly. And that's where clarity becomes so important.

There's a passage in Matthew that says where your treasure is, there your heart will be also. That points to the connection between what we value and how we live. If values aren't clear, priorities drift. And when priorities drift, decisions become inconsistent. But when values are clear, they anchor decisions.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So clarity becomes a filter. Instead of evaluating every decision in isolation, you're measuring it against something.

**Tom:** And one of the ways we help families think about this is a simple framework. Values, mission, and vision.

Values define what matters most. They shape how a family lives and what they prioritize. Mission describes what they're about, how those values take shape in action. And vision looks ahead. It answers where they're going and what they're building toward.

When those three come together, they create clarity that guides decisions.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that's what makes it usable. Without something like that, values stay abstract. People might say they matter, but they're not actually shaping how decisions get made. This connects the dots. It gives a family a way to say, here's what matters, here's how that shows up, and here's where we're headed.

**Tom:** And when clarity begins to take shape, you see a shift. There's a passage in Proverbs that says the beginning of wisdom is this, get wisdom, and whatever you get, get understanding. Clarity is part of that understanding.

Without it things feel scattered. With it, things come into focus. It doesn't mean every decision becomes easy, but decisions become more consistent.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And without that clarity, you feel the opposite. Decisions take longer. Conversations feel repetitive. People hesitate, not because they don't care but because they're not sure what they're aiming at. So instead of moving forward with confidence, families second-guess or default to whatever feels easiest in the moment. That's where you see drift. Not intentional, just gradual.

**Tom:** And that's where planning begins to change. When clarity is in place, planning becomes more effective because it's built on something solid. Without clarity, planning still happens, but it's often disconnected and it doesn't hold over time. With clarity around values, mission, and vision, planning becomes an extension of that.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that's important, because if you skip that step you end up with language that sounds right and doesn't mean anything to the people in the room. **Tom:** And there are a few common challenges. Sometimes families haven't taken the time to step back and reflect. Sometimes there's an assumption that everyone already sees things the same way. And often there's a tendency to focus on immediate decisions instead of the values that should guide those decisions.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So they go straight to structure without building the foundation. And when that happens, even good decisions feel disconnected.

**Tom:** And when clarity develops, you can see it. There's more consistency in how people talk about what matters. There's more shared understanding. Decisions begin to reflect those values. There's less confusion and more alignment.

**Tom:** Clarity doesn't just happen. It has to be created.

# SESSION 4

## Creating Alignment in the Family

### Getting Everyone on the Same Page

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

**Brett:** Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, I'm with Tom, and we're glad you've joined us.

Last session we talked about clarifying what matters most. But clarity at an individual level doesn't automatically create alignment at a family level. You can have people in the same family who genuinely care about the same things and still not be on the same page. So Tom, when you're helping a family get there, what are you really helping them move toward?

**Tom:** You're helping them move from individual perspectives to shared understanding.

Every person in a family brings their own experiences, their own way of seeing things, their own assumptions about what matters. That's natural. It's part of what makes a family what it is. But when those perspectives stay separate, when they're never brought into the same conversation, it creates misalignment even when everyone's intentions are good.

So alignment is really about helping people understand each other well enough to move forward together.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So it's not about removing differences. It's about making them visible and workable. Because what happens is people assume they're aligned simply because they haven't disagreed yet. And then a real decision shows up, something that requires direction, and that's when you realize people were operating from different assumptions all along.

**Tom:** That's right. Alignment doesn't mean everyone thinks the same. It means there's enough shared understanding that people can move in the same direction.

There's a passage in Amos that says, do two walk together unless they have agreed to do so. That idea of walking together captures it. It's about direction.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that's a helpful distinction, because a lot of people hear alignment and immediately think agreement. What you're describing is more foundational. If the understanding isn't there, agreement tends to be shallow or temporary.

**Tom:** That's exactly right, and it's an important shift for families. Alignment starts with understanding, not agreement.

If you move too quickly to agreement, you miss what people are actually thinking, and what looks like agreement on the surface may not hold. When you take time to build understanding first, agreement is much stronger when it comes.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So rushing to agreement can actually work against alignment, because it shortcuts the process. What you end up with is people nodding along in the moment while still holding different interpretations underneath. And those differences show up later, usually at the worst time.

**Tom:** That's right. And one reason it happens is that people assume alignment without confirming it. They assume others see things the same way. Or they assume that because something was said once, it was understood the same way by everyone.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And that's where things quietly drift, because no one is intentionally trying to be misaligned. There just hasn't been enough space for people to express how they're thinking.

**Tom:** Exactly. I worked with a family where this became very clear. The parents felt they had been clear about what mattered and where things were going. But when the next generation shared how they were thinking, it was obvious they had interpreted things very differently.

They all cared about the family. They all had good intentions. They weren't actually aligned.

So we created space for conversation, not to force agreement but to build understanding. Each person had the opportunity to share how they were thinking, what mattered to them, how they saw the future. And something important became visible. There were areas of alignment and areas where they were not aligned, and now they could see both clearly.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** That's a big moment, because before that everything is assumed. Once it's expressed, you can work with it. And that's where alignment becomes real, not because everything is resolved but because everything is understood.

**Tom:** And that's where listening becomes so important. Not just hearing words, but really listening to understand how someone else is thinking.

There's a principle in Scripture about being quick to listen and slow to speak, and it applies directly. You're creating space for each person's perspective to be heard, and helping reflect it back so others can understand it. That kind of listening builds understanding, and understanding leads to alignment.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

### Why Communication Breaks Down and How to Guide It

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

**Brett:** Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we're grateful you're back with us.

Last session we talked about creating alignment. Alignment sounds good in theory, but in practice it requires conversation, and not just surface-level conversation. So Tom, why do the conversations that matter most so often become the hardest ones for families to have?

**Tom:** A big part of it is that those conversations are personal.

When you're talking about values, expectations, roles, or the future, you're not just talking about ideas. You're talking about how people see themselves, how they see each other, and sometimes what they've assumed for a long time.

Because of that, people become cautious. Or they avoid the conversation altogether. Not because they don't care, but because it matters.

There's a passage in Proverbs that says the tongue has the power of life and death. Words carry weight, and people sense that even when they don't say it. So when the stakes feel high, communication either becomes very careful, or it breaks down.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So the importance of the conversation actually raises the pressure around it. It's not just, let's talk this through. It's, if this doesn't go well it could affect relationships, trust, even how people see each other going forward. And because of that, people either pull back or they move too quickly and react.

**Tom:** That's right. And there are a few common ways communication breaks down. Sometimes it's avoidance, people just don't bring things up. Sometimes it stays at the surface, conversations happen but they don't go deep enough to matter. And sometimes it turns into tension, where people react quickly instead of thinking carefully.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** And once that pattern starts, it reinforces itself. If a conversation gets avoided, it's easier to avoid the next one. If a conversation goes poorly, people remember, and they're less willing to step into another one. So over time it's not one conversation. It becomes a pattern.

**Tom:** And underneath that, there are a few reasons those conversations don't happen at all. Sometimes people assume things are already understood. Sometimes they want to protect others or avoid discomfort, so they keep things to themselves. And sometimes there's uncertainty about how to even start, so it gets delayed.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So it's not usually resistance. It's hesitation. People are trying to do the right thing, but without a clear way to approach it they default to not saying anything. And over time that creates a gap, not because anyone is keeping things from each other but because important things aren't being shared.

**Tom:** That's right. And sometimes it shows up as people being unintentionally left out. Important decisions get made, or plans get put in place, and not everyone understands what's happening or why. That creates confusion, and over time it can create mistrust.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So even if the intent is good, maybe to simplify things or avoid complexity, it can have the opposite effect. Because when people don't understand what's happening, they fill in the gaps on their own. And those assumptions aren't always accurate.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So communication isn't just about sharing information. It's about building connection. And when that happens consistently, it strengthens relationships, which makes future conversations easier rather than harder.

**Tom:** And that's where guidance becomes important. When a conversation is guided well, it comes back to a few things. Creating space for each person to speak. Listening without immediately responding. And slowing the pace so people can think instead of react.

There's a principle in James about being quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to become angry. That captures the posture these conversations need.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

**Brett:** So instead of trying to control the outcome, you're shaping the environment. You're creating a space where people feel heard, where they're not rushed, and where the goal is understanding rather than just resolution.
