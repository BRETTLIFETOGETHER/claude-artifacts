%%DAY%%Day 1
%%TITLE2%%Five Areas
%%SUBT%%People don't focus it
%%VIDEO%%Session 1.1
%%VERSE%%"Teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom."
%%REF%%Psalm 90:12

A lot of times when people think of legacy, it's a very big, broad term, and people don't focus it. So we've come up with five areas that we think are really critical for people to think about.

The first is your personal legacy. What do you want to be remembered for? And that includes your spiritual legacy. What is your spiritual legacy going to be?

The second is your family legacy. Tell me about your kids, your grandkids, your parents if they're still alive. What has your family legacy been, and what do you want it to be going forward?

The third is your financial legacy, and this is the wealth transfer part. What's going to be the inheritance you give your kids or grandkids that's going to be a blessing and not a curse? I raise the possibility of a curse on purpose, because you leave a lot of money to the wrong people and it's not going to benefit their life. It's going to have a negative impact.

The fourth is your business legacy, if you own one. What's your succession plan? What's going to happen to the business? Are you going to sell it? Pass it to your kids? And what about continuity? Say you're in a car wreck and you're in the hospital for a month. Who's running the business when you're not there?

And then lastly, and really importantly, your charitable legacy. What do you want to do for the Kingdom of God, both now and at the end of life?

Now notice what the five are actually doing. Legacy on its own is too squishy. Ask a person what they want their legacy to be and you get something broad and warm that nobody can act on, and everyone walks away from the conversation feeling like something happened when nothing did. I've sat in a lot of those meetings. They're pleasant and they change nothing.

Five gives a family somewhere to stand. Each one has an answer, and each answer can be written down and checked against what's actually in place. That's the difference between a sentiment and a plan.

Here's what happens when nobody does this. A family works hard for forty years and ends up with an arrangement that covers the financial area thoroughly, touches the business area partially, and never addresses the other three at all. Not because those three don't matter to them. Because nobody ever named them as separate things, so there was never a moment where their absence became visible. The plan looks complete because every question anybody asked got answered.

And as you talk through the five, other ideas come up in a family's mind that never would have surfaced otherwise. That's a good sign, and it's worth saying out loud that it happens. We're not trying to limit anybody to five. We're trying to get them focused enough that the conversation can move.

Now do the harder part, which is to run the five on yourself before you run them on anybody. You already know which one you'd score high. You probably also know which one you've been leaving alone, and it's likely the same one you skip with clients, though you'd have told yourself the reason was theirs rather than yours.

The psalm asks God to teach us to number our days. That's not a morbid request. It's the recognition that a life is finite and has parts, and that wisdom comes from looking at the parts squarely instead of at the whole thing all at once.

Nobody gains a heart of wisdom by considering their life in general.

%%DAY%%Day 2
%%TITLE2%%Clarity Is the Most Important Step
%%SUBT%%This is what I really want
%%VIDEO%%Session 1.2
%%VERSE%%"Commit to the Lord whatever you do, and he will establish your plans."
%%REF%%Proverbs 16:3

People want to have something happen out of their life. So what we're trying to do is quantify that, or understand what it is, and then see whether we're actually working toward making it happen.

I just think clarity is the most important step. If you can help a person decide, this is what I really want, this is not what I want, I may be playing around over here but it's really not my passion, my passion is over here, then we've done a really good job.

Read that phrase again, because it's the sharpest thing in the sentence. Playing around over here.

That isn't laziness and it isn't failure. That's a serious, capable person spending real years on something that turns out not to be the thing, because nobody ever asked him to hold the two side by side. He isn't confused. He's unexamined, which is different and much more common.

Brett describes the difference as transactional versus transformative. Most conversations stay at the surface. People talk about strategies and decisions and investments, and nobody slows down long enough to ask what's actually driving all of it. That's not because the people in the room are shallow. It's because nothing in the structure of a normal meeting makes room for the other question, so it never gets asked.

And so year after year the conversation stays where it started. The client keeps making sound decisions inside a frame nobody has examined. The returns are fine. The documents are current. And the whole apparatus is pointed somewhere the client never actually chose.

I've watched what that costs, and it usually shows up late. A man in his seventies looks back and can name three or four things he spent a decade on that he'd have handled differently if anybody had pressed him early. He isn't bitter about it. He's just clear now in a way that would have been worth a great deal more at fifty.

That's what you're for. Not to supply the answer. To create the moment where he separates the two, while there's still time for the separation to matter.

What clarity does is give a person a name for the thing they've been circling. And once it has a name, every decision downstream gets easier, because there's finally something to measure against. Without it, each decision has to be evaluated on its own, which is exhausting and produces drift.

Ask this of yourself first. There is something in your practice you keep doing because you've always done it, that has nothing to do with what you'd say your work is for. You could name it right now if you let yourself. Most advisors can, and most don't, because naming it starts a conversation with yourself that will take a while to finish.

The proverb says commit your work and your plans will be established. Established is a strong word. Not launched, not attempted, not begun. Made to stand.

A plan stands when the person who made it can say plainly what it's for.

%%DAY%%Day 3
%%TITLE2%%If It's Not in Your Documents
%%SUBT%%Whatever it says is where the money is going to go
%%VIDEO%%Session 1.3
%%VERSE%%"Write down the revelation and make it plain on tablets."
%%REF%%Habakkuk 2:2

Even when people get clarity, if it's not reflected in your documents, it doesn't matter what you say.

You can say, I really want to do this, or I really want to do that. If it's not in your documents, it means nothing. Because when you're dead, they're going to pull out those documents, and whatever it says is where the money is going to go.

Sit with that, because it's the reason clarity by itself isn't enough. When you're gone, the document speaks and you don't. Every conversation you had, every intention you carried for forty years, every thing you meant to get around to, is replaced by whatever a lawyer wrote in a room you may only half remember.

If those two things disagree, the paper wins. Every time, without exception, and with no one present to explain the difference.

So the plan is incomplete if we don't dig into where you are right now and make adjustments as necessary so it aligns with what you said you wanted.

Here's how the gap forms, and it's almost never carelessness. A document gets drawn up for a situation that's real at the time. Then a child grows up, or a business quadruples, or a marriage happens, or someone dies, and each of those changes the picture. Nobody goes back, because going back requires somebody to raise it and there's no natural occasion to raise it. Ten years pass. The document still says what it said, and it now describes a family that no longer exists.

Brett tells a story that shows the other side of this. He talked with Dan Cathy, the president of Chick-fil-A, who had handed the company to his son and taken up projects in underdeveloped parts of downtown Atlanta, places that could use his leadership more than his money. Cathy told him his work at the company wasn't his day job anymore. This was his day job. This was his passion.

That's a man whose arrangements caught up with what he actually wanted. Most people feel the disconnect for years and never close it, because feeling it and changing a document are two entirely different acts, and only one of them requires an appointment.

You can have great advisors, good investments, everything organized, and the question still comes back to why we're doing all of this.

Now turn it on yourself, because this one lands closer than most. You have documents. You almost certainly signed them at a point when your children were younger or your practice was smaller or your convictions about giving were different. Ask yourself what they'd do if they executed tomorrow, and whether any part of that is something you would not choose.

Most advisors have never done that exercise on their own file. It takes an hour.

Habakkuk is told to write down the revelation and make it plain, so that somebody who wasn't there can pick it up and carry it. Not a private impression. Not a settled conviction. A tablet, legible to a stranger.

Your intentions are not a legacy. Your documents are.

%%DAY%%Day 4
%%TITLE2%%You Retire to Something Else
%%SUBT%%A lot of people who retire just don't live very long
%%VIDEO%%Session 1.4
%%VERSE%%"For we are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance for us to do."
%%REF%%Ephesians 2:10

I have a client who's leaving his company in the next year, and he's trying to figure out what he's going to do next.

You've heard the expression. You don't retire from something, you retire to something else. I think that's really important. A lot of people who retire just don't live very long, because they've got no purpose. The people we're working with, we're trying to help them retire to something they can use for the rest of their life.

That's not a motivational line, and I don't say it lightly. I've watched it happen more than once. A man leaves the thing that gave his days their shape, there's nothing waiting on the other side, and within a few years he's gone. Not because he was weak. Because purpose was doing more work in his health than anybody around him understood, including him.

And there's a process to discerning what comes next. It takes more than one conversation and more than one week. It may not be obvious in the moment, but over time it clarifies, and the clarifying is what the conversations are for.

Here's what you're up against. When a man in his sixties is asked what he'll do next, the default answer is already loaded and it's some version of Florida and golf. It's not a bad answer. It's just an answer he inherited rather than chose, and it does very little with a life God has spent four decades preparing. If you don't ask a second question, the default is what he'll take, because it's the only option anybody has put in front of him.

This is where you get to the heart of a client. Not just what they're doing now, but what they believe, what matters to them, what they feel responsible for, and what they've been entrusted with.

Part of that is asking them to think personally. What do you really want to be remembered for? What do you want people to say about you when you're not here anymore? Those questions clarify what matters underneath everything else, and they do it faster than any amount of goal setting, because standing at the end and looking back sorts what mattered from what was merely urgent.

You're not forcing an answer. You're helping them recognize what's already there that they've actually done. And once that's clear, it starts to shape the direction.

Watch for the spouse in this conversation too. A man planning his next season is very often planning around his wife's vision rather than his own, because she supported his for thirty years and he knows it. She's usually sitting right there with something specific in mind that nobody has ever asked her to say out loud.

The passage says the works were prepared in advance. Not invented at sixty-five. Prepared, which means the second assignment was already there, waiting, and the only question is whether anybody helps a person find it.

Somebody has to ask. It's almost never the person themselves.

%%DAY%%Day 5
%%TITLE2%%Three Years From Now
%%SUBT%%That's why we're asking you that question
%%VIDEO%%Session 1.5
%%VERSE%%"To answer before listening, that is folly and shame."
%%REF%%Proverbs 18:13

People need to reset from time to time. So I often ask this.

If we're meeting three years from now, and you were to look back on your life over the past three years, what would you like to have seen take place so that you'd feel better three years from now than you do right now?

People say, well, that's a really deep question. I need to think about that.

Well, that's why we're asking you that question. We want you to think about it. Because that helps you see what's really in your heart that you want to accomplish.

Notice what the question does mechanically. Ask a person what their goals are and you get this year's projects, because that's what's in front of them. Move them three years out and ask them to look backward, and something else surfaces, usually a relationship or an unfinished thing they've been carrying quietly and haven't mentioned to anyone.

The reaction is the proof it's working. When somebody tells you they need time with it, that's not resistance. That's a person who has just been asked something no one has asked them, and I'd rather have the answer that comes after a week than the one that comes in ten seconds.

But asking well is only half of it, and the other half is where most of us fall down. Listening is really important, and it's one of the skills you need for this kind of work. You've got to be able to listen, not to judge. You're listening for understanding. You're understanding why they say this or say that. It's almost what's behind what they're saying.

Most professional listening is a search for the place where you can be useful. You track what someone says while assembling your response, and the second you have something worth contributing, your attention shifts from them to it. That's not a character flaw. It's what expertise does to a person, and every advisor I know does it, myself included.

What it costs you is the thing underneath. A client says he's worried about his son's readiness. The content is a question about trust structures. What's behind it might be that he doesn't respect his son and has never admitted that to anyone, or that he sees himself in the boy and it frightens him. You'll never hear any of that if you're listening for the place to answer.

Then with a couple there's another layer. You want to know what the other spouse thinks about the desires the first one expressed. Usually a lot of men, when it comes to the end, want to help their wife accomplish what she wants, because she's basically supported them in their vision, and now they want to help her do hers.

She's often sitting right there with something specific in mind that nobody has ever asked for. And she won't volunteer it, because after thirty years of not being asked, people stop expecting the question.

The proverb calls answering before listening folly and shame, which is stronger language than we'd use. But it names something true. Answering early feels like competence, and functions as deafness.

Before you take the Three-Year Question anywhere, answer it yourself. You can't take a family somewhere you've never been.

%%DAY%%Day 6
%%TITLE2%%Who Am I?
%%SUBT%%What really matters to me
%%VIDEO%%Session 2.1
%%VERSE%%"Search me, God, and know my heart; test me and know my anxious thoughts."
%%REF%%Psalm 139:23

You're helping them step back and think about their life in a more intentional way.

People are moving forward. They're busy. They're responding to what's in front of them, but they haven't really taken the time to reflect on the deeper questions. Who am I? What really matters to me? What am I hoping to do with my life?

And until there's clarity there, it's difficult for them to contribute clearly to the family. They're still making career decisions, financial decisions, family decisions, but they're doing it without a real sense of direction underneath it. So over time, those decisions don't really connect to the rest of what the family is trying to do.

That last sentence is the whole cost, and it's worth slowing down on. Every one of those decisions was defensible when it got made. The career move made sense. The purchase made sense. The way they handled a child made sense at the time. What's missing isn't judgment. It's that nothing underneath was holding them together, so twenty years of good individual decisions add up to a direction nobody would have chosen.

Most people don't stop long enough to ask those kinds of questions. And even the ones who start it sometimes don't finish it, because they've got no accountability to get there. But to never think about it at all is worse, because then there's no hope of engaging it or making it a reality in their life.

So where do you begin? One of the most important things in everything we do is asking questions. You ask questions that help them slow down and think. And you give them time to answer. You don't just move to the next question.

What has shaped your life? What experience has influenced how you think? What matters to you most right now?

As they begin to answer, they start to see patterns. You're not giving them answers. You're just helping them discover what's already there.

Notice that this has to come before the family conversation, not after. Put five people in a room who have never each done this work alone, and you'll have five people hearing the same questions from five completely different internal places. One is clear. One is still figuring it out. One isn't sure what any of this is about. That difference doesn't announce itself in the meeting. It shows up months later in how people respond and what they decide.

Now turn it around. You are somebody's individual too. You've been moving forward, busy, responding to what's in front of you. And you probably haven't sat down with those three questions in a serious way in a long time, because nobody has ever asked them of you and there's no natural occasion to ask them of yourself.

The psalm is the model, and it's a harder prayer than it looks. David doesn't ask for encouragement or confirmation or a general sense that things are fine. He asks to be searched, and he specifically invites examination of the anxious thoughts, which are the ones we keep moving in order to avoid.

You're about to ask a lot of people to be examined. Start with the request being made of you.

%%DAY%%Day 7
%%TITLE2%%Not Living in Alignment With It
%%SUBT%%That's where the gap shows
%%VIDEO%%Session 2.2
%%VERSE%%"Do not merely listen to the word, and so deceive yourselves. Do what it says."
%%REF%%James 1:22

Most people are clear when they answer those questions. But they're not always living in alignment with it. That's where the gap shows, between what they say matters and how they're actually living.

You might say, I want to be remembered as somebody who was present with my family. But when you look at their life, most of the time is going somewhere else. They're more interested in work than they are at home.

Or you might say, I want to be known as a generous person. But then you look at how they're using their resources, and it's not showing up that this is a generous person in any way.

That's not a failure. That's just helping them understand and get clarity about where they are compared to where they say they want to be.

I want to be careful here, because advisors get this wrong in both directions. Some see the gap and turn it into an indictment, and they lose the client in one sentence. Others see it just as clearly and never mention it, because mentioning it feels like overstepping. So the client keeps drifting, politely, with a professional in the room who could see it the whole time.

The way through is neither. It's a mirror. Brett puts it well, that this stops being a conversation and becomes a mirror you're holding up. People don't ask themselves these questions on any given day. But when they're offered by someone who's been invited into that space, or who's already there in a trusted relationship, they can start to see the difference between what they say matters and what their life is actually reflecting.
