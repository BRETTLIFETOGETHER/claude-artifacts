Writing a Good Prompt
The Problem

Most prompts fail
before the work begins

AI does not read minds. It reads instructions. When the instruction is thin, the result is thin, and most people blame the tool when the real issue is the ask. Nearly every disappointing answer traces back to one of four gaps.

1

It lacks context

The model is handed a task with none of the background a capable assistant would need to do it well.

2

It is vague

Wishes stand in for specifics. "Make it good" is not a direction anyone, human or machine, can follow.

3

It never defines success

Without a picture of what "done" looks like, there is no way to land the result on target.

4

It gives no rules or examples

Tone, format, and constraints are left to chance, so the output drifts away from what you pictured.

The Framework

The Three-Part
Prompt Framework

A good prompt moves in order, the same order a wise leader uses when handing off important work. Set the stage, define the task, then specify the rules. Follow the sequence and clarity takes care of itself.

01

Set the Stage

Tell the model who it is, what world it is working in, and what you are ultimately after. A clear stage shapes everything that follows.

rolecontextobjective
02

Define the Task

Name the specific work. Provide the material it needs to do that work. Then describe what a finished result actually looks like.

detailsinputsdesired outcome
03

Specify the Rules

Set the guardrails. Show the shape you want. One good example is worth a paragraph of explanation, so give the model something to match.

The Foundation

Built on four
core competencies

The framework is simple to follow because it rests on four habits of mind, the four D's, that have always marked wise leadership and good delegation. Learn these, and you will write a better prompt without thinking about the steps.

D

Delegation

Knowing exactly what you are handing off, and why. You cannot ask well for what you have not yet decided. Clarity begins in your own mind before it reaches the page.

D

Description

Giving clear, sufficient detail and context. The model can only work with what you make plain, so the more honest and specific your description, the closer the result.

D

Discernment

Judging what matters, what is missing, and what a good result actually looks like. Wisdom is knowing which details to include and which to leave out.

D

Diligence

Reviewing, refining, and improving the result. The first draft is the beginning of the work, not the end. Faithful effort turns a fair answer into a fine one.

See It in Practice

The same request,
asked two ways

The difference between a frustrating answer and a useful one is rarely the tool. It is the prompt. Here is one ordinary request, written first as a wish and then as an instruction.

The WishWeak
Make me a website about prompt writing.

→It names a topic and nothing else. No role, no audience, no purpose, no shape. The model has to guess, and guesses rarely match what you pictured.

The InstructionStrong
Role: You are a senior web designer and clear-spoken copywriter who builds calm, premium teaching pages. Context: The page teaches one lesson, how to write a good AI prompt, to non-technical families, advisors, and leaders. Task: Design and write a single landing page that explains a three-part prompt framework and lets a visitor practice it. Rules: Warm, clear, and unhurried. No hype or jargon. Elegant serif headings, generous spacing, a navy and gold palette. Format: One page, a hero, the framework in three parts, a worked example, and a simple prompt builder. Example: Tone like a wise mentor explaining something important to a friend, plain and encouraging. Success: A first-time visitor understands the framework in one read and writes a better prompt before they leave.

→Same idea, fully staged. The model now knows who to be, who it serves, what to make, and how to know it is finished.

        A Word on Wisdom
        
New tools.
The same old wisdom.

        
Clarity is not a technical skill. It is a stewardship, a way of honoring the people and the work you are responsible for.

        
At LifeTogether, the conviction has always been simple: we are managers, not owners, of everything entrusted to us, including our time, our words, and now our tools. A new technology does not change that calling. It only gives it a new language to speak.

        
Writing a good prompt asks the same things wise leadership has always asked. Know what you are handing off. Describe it plainly. Judge what matters. Then return to the result with care and make it better. Delegation, description, discernment, and diligence are not new ideas dressed up for a new machine. They are old habits of a faithful manager, applied to a tool that rewards them more than most.

        
Used with wisdom, AI can give you back hours for the work that truly needs you. Used carelessly, it multiplies confusion. The difference, as it has always been, is the clarity and the conscience of the person giving the instruction.

        — The LifeTogether teaching tradition

          Resource · Library
          
Example Prompt Library

        

        

          
        

      

      

        
Six prompts built on the three-part framework. Copy one, change the details to fit your situation, and send. Anything in brackets is yours to replace.


        

          
A Welcome Email
Communication

          
Role: You are a warm, plain-spoken writing assistant for ministry and team leaders.
Context: I lead a six-week small group for twelve adults who are new to the topic.
Task: Draft a short welcome email to send the week before we begin.
Details: We meet Tuesday evenings; the first night is light and conversational; no one is ever asked to share private numbers.
Rules: Warm, never salesy or preachy. Short sentences. No jargon.
Format: About 150 words, with a clear subject line and a simple sign-off.
Success: A first-timer feels welcomed, not recruited.

        


        

          
A One-Page Lesson Summary
Teaching

          
Role: You are a careful curriculum editor who writes for busy volunteers.
Context: I have a 45-minute teaching session and need a leader's one-pager.
Task: Summarize the session into a single page a volunteer can lead from.
Details: [Paste the session outline or transcript here.]
Rules: Plain language. Keep the original's meaning and invent nothing.
Format: A big idea, three talking points, two discussion questions, one closing line.
Success: A first-time leader could run the session from this page alone.

        


        

          
A Hard Email, Said Kindly
Communication

          
Role: You are a thoughtful communications coach.
Context: A volunteer missed two commitments and I need to address it without discouraging them.
Task: Draft a brief, kind email that names the issue and invites a conversation.
Rules: Direct but gracious. No guilt and no lecture. Assume good intent.
Format: Under 120 words, ending with one clear next step.
Success: They feel respected and know exactly what to do next.

        


        

          
A Launch Plan
Planning

          
Role: You are an organized project planner.
Context: We launch a new study series in eight weeks and I am the only person on it.
Task: Build a simple week-by-week plan from now to launch.
Details: We need printed guides, a sign-up page, leaders recruited, and a promo email.
Rules: Realistic for one person. Group tasks by week. Flag anything with a long lead time.
Format: A short table — week, focus, and the two or three tasks that matter most.
Success: I can see the whole runway at a glance and know what to do first.

        


        

          
A Devotional Reflection
Writing

          
Role: You are a gentle devotional writer.
Context: I am writing a short daily reading on contentment for a general adult audience.
Task: Draft one day's reflection of about 200 words.
Details: Anchor it in one short Scripture and one everyday, relatable moment.
Rules: Warm and honest, never preachy or sentimental. One idea, well said.
Format: A title, the reading, and one question to sit with.
Success: A reader feels met where they are and carries one thing into the day.

        


        

          
A Meeting Recap
Productivity

          
Role: You are a precise note-taker.
Context: I just finished a planning call and need to send a recap to four people.
Task: Turn my rough notes into a clear recap with owners and dates.
Details: [Paste your rough notes here.]
Rules: Keep only what was decided or assigned. No filler. Do not invent details.
Format: Decisions, action items (owner and date), and open questions.
Success: Everyone knows what they own and by when, in under a minute of reading.

        

      

    

  


  
  

    

    

      

        

          Resource · Guide
          
Facilitator Guide

        

        

          
          
        

      

      

        
You do not need to be a technology expert to lead this lesson. You need to help people slow down and ask well. Here is everything to run it in about 45 minutes.


        
The goal

        
By the end, each person can name the three parts of a good prompt and write one of their own. That is the whole win. Understanding beats coverage, so do not rush to get through every point.


        
What you'll need

        
    
          
  • A screen showing the framework, or printed copies of it.
    • 
          
  • The worksheet, one per person, and something to write with.
    • 
          
  • The live builder open on a screen if you have one.
    • 
        


        
A 45-minute plan

        
    
          
  • 0–5 minWelcome and one question: "What is a task you wish you could hand off this week?"
    • 
          
  • 5–10 minShow why thin prompts fail. Read the weak example aloud together.
    • 
          
  • 10–25 minTeach the three parts. Walk the strong example slowly, one part at a time.
    • 
          
  • 25–40 minEveryone writes their own, using the worksheet or the live builder.
    • 
          
  • 40–45 minA few read theirs aloud. Close with the four habits.
    • 
        


        
Teaching each part

        
Set the Stage

        
Help them see that who, where, and why come before what. Most weak prompts skip straight to the task.

        
Define the Task

        
Push for one specific ask and the material it needs. Vague verbs like "improve" or "help" are where results go sideways.

        
Specify the Rules

        
Remind them that one example saves a paragraph. If they can show the shape they want, they rarely have to explain it.


        
Questions people often ask

        
"Isn't this just for tech people?"

        
No. It is structured asking, the same skill behind a good brief or a clear request. Anyone who can give instructions can do this.

        
"Does the AI need all of this every time?"

        
No. Short tasks need less. The framework is what you reach for when the result matters or the first try missed.

        
"What about privacy?"

        
Treat it like any shared document. Do not paste private financial details, passwords, or anything you would not put in an email.


        
A closing thought to leave them with: the goal is not speed for its own sake. It is to free your attention for the work that truly needs a person. Clear instruction is good stewardship of both the tool and your time.
