# SCENARIO RESPONSES

*[Full-page divider. Nothing else on this page.]*

Work the scenario first. These responses are here to sharpen a decision you have already committed to, and they lose most of their value if you read them in advance.

They are also the only answer key in this book. Everything else asks you a question. This section tells you what Tom would do.

---

## SESSION 1

### Scenario One
*Forty minutes in, the husband has answered everything, his wife has said a dozen words, and you have twenty minutes and one agenda item left.*

You have been running a one-client meeting with two clients in the room, and you have been doing it for forty minutes with her permission. That permission is worth nothing. Her silence is not agreement, it is not deference, and it is certainly not an absence of opinion. What it is, most often, is a pattern established long before you arrived, in which he speaks for the household and she lets him because correcting it in public would cost more than staying quiet.

If you close the meeting on that silence you will leave with a plan she has never endorsed, and you will find out what she actually thought at the worst possible moment, which is usually after he is gone and she is the client.

Tom's practice here is specific and it is not a matter of general courtesy. Being attentive to both spouses is one of his three conditions for a successful goals conversation, and in the goals conversation itself he does not merely invite the spouse to add anything. He asks her the same question, separately and in full, and writes down what she says. The separation is the mechanism. A shared question in a room with an established speaker gets answered by the established speaker.

Drop the last agenda item. It will keep. Turn to her by name and ask the three-year question, then stop talking and write down whatever comes, without improving it. If she gives you a short answer, wait rather than moving on. The second sentence is usually the one you needed.

### Scenario Two
*A client says he is worried his son will not be ready to run the business, and you already have three structures in mind.*

He did not ask you for a structure. He told you he is worried, and worry is a statement about a relationship and about himself as much as about his son. Answering it with a trust design is a way of converting an emotional disclosure into a technical problem, which is more comfortable for both of you and which loses the only genuinely useful thing that has happened in the meeting.

There is also a real risk that you solve the wrong problem. "Not ready" can mean he lacks the skills, or he lacks the character, or he does not actually want it and has never said so, or the father cannot picture handing anything to anyone. Those four situations look identical from the outside and require four different responses. Tom is direct that preparation is the priority and that passing ownership before preparation is complete is a mistake, but you cannot prepare someone until you know what is missing.

This is the session's whole argument in miniature. Your value in this moment is what you ask, not what you know.

Hold the structures. Ask two questions and let them run. What would ready look like, and what have you seen that concerns you. Then listen to the answers rather than sorting them into the categories you already prepared. The structures will still be there in twenty minutes, and by then you will know which of the three is actually right, which is more than you know now.

### Scenario Three
*You ask an open question, the room goes quiet for eight seconds, and the client's adult daughter is looking at the table.*

This is the meeting working, and it will feel like the meeting failing.

Tom is explicit that most people are uncomfortable with silence and fill it quickly, and that the silence is precisely where people begin to think more deeply. Eight seconds is not a long time in a family that has never been asked anything. She is not disengaged. She is running the calculation every person in that position runs, which is whether it is safe to say the true thing in front of her parents, and whether you are the kind of person who will handle it well if she does.

Everything you might do to relieve the discomfort will answer that question badly. Rephrasing the question reads as a retreat and tells her you did not mean it. Filling the gap with an observation of your own tells her the invitation was decorative. Calling on her by name converts an invitation into a demand and puts her on the spot in front of the people she is deciding whether to trust.

Let it run. If it goes past twenty seconds and the discomfort is genuinely rising, address the room rather than a person, with something that gives permission rather than pressure. *Take your time.* Then wait again. The person who speaks first is very often not the person you expected, and what they say is usually worth the eight seconds.

## SESSION 2

### Scenario One
*Clients in their late seventies who agree it is a good idea and suggest revisiting after the holidays, three years running.*

This is timing, the fourth barrier, and Tom's warning about it is the sharpest thing he says on the subject. Later does not always come, and while everyone waits the stakes rise, so that a conversation which would have been straightforward at seventy-four becomes complicated at seventy-seven and impossible at eighty-one.

They are not resisting the meeting. They are deferring the discomfort, and deferral is cheap because it never requires anyone to say no. Notice your own part in it, because it is real. Three years of "good idea" without a date is a pattern you co-authored, and you have been accepting a pleasant answer in place of a commitment because the pleasant answer keeps the relationship comfortable.

The barrier does not break by being described. It breaks when the deferral becomes visible.

Stop asking whether and start proposing when. Say what you have observed, without accusation and without softening it, then put two specific dates on the table and let them choose one. *We have agreed this is a good idea three times now, and we have not put a date on it. I would like to do that today.* If they defer a fourth time, that is information rather than a scheduling problem, and the conversation you need to have is about what they are actually avoiding.

### Scenario Two
*A client tells you his children already know how he and his wife think about all this. You have met two of them and neither has mentioned it.*

Assumption, and he is not lying to you. He believes it completely, which is what makes this barrier so durable. Tom's line is that assumptions are rarely as accurate as people think, and the mechanism here is ordinary. The children grew up inside those values and watched them lived, so the parents have concluded that exposure and communication are the same thing. They are not. Watching someone be generous for thirty years tells you nothing about what they intend to do with the business.

Arguing the point will not work and will cost you standing. He has more evidence than you do, or believes he does, and you would be asking him to accept that he knows his own children less well than you do.

Verify it instead. Tom's own process for the next generation begins with permission from the first generation and a promise of confidentiality, and that sequence exists for exactly this situation.

Ask for permission to talk to the children. Frame it as work on the plan rather than as a challenge to him. *I would like to hear it back from them, so we know the plan lands the way you intend it to.* If he agrees, you will have your answer within two conversations and it will be his data rather than your opinion. If he refuses, you have learned something considerably more important than what the children know.

### Scenario Three
*You mention business succession in passing, the room goes flat, the subject changes, and nobody returns to it.*

Silence around an important topic is the first of the three signs Tom names, and this one is unmistakable because you felt the temperature change before anyone spoke.

What you have hit is the invisible elephant, which is Tom's own phrase for the thing that is simultaneously the most valuable item on the balance sheet and the least discussable item in the family. It stays invisible because at least one person in the room benefits from it staying that way, and everyone else has learned that raising it costs more than living with it. Somebody at that table knows exactly why the room went flat.

Do not chase it in the meeting. Pressing a topic that the room has just collectively agreed to drop will force it in front of whoever the silence was protecting, and you will get a managed answer and lose the chance at a real one.

Get it into a one-to-one within the week. Call the person most likely to speak plainly, and describe what you observed without interpreting it. *When succession came up, the room changed. I would like to understand that.* Then listen. One caution, and it matters: do not accept a private account and carry it into the next meeting as something you know and the others do not. That is the triangle Session 4 is about, and you will have joined it in the name of understanding.

## SESSION 3

### Scenario One
*Your client wants a full day with all four adult children and their spouses, covering the entire structure, next month.*

Starting too big, which is the first of the three mistakes Tom names for this stage. His enthusiasm is genuine and his instinct is generous, and it will produce a bad meeting.

A family that has never had this conversation cannot absorb a full disclosure in one sitting. What actually happens across a day like that is a long presentation with polite questions, followed by four separate car journeys home in which the real conversation finally takes place, in pairs, out of the room. Worse, a first meeting that goes flat makes the second one harder to convene, because everyone now has a reference point for what these meetings are like.

There is a second problem underneath the first. Tom's instruction is to clarify what type of meeting this is before planning anything, and this one is currently being planned as informational, decision-making, educational, and relational simultaneously. No single gathering does all four.

Keep the date and shrink the content. Propose that the first meeting be the first movement only, which is listening for the heart, and that the structure and the numbers come at a second meeting once everyone has been asked something. *Let us make the first one about hearing from them. The plan will land better once they have been asked.* Most clients accept this readily, because the version you are proposing is considerably less frightening than the one they were bracing for.

### Scenario Two
*The youngest son says he has always assumed his parents trust his brother more. His mother's expression changes. Nobody has spoken yet, including you.*

This is the meeting working, and for the next ninety seconds it will look exactly like the meeting failing.

He has said the true thing. Everything you did in Session 3 to build safety was done so that a sentence like that could be spoken out loud, and it has been. The entire outcome now rests on what happens in the next few seconds, because if the mother answers first the conversation closes and does not reopen. Her answer will be defensive, not because she is defensive as a person, but because she has just been told in front of her whole family that one of her children has spent years believing she loves him less.

Tom's discipline in this moment is to slow the conversation down and make sure people feel heard before anything moves forward. The sequencing is the whole intervention. Acknowledgment has to arrive before correction, or the correction will be heard as a dismissal.

Speak before she does. *Thank you for saying that. I want to make sure we have understood it before anyone responds.* Then turn back to the son and ask him to say more, and let him finish. When you eventually come to the mother, her answer will be a better one than the answer she was about to give, and it will be heard.

### Scenario Three
*Two children reply warmly to the invitation. The third does not reply at all, and the parents ask you what it means.*

It means nothing yet, and the parents are asking you to interpret because interpreting is less frightening than asking. A non-reply covers everything from a full calendar to a fifteen-year grievance, and the gap will fill with a story if you leave it empty. That story will be worse than the truth and it will shape how the parents behave toward that child at the meeting, which is how a non-reply becomes a real problem.

Hold the line on what the invitation was. Tom's rule for the invitation letters is that the goal is invitation rather than instruction, which necessarily includes the right not to respond. Chasing hard now converts the invitation into a summons and confirms whatever the child suspected.

Do not speculate with the parents. Say plainly that you do not know and that neither do they.

Then ask their permission to reach out once, directly, with no pressure and no second ask. Use the framing from the invitation to the next generation, which releases the person from having answers or certainty and asks instead what would be helpful. If there is still nothing, hold the meeting without them, and send the follow-up letter afterward to everyone, including the one who did not come. That letter is the door left open, and in a fair number of families it is the thing that eventually brings the person in.

## SESSION 4

### Scenario One
*A sibling calls privately two days before the meeting to tell you what her brother is really like, and asks you to keep it between the two of you.*

The triangle is forming and you are being offered the third side.

She is not malicious. She is uncomfortable with her brother, that discomfort has nowhere to go, and it is being routed to the most neutral and most trusted person available, which is you. Tom's rule is simple and he does not qualify it: when someone has a problem with another person, they go to that person, rather than collecting an ally first.

Understand what accepting the confidence would cost. From that moment you cannot facilitate the meeting, because you will be managing information that half the room does not know you have, and every intervention you make will be quietly shaped by it. The family will feel that even if they cannot name it. Tom's account of emotional triangles is that they preserve themselves and resist the people trying to dissolve them, which is why you cannot accept the information now and be neutral later.

Take the call and decline the condition, in that order, because she needs to be heard and the terms need to change. *I want to hear this, and I cannot hold it as something separate from the meeting. Tell me what you would be willing to say in the room, and let us work out how you say it.* If she agrees, you have converted a triangle into a conversation. If she will not, you have learned the true state of that relationship two days early, and you will facilitate very differently for knowing it.

### Scenario Two
*Three adult children in very different circumstances, and parents who want to be fair by giving each a third.*

They are using "fair" to mean "equal" because equal is the only definition of fairness that requires no conversation and no judgment. It is defensible to everyone, it cannot be argued with, and it lets them avoid saying out loud that their children are not in the same place. In a family with a child in recovery, an impulsive earner, and a careful giver, an equal split is not a neutral decision. It is a decision with three very different consequences.

Tom's way into this does not attack the split. It asks what the money will do. His question is what amount of inheritance would be a blessing to them and not a curse, followed by what they hope the inheritance accomplishes in their heirs' lives. He also quotes Ron Blue's formulation, which does the reframing in a single line: you love your children equally, therefore you treat them uniquely.

Notice that this is not an argument for unequal treatment. Tom's own experience includes a child who asked to be treated the same as her siblings for reasons that had nothing to do with money, and he changed his approach because of it. The point is the conversation, not the outcome.

Do not argue against thirds. Take them through it one child at a time and ask what a third would do in that specific life. Then ask what they hope it accomplishes. They may land exactly where they started, and that is a fine result, because they will have decided rather than defaulted and they will be able to explain it to their children.

### Scenario Three
*The son with eighteen years in the business says the ownership split is not fair, his sister answers immediately, and the father's jaw sets.*

Three people are about to speak in sequence and not one of them will have listened.

The father speaking now is the worst available outcome, because it converts a disagreement between siblings into a verdict from the person who controls the asset. Whatever he says will end the conversation, and it will be remembered for twenty years by whichever child it goes against. Tom's account of a nearly identical moment is that handled poorly it creates long-term division, and that what changed it was slowing everything down and giving each person the chance to explain how they were thinking and why.

There is something true underneath both positions and neither is being heard. The son is not really talking about percentages. He is talking about eighteen years and whether anyone noticed. The sister is not really defending her stake. She is responding to what sounded like a claim that she is worth less to this family.

Interrupt the sequence, and do it before the father speaks. Tom's own line is available and it works: *let us take a moment and make sure we understand what is being said.* Then give the son the floor to finish without interruption, and before the sister responds, ask her to say back what she heard him say. Agreement is not on the table today and you should not pretend otherwise. Understanding is, and it has to come first.

## SESSION 5

### Scenario One
*The eldest son has spoken for eighteen of the first twenty-two minutes. He is engaged and articulate. Two siblings have said nothing.*

Uneven participation, the first challenge Tom names in a group setting. He is not the problem to be solved. He is doing what he has always done in this family, probably in a role that was assigned to him decades ago, and he is trying to help.

The structure is the problem. Every minute this runs, the two silent siblings receive confirmation that this meeting is the same as every other family conversation, in which one person talks and the rest agree. By minute forty they will have settled into it, and you will not get them back inside this session.

Correcting him directly is the obvious move and the wrong one. It humiliates a person who was cooperating, in front of the family, and it costs you an ally you will need in the second half.

Use the structure rather than the person. Make the round explicit, so that the constraint comes from the process rather than from you. *I want to make sure we hear from everyone on this first question, so let us go around.* Then go to a specific sibling by name rather than opening it to the room, because an open invitation will be answered by the person who answers everything. If he re-enters out of turn, thank him and hand it on without comment. Tom's description of the facilitator's job is gentle guidance that makes sure everyone has a voice, and gentle is doing real work in that sentence.

### Scenario Two
*Ninety seconds after someone mentions the lake house, the room is relitigating a decision from 2011.*

Drifting into old issues is Tom's third named challenge, and the lake house is a proxy. No family spends ninety seconds on a fourteen-year-old decision unless something current is attached to it, and the current thing is almost never property.

You have two failure modes available. Letting it run consumes the afternoon and ends with everyone confirmed in their existing positions. Shutting it down flatly tells the room that some subjects are off limits here, which undoes the safety you spent two sessions building.

The move is to name the drift while honouring what sits underneath it. Tom's redirect is designed for this: *let us come back to what we are trying to understand here.* Attach an acknowledgment to the front of it so that the person who raised it is not the person who gets corrected.

Say something close to this. *That clearly still matters, and I do not want to lose it. Can we park it and come back to it properly?* Then write it somewhere visible, so parking looks like storage rather than burial. Return to the question. Then follow up on the parked item within the week, in a smaller setting, because if you never come back to it you will have taught that family that parking means burying, and the next drift will be harder to stop.

### Scenario Three
*Ten minutes left, the daughter-in-law has not spoken all afternoon, and you know she has the clearest view of the family in the room.*

She has spent three hours reading the room and has reached a conclusion about her standing in it, which is that in-laws speak last or not at all. That is not shyness. In most families it is an accurate assessment, learned over years, and she is behaving sensibly.

Tom watches for exactly this, tracking who is speaking and who is holding back, and the reason it matters here is that the people at the edge of a family system frequently see it most clearly. They are inside enough to observe and outside enough to be honest.

The risk is real, though. Calling on her cold at minute one hundred and eighty, in front of the family she married into, and asking for a position on a family matter, will expose her. She will give you something safe, and it will be less than she has.

Ask for observation rather than position, which is both safer and closer to what you actually want. *You have been listening to all of this from a slightly different seat. What have you noticed that the rest of us might have missed?* That framing is honest, it gives her a role she can legitimately occupy, and it does not require her to take a side in her husband's family. Thank her when she finishes and do not comment on the content, because commenting turns her observation into a proposal she then has to defend.

## SESSION 6

### Scenario One
*A good first meeting eight months ago, nothing since, and the parents seem content.*

This is the first of Tom's two families, drifting the same way and for the same reason. Nobody decided to stop. There was a move, a new grandchild, and a busy year, and busyness is the first eroder Tom names precisely because it never announces itself as a decision.

Contentment is not evidence of health here. It is evidence that nothing has forced the issue, which is the state that first family was in for the entire period during which everything quietly returned to how it had been. The eight-month gap is not really the problem. The absence of a next date is, and the absence of a next date is your responsibility more than theirs, because you were the one who knew this pattern existed.

Do not propose a new initiative. Anything that sounds like a fresh piece of work will be received as work and deferred accordingly.

Propose the next instance of something that already went well. Call and reference the last meeting specifically, because the memory of it is your strongest asset. *The last one was good. What is the right month for the next one?* Then, in that meeting, apply Tom's instruction without exception and schedule the following one before anyone leaves the room, even if it is a year out. A date in the calendar is the whole difference between his two families.

### Scenario Two
*Your client says everything is fine, the family gets on well, and he does not see the need.*

Tom's second eroder exactly, which is assuming things are fine because there is no visible conflict.

He may well be right, and that is the argument rather than the obstacle. A family that gets on well is the ideal condition in which to have this conversation, because nothing is at stake yet and nobody is defending a position. The families who fracture are almost never the ones who talked while things were calm. They are the ones who waited until there was a reason to talk, by which point there was also a reason not to.

Do not argue that something is wrong. You cannot prove it, he will resist it, and you will have converted a good relationship into an adversarial one over a meeting.

Reframe the meeting as protection rather than repair, and use Tom's own line, because nothing you compose will be stronger. There is going to be a family meeting, and the only question is whether he is in the room for it, because when he is gone his family will gather and talk about his affairs regardless. Then make it concrete. *I would rather they hear it from you than reconstruct it from documents.* Ask for one afternoon, not a process. Most clients can say yes to an afternoon.

### Scenario Three
*Six years of annual meetings that have become a business update, with no real conversation in two years.*

The rhythm survived and the content died. This is the third eroder, which is that people do not know how to continue the conversation, so it defaults to the safest available material. A business update is a legitimate agenda, which is exactly what makes it such an effective hiding place. Nobody has to admit that anything is being avoided.

Notice what has quietly happened to the meeting's purpose. Tom names five purposes for a family meeting, of which updates on family and business plans is only one, and communication between generations and building trust are the two that have been squeezed out. He also names a fourth type of meeting, the relational one, which exists only to connect and which almost no advisor ever schedules because it produces no deliverable.

Exhorting them to go deeper will not work. Change the structure and the content follows.

Put Movement One back at the top of the agenda and give it real time before a single number appears. If they do not have a binder, introduce one, so that each meeting starts from where the last finished rather than from the balance sheet. Then propose that next year's gathering be a relational meeting with no business content at all. That proposal will feel unserious to you and it is the most serious thing on the list.

# FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

**I am working through this alone. Does it still work?**
Yes, and it was built that way rather than adapted. Every shared prompt carries a solo fork, the exercises in each session are private by design, and nothing in the six sessions requires another person in the room. The only group-specific material is in the section at the back, and it is optional.

**How do I introduce this to a client without it sounding like a new product I am selling?**
Do not introduce the series. Introduce a question. In practice this begins when you ask something in an ordinary review meeting that you have not asked before, most often the three-year question or the two concern questions from Session 1. The conversation either opens or it does not, and if it opens you have a reason to propose more. Advisors who lead with a process get a polite no. Advisors who lead with a question that lands get invited back.

**What if the first generation will not let me speak to the children?**
Then you do not speak to them. Tom's own sequence begins with permission from Generation 1 and a promise of confidentiality, and going around that would cost you the relationship and deserve to. A refusal is also information. It usually means there is something the parents are not ready for the children to know, and the useful work is finding out what it is.

**How long does an engagement like this take?**
Longer than you expect, and Tom is candid about it. His own experience includes clients who took years to agree to something he recommended at the first meeting. His counsel is to be persistent without being pushy, and to accept that a client frequently will not do what you want the first time you ask.

**I am not the client's primary trusted advisor. Can I still do this work?**
Yes, and in some cases it is easier, because the relational conversation is not the one anyone else is competing for. Tom's collaboration principle applies here, which is knowing when and where to bring in other professionals rather than trying to hold every role yourself.

**Do I have to facilitate the family meeting myself?**
No. Some advisors facilitate, some co-facilitate, and some bring in someone else and stay in the room. What matters is that a neutral third party is present, which is the mechanism that makes the difficult subject discussable in the first place.

**How should I charge for this?**
*[Client to supply. The workshop material addresses fee approach at a level of generality that will not survive being written down as guidance, and this book should not invent a number or a model. Recommend a short, honest section here on how Tom actually thinks about scoping and value, drafted with him directly.]*

**What if a family I serve is not Christian?**
The frameworks in this guide are structural rather than devotional, and the questions work regardless. Tom's scriptural grounding is where the material comes from, and how much of it you carry into a given room is a judgment you are qualified to make.

**What if the meeting goes badly?**
Some of the best ones do, for a while. Tom rates one family meeting as beginning at a five, dropping to a three as frustrations came out, and finishing at an eight. A meeting getting worse before it gets better is frequently the meeting working. What you are watching for is whether people are still engaged and whether the tone stays respectful, not whether anyone is comfortable.

**I am a technician by temperament. Am I actually suited to this?**
Tom was told he was an accountant with a personality, and he treats the relational side as a set of learnable skills rather than as a disposition you either have or do not. Listening improves with practice. Question-asking improves with practice. The evidence that you are suited to it is that you are on this page.

*Built from Tom's questions in this series.*

This is not another self-rating. The self-checks in each session measure your skills. This one measures your book.

**Total Y responses:** *[line]* out of 80.

**Reading it.** Ignore the total for a moment and read down the columns. A household with two or three yes responses is a relationship you have been servicing technically and not much else, and it is the one most exposed when the transfer happens. # TOP 10 COACHING EXERCISES

Every one of these is Tom's, and every one has been used with real families. The session reference tells you where it is taught in this guide. The source tells you where to read more.

**1. The Three-Year Question.** If we met three years from today, what must have happened for you to feel good about your progress? Asked of each spouse separately, with everything written down.
*Session 1 · Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Ch. 7*

# ABOUT FAMILY LEGACY BY DESIGN

Family Legacy by Design exists because the largest transfer of wealth in history is under way, and most of the professional attention pointed at it is aimed at the smallest part of the problem.

The approach rests on three words. **Clarity** about a family's values and vision. **Alignment** between their plans and that vision. **Communication** that builds and holds trust across generations. It expands the definition of legacy past money into five areas: personal, family, financial, business, and charitable. When all five are working together, families do not merely preserve what they have. They multiply what it does.

The work is built for advisors who have come to suspect there is more to this than numbers on a page. It asks for a shift from managing money to shaping legacies, from transactions to transformation, and from guarding portfolios to shepherding the people who own them.

The same relational dynamics that decide whether a family flourishes or fractures during a transfer show up in marriages, churches, ministries, and organizations. Christian counselors, pastors, nonprofit directors, and executive coaches will find the frameworks here work outside the advisory context as readily as inside it.
