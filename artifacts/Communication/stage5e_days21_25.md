# Day 21

## You're Helping the Family Think Together
### Most families don't naturally do that

**"Everything should be done in a fitting and orderly way."**
1 Corinthians 14:40

You are helping the family think together. That is really the goal.

Not just sharing information, but creating a setting where people can listen, understand each other, and begin to move toward alignment.

Most families do not naturally do that. Conversations tend to happen in smaller groups or around specific issues. Bringing everyone together in a thoughtful way creates a different kind of dynamic.

I want you to take the phrase think together literally, because it names something most families have genuinely never done, and once you see it you will see it everywhere in your book.

Consider where a family's real conversations actually happen. Two people in a car. A phone call between two siblings after a holiday. A husband and wife at the end of an evening. A mother and one adult child who has always been the one she talks to. Every one of those is a real conversation and every one of them is partial, and the family's understanding of itself is assembled out of a dozen partial conversations that have never been in the same room.

That is not dysfunction. That is how families work, and it works fine for forty years. It stops working when something has to be decided that affects everybody, because at that point the family discovers it has no shared account of anything.

So when you bring them together, you are not convening a bigger version of a conversation they already have. You are doing something structurally new, and you should expect it to feel new to them, which is why the first twenty minutes are usually stiff.

There is a second thing worth naming. In the smaller groupings, certain people never appear. There is almost always a family member who is present at every gathering and party to none of the conversations. Frequently it is an in-law. Frequently it is the child who moved away. Sometimes it is the second spouse. That person's understanding of the family has been assembled entirely from what other people have told them, and nobody has ever asked them anything.

Bringing everyone together changes what is available to that person, which is why some of the most useful things said in a first family meeting come from the edge of the room rather than the middle.

Paul's instruction in 1 Corinthians 14:40 arrives at the end of a long passage about a gathering that had become chaotic, with everyone contributing at once and nobody able to follow. His answer is not fewer voices. It is order, so that the voices can actually be received.

That is the correct diagnosis for most family meetings I have walked into. The problem was never that too many people wanted to speak. It was that there was no way for what anybody said to land.

# Day 22

## Without Structure, Conversations Drift
### When a conversation has structure, it allows people to engage more fully

**"Each of you should use whatever gift you have received to serve others, as faithful stewards of God's grace in its various forms."**
1 Peter 4:10

Without structure, conversations can drift or become dominated by one or two voices. When a conversation has structure, it allows people to engage more fully.

The structure, the tone, the way the conversation is guided, all of that shapes what happens.

Advisors tend to hear structure as constraint, and families tend to hear it as formality, and both readings are wrong in the same way. Structure in a family conversation is not a limit on what can be said. It is the thing that makes it possible for the quieter half of the family to say anything at all.

Watch an unstructured family conversation and you will see it go one of two ways.

It drifts. Somebody mentions something adjacent, someone else follows it, and forty minutes later the family is deep in a subject nobody came to discuss. Drift feels productive while it is happening because people are talking. Nothing has been understood.

Or it consolidates. Two people who are comfortable talking establish a rhythm within the first ten minutes, and the rhythm becomes the meeting. Everyone else settles into the audience, and here is the part that matters: they do not experience this as being excluded. They experience it as normal, because it is what always happens, and they will not tell you afterward that they had something to say.

Both failures come from the same absence. Nobody said how this conversation is going to work.

The structure that prevents both is not elaborate. A stated purpose. An opening question every person answers, in turn, before open discussion begins. A rule about one voice at a time. A stated point where the conversation moves from listening to naming themes. That is close to all of it, and it takes two minutes to say at the start.

The reason the round matters more than anything else on that list is worth understanding. Once a person has spoken, even briefly, they are a participant. Once ten minutes have passed and they have not spoken, they are an observer, and moving from observer back to participant is a much larger step than speaking early was.

So the round is not a courtesy. It is load bearing.

Peter tells each person to use what they have received to serve, as faithful stewards of grace in its various forms. Various forms is the phrase. He is describing a body where different people carry different things, and none of it is any use to anyone if the person carrying it never gets to bring it out.

A family is exactly that. The person with the clearest view of what is happening is very often not the person who talks most.

Structure is how you get to hear from them.

# Day 23

## It Starts With a Clear Purpose
### Why are we meeting? What are we trying to accomplish?

**"Be devoted to one another in love. Honor one another above yourselves."**
Romans 12:10

It starts with a clear purpose. Why are we meeting? What are we trying to accomplish?

Then you create a flow. You might begin with something simple, having each person share what matters most to them or how they are thinking about a particular area. That sets the tone. It invites participation and helps everyone get engaged.

I want to press on purpose, because it is the thing advisors think they have and usually do not.

Here is the test. Write down, in one sentence, what your next family meeting is for. If the sentence contains the word discuss, or the word review, or a list joined by and, you do not have a purpose. You have an agenda, and an agenda is a list of subjects rather than a statement of what this gathering is trying to accomplish.

The difference is not academic. A family walking into a meeting whose purpose is unstated will supply one, and what they supply is almost always worse than the truth. Most commonly they assume a decision is going to be extracted from them. Occasionally they assume somebody is in trouble. I have had adult children arrive at first meetings genuinely braced for bad news about a parent's health, because a family gathering with a professional present had no other available meaning.

That is what an unstated purpose costs you. Forty minutes of a defended room.

A real purpose has three properties. It is one thing. It is achievable in the time available. And it is honest about what will not happen, which is usually the most reassuring part. Telling a family that no decisions will be made today is not a limitation on the meeting. It is what allows people to speak, because it removes the possibility that something they say will be treated as a commitment.

Then the flow, and the first move in it. Something simple that everyone can answer, which does two jobs at once. It gets each person's voice into the room early, and it tells everyone what register this conversation is in. If the first question is about hopes, the meeting is about hopes. If the first question is about the trust, the meeting is about the trust, and it will stay there.

Romans 12:10 asks us to honor one another above ourselves. In a family meeting, that instruction has an unglamorous practical form.

It means the person who has the most to say goes second.

# Day 24

## They Began to Hear Common Themes
### They weren't starting from disagreement, they were starting from shared values

**"Gracious words are a honeycomb, sweet to the soul and healing to the bones."**
Proverbs 16:24

I worked with a family where this was very clear. They had never had a full family conversation about their future. There were assumptions, some tension, and a lot of uncertainty.

When we brought them together, we did not start with decisions. We started with a simple question. What do you hope for this family moving forward?

Each person had the opportunity to respond. At first it was a little cautious. But as people shared, something shifted. They began to hear common themes, things they all cared about, even though they had not expressed it before.

Once that happened, the rest of the conversation became much more productive. They were not starting from disagreement. They were starting from shared values.

I have watched that pattern hold in family after family, and it still surprises the families every time, so let me say plainly what is going on.

The alignment was already there. Nobody built it in that room. What had been missing was not agreement, it was evidence of agreement, and those are different things. Every person in that family had been holding a hope for the family and had assumed they were more or less alone in it, because nobody had ever said it in a setting where the others could hear.

That assumption of isolation is what tension is made of in a lot of families. Not conflict. Isolation. Each person quietly believing they are the one who cares about the thing.

And notice how ordinary the question was. Not a values instrument, not a facilitated exercise. What do you hope for this family moving forward. It works because it is wide enough that everyone has an answer, forward-looking so it does not summon old material, and about the family rather than about the money.

Notice also what it does not ask. It does not ask what they want. Want produces a list of positions. Hope produces something closer to the ground level of what a person actually cares about, and at ground level families overlap far more than they do at the level of positions.

The other thing I would have you take from this is that the caution at the beginning was not resistance. First answers in a round are always short, because nobody knows yet what register is permitted. The third answer is longer than the first. The fifth is longer still. If you cut the round short because the early answers were thin, you will never reach the part where it starts working.

Proverbs 16:24 calls gracious words a honeycomb, sweet to the soul and healing to the bones. Healing is the word I want. The proverb is not describing pleasantness. It is describing repair, and it locates the repair in the body, which is to say it is real rather than atmospheric.

Something does get repaired when a family hears itself agree about something it has never discussed.

Most of them had no idea it was broken.

# Day 25

## Who Is Speaking? Who Is Holding Back?
### You're not trying to force outcomes

**"So in everything, do to others what you would have them do to you, for this sums up the Law and the Prophets."**
Matthew 7:12

You are watching engagement. Who is speaking? Who is holding back? Is anyone dominating the conversation?

You are also listening for themes. What is being repeated? Where is there alignment? Where are there differences that need to be explored?

And you are managing the pace. Keeping it moving, but not rushing it.

You are not trying to force outcomes. You are helping the conversation stay healthy and productive.

Three tracks at once, and I will not pretend that is easy. The first time you facilitate a family conversation you will manage one of the three and lose the other two, and the one you manage will be content, because content is what you are used to.

So here is how I would sequence the learning.

Start with engagement, because it is the one nobody else in the room is watching and the one with the highest cost when it is missed. Concretely: keep a mental list of who has spoken and how much. Notice the person who started engaged and went quiet, which is a different signal from the person who was quiet throughout. The first one usually means something specific happened, and it is worth knowing what.

Themes come second, and the discipline here is to listen for repetition across people rather than for the strongest statement from one person. The strongest statement is memorable and is usually a position. A theme is what shows up in three answers in slightly different words, and it is less quotable and more useful.

Pace is third and it is largely handled by what you did before the meeting, which is why yesterday's work on purpose and structure matters more than anything you do in the room.

Now the sentence I want to underline. You are not trying to force outcomes.

I want to be honest that this is hard for us specifically. We are outcome people. We are paid for outcomes, we measure ourselves by them, and sitting in a room for two hours where the objective is understanding rather than resolution will feel to you like a meeting that has not happened yet.

But a forced outcome in a family conversation is worse than no outcome, because it is a real decision made by people who had not finished understanding each other, and it will be relitigated at exactly the worst moment.

Matthew 7:12 asks us to do to others as we would have them do to us, and Jesus says this sums up the Law and the Prophets, which is an extraordinary claim to attach to a rule that simple.

Applied to a room, it is not abstract at all. If you were the quiet one, you would want somebody to notice. If you were the one who had gone silent halfway through, you would want somebody to ask. If you were the person whose view was inconvenient, you would want it explored rather than managed.

That is the whole job, and it is available to you in every meeting.
