Course Description

A first course in how doctrine works

Most Christians have absorbed a great deal of doctrine without ever being taught how doctrine actually works. They know what their church believes. They are far less sure where those beliefs came from, how they were tested, which of them are worth dividing over, and what any of it has to do with a Tuesday afternoon in a hospital corridor.

This course does not attempt to teach the doctrines. That is what the eleven courses after it are for. This one teaches a person how to hold one. It traces the road from a biblical text to a settled teaching, walks through the creeds and confessions that the church has used to guard that road, and gives the student a repeatable method for testing a doctrinal claim. It ends where every course in this program ends, with the student teaching what they have learned to somebody else.

Learning Outcomes

  1. Explain in plain language what doctrine is, where it comes from, and what it is for.
  2. Trace a single teaching from biblical text through theological synthesis to confessional statement.
  3. Distinguish first, second, and third order convictions, and explain why that distinction keeps congregations together.
  4. Apply a repeatable four-step method for testing whether a doctrinal claim is sound.
  5. Describe how doctrine functions pastorally in suffering, in decision-making, in worship, and at a deathbed.
  6. Teach one doctrine clearly to another person and receive feedback on it.

Teaching Script

Lesson One, written out

Ninety minutes, timed. A volunteer facilitator reads this and can teach. A master teacher reads it and makes it their own.

The Question Under the Question

Big idea: everyone already has a theology. The only question is whether it was chosen or absorbed.

Open  ·  10 minutes

Ask the question before any teaching. Let the silence sit. Do not rescue it.

Start with a question, and resist the urge to answer it yourself. Ask the room: when was the last time something you believed about God actually changed how you acted?

Some people will have an answer immediately. Most will not, and their hesitation is the doorway into this entire course. It is not that they have no beliefs. It is that their beliefs have never been examined closely enough to be traced back to anything. They inherited them from a parent, a worship song, a bad season, a good pastor, a book they half-finished. And inherited beliefs work fine right up until the day they do not.

Movement One  ·  20 minutes

Everybody in this room is already a theologian. That word sounds like it belongs to somebody with a book contract and a study full of Greek lexicons, but it does not. A theologian is simply a person who has thoughts about God, which makes the category roughly universal. The pastor is a theologian. So is the woman who quietly believes God is disappointed in her. So is the man who has decided God mostly does not get involved. Those are all doctrines. They are just doctrines nobody wrote down and nobody tested.

So the question this course asks is not whether you have a theology. You do. The question is where it came from and whether it will hold. Because it will get tested. Not in a classroom, but in an oncologist's office, at a graveside, in a marriage that has gone quiet, in the moment a child asks whether their friend who died is somewhere. What you actually believe surfaces then, and whatever you absorbed without examining is what will surface.

Movement Two  ·  20 minutes

Read 1 Timothy 4:16 aloud. Have someone else read Titus 2:1. NIV.

Paul writes to a young pastor and tells him to keep a close watch on two things at once, his life and his teaching, and he links the two so tightly that you cannot separate them. Notice what he does not say. He does not tell Timothy to watch his doctrine so that Timothy will win arguments. He tells him to watch it because people are depending on it. Doctrine, in Paul's hands, is a pastoral instrument. It is what you hand somebody when their life is coming apart.

That is worth sitting with, because most of us have met doctrine in its worst form first. We met it as a weapon in an online argument, or as a fence somebody used to keep us out, or as a dry list we were made to memorize and never told the purpose of. If that is your experience, this course is partly an attempt to give the word back to you. Sound doctrine, in the New Testament, is consistently described the way you would describe medicine. It is health-giving. It is what a person needs.

Movement Three  ·  15 minutes

Here is the road we are going to walk over the next eleven weeks, and it helps to see the whole map before we take the first step. It runs in four stages. It starts with revelation, because Christian doctrine is a response to something, not an invention from nothing. It moves to Scripture, which is where that revelation is fixed in a text the church has agreed to stand under. It moves to the rule of faith and then the creeds, which is the church saying out loud, together, what the text had been teaching all along. And it ends in confessions and catechisms, where particular churches in particular centuries wrote it down again in their own words for their own people.

Every doctrine you hold sits somewhere on that road. Part of the work of this course is learning to find the spot.

Discuss  ·  20 minutes

Two questions. Do not add a third. Let the second one run long.

One. Name one thing you believe about God that you are fairly sure you absorbed rather than chose. Where do you think it came from?

Two. Think of a moment when what you believed about God actually carried weight, or failed to. What was the belief, and what did it do?

Close  ·  5 minutes

Give the assignment before you pray, not after, because people stop listening after amen. This week they read the Apostles' Creed once a day, out loud, and write down every line they do not fully understand. That list becomes their personal syllabus for the rest of the course, and in week twelve they will look at it again.

Then close by praying the creed rather than talking about it. It was written to be said by a congregation, and saying it together on the first night does more to frame the course than any explanation would.

Format note: every one of the twelve scripts follows this same six-stage shape. Open, three movements, discuss, close. Once a writer has the shape, a script takes hours rather than days, and a master teacher can film against it without rehearsal.

Production

What it takes to do this four hundred times

A syllabus page like the one above runs about six hundred words and is almost entirely derivable from the title, the subtitle, and the discipline. That is the layer to ship first, because it is what makes the site look like a school on the day it launches, and it is what a pastor clicks before deciding anything.

The twelve build sheets are the second layer, and they are where the real intellectual work sits. The teaching scripts are the third, and they are the slowest. Sequence accordingly: publish all four hundred syllabi, build twelve-lesson maps for the courses churches actually request, and write full scripts only where a course is being taught this season or filmed.

Honest constraint: a syllabus that promises twelve lessons the course does not yet have is a credibility problem, not a marketing shortcut. Label the catalog plainly. Available now, in production, planned. Churches will forgive a roadmap. They will not forgive a course that turns out to be an outline.
