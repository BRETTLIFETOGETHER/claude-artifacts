01
The Observation

The boxes in the garage

Ken Sparks handwrote every message he ever preached. Decades of Sundays, box after box of paper, stacked in the order he delivered them. He is not unusual. Walk into the study of almost any pastor who has served twenty-five years or more and you will find some version of those boxes — legal pads, three-ring binders, cassette tapes, aging hard drives, a church website that keeps the last two years online and quietly drops the rest.

Here is the observation this project rests on: the most under-stewarded resource in any congregation is the senior pastor’s sermon library. Not the building, and not the endowment — the preaching. A congregation will insure its roof and audit its books, and let fifty years of its pastor’s best work sit where humidity and a housecleaning can take it.

There is a reason, and it is structural rather than negligent. Every other asset a lifetime produces has an institution built to receive it. Banks receive money. Museums receive art. Universities receive the papers of statesmen and scientists. The Church has never built the institution that receives the preaching. So when a pastor retires, the boxes go to the garage. When he dies, they usually go to the curb within the year — and everything God said through those words goes with them, except what people can still remember.

This is a proposal to build the institution that receives it, and to begin with one hundred pastors whose libraries deserve a generation’s care.

The Asset

Fifty years, counted

The arithmetic is worth doing slowly. A pastor who preaches forty-five weekends a year for thirty years delivers roughly thirteen hundred and fifty messages. Many of the pastors this project will serve have gone longer. Across one hundred such ministries, the body of work runs on the order of one hundred thirty thousand messages — each one prepared under a real deadline, tested in front of a living congregation, and revised by the only editor that finally matters: years of shepherding the very people who heard it.

And the sermons are only the visible layer. Inside a mature library sit the wedding homilies and the funeral messages, the illustrations gathered across a lifetime, the series that carried a church through a building program or a tragedy, and — almost always unrecognized by the pastor himself — a life message: the one thing God has been saying through this one man for fifty years, scattered across a thousand Sundays and never yet gathered into a single place.

Scripture gives the project its instruction. Paul told Timothy, “And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others” (2 Timothy 2:2, NIV). Entrusting assumes the things still exist. What was heard in the presence of many witnesses has to be kept in a form the next teacher can receive — and for most of the pastors in this first hundred, the clock on that is running.

The Work

From boxes to a living library

Every library moves through the same six steps, whatever condition it arrives in.

1UploadEverything, in whatever form it exists — audio, video, documents, photographed handwritten pages. Nothing has to be organized first, and no format is refused.
2TranscribeEvery message becomes clean, accurate text, with Scripture references identified and linked.
3IndexThe whole body of work is organized by topic, passage, series, and occasion, with illustrations identified and set apart from exposition.
4The Coverage ReportWhat he has preached, what he returns to, and what he has never addressed — a portrait of a life’s work most pastors have never seen.
5ConvertThe strongest material takes new forms: a series becomes a six-session study, a message becomes a week of daily readings, a life message becomes a book.
6ApproveNothing is published in the pastor’s name that he has not read and approved. Nothing.

Two commitments govern every engagement. The rights stay with the pastor — his words remain his own, and his family’s after him. And technology serves the work without ever replacing the author: artificial intelligence does the assembly, the pastor’s own preaching supplies every idea, and his approval is the final gate before anything ships.

The Deliverable

What one library becomes

A flagship engagement takes one pastor’s complete archive through the full work — typically nine to twelve months from the first box to the finished volume. It produces seven things.

The complete archive — digitized, transcribed, indexed, and searchable.
The Coverage Report — the portrait of the life’s work, delivered in person.
The life message — identified, gathered, and written into a single body of teaching.
A published legacy volume — hardcover, in a congregation edition and a family edition.
A churchwide campaign and small-group studies — drawn from his strongest series, ready for any congregation to run.
Succession access — the library formally entrusted to the church and to the family.
The circulation covenant — every funded library carries free access for small congregations, forever.

Selection

Why these hundred

The first hundred are chosen by stewardship urgency and demonstrated fruit — never by celebrity, and never by self-nomination.

TenureTwenty-five years or more of faithful preaching, most of it in one place — the libraries deep enough to justify the full work.
FruitChurches strengthened, leaders raised, a congregation that would testify to what the preaching produced.
SeasonMost will be past fifty, many past seventy. This is the only asset in the proposal that decays — every year of delay, libraries are lost.
BreadthAcross traditions, regions, and church sizes, so the first hundred looks like the Church rather than one corner of it.
NominationNamed by denominations, networks, peers, and funding families — with the funder’s own nominations welcomed first.

Some of the hundred will be names the whole country knows. Most will be known only in their own city, and that is by design. The known names open the door; the faithful unknown are the reason the door exists.

After Preservation

One life, many churches

Preservation alone would justify the gift. It is not where the work stops. Every library, once transcribed and indexed, becomes source material — and the conversions are built for the churches that can least afford to build anything themselves.

Thousands of American congregations are led by pastors serving alone, many while holding a second job. They have no research assistant, no teaching team, and no budget for curriculum. The circulation covenant exists for them: every funded library endows free access for small congregations, so that a bivocational pastor preparing on a Thursday night can draw on the indexed wisdom of men who preached for fifty years. The funding family’s gift is made once and keeps giving for as long as the library exists.

Something larger happens at one hundred. A single preserved library serves one legacy. A hundred libraries, cross-indexed, become what no ministry has ever held — the recorded wisdom of a hundred lifetimes of faithful preaching, searchable by the next generation of teachers, with every conversion carrying the original author’s name and his approval.

One pastor’s fifty years becomes five hundred churches’ next five years. That is the multiplication the first hundred sets in motion.

For the Funder

One family, one library

This is not a request to fund software, and it is not a grant that disappears into a budget. The project is structured so that one gift preserves one pastor’s complete life work — a whole library, carried from the boxes to the finished volume, with the funding family attached to it by name if they wish, or in complete anonymity if they prefer.

The design is deliberately relational. The funding family is introduced to the pastor whose library they preserve. They receive the first copy of the legacy volume. They are invited to the presentation when the finished library is formally entrusted to the church and the family — a room, in most cases, of three generations discovering together what fifty years of preaching amounts to.

For a family accustomed to giving, the leverage is unusual. The engine this work requires already exists and already runs; the gift buys the work itself, not the building of a capacity to do it. The outcome is concrete enough to hold in your hands, personal enough to know the man it honors, and permanent enough to outlast every party to the gift.

A lead gift can carry the entire pilot cohort and, if desired, carry its name. The structure serves either instinct — one family behind one pastor, or one family behind the first ten.

Accountability

What will be counted

Every funder receives a written report each quarter and an invitation to an annual gathering of funders and pastors. The report counts seven things, the same seven every quarter, so progress can be judged rather than narrated.

Libraries completed — against the cohort schedule.
Messages recovered and indexed — the running total across all libraries.
Coverage Reports delivered — each one presented to its pastor in person.
Volumes published — congregation and family editions in print.
Studies and campaigns produced — conversions approved and released.
Congregations served — churches drawing on the libraries through free access.
The stories — testimony from pastors, families, and churches, gathered with permission.

One thing will not be claimed. Formation is testified to, not measured, and no report from this project will dress testimony up as data. We will count what can be counted and tell the truth about the rest.

Why LifeTogether

Built by the people who build campaigns

LifeTogether has spent twenty-five years turning preaching into formation — churchwide campaigns, small-group curriculum, devotionals, and publishing — across more than five hundred church relationships, with more than fifty million campaign resources placed in circulation.

The organization was founded by Brett Eastman, who built the small-group ministry at Saddleback Church and served at the center of the Purpose Driven campaign era, when tens of thousands of congregations ran a single formation journey together. The conviction behind this project comes directly from that era: a proven message, put into a proven structure, changes churches at scale — and there is a Purpose Driven Life waiting inside the archive of nearly every faithful pastor. It simply has never been gathered.

The engine this project requires — transcription, indexing, editorial curation, curriculum conversion, design, publishing, and print — is not a plan awaiting funding. It is the system LifeTogether operates today. A funder’s gift purchases the work itself, at the pace the gift sets, rather than the construction of a capacity to attempt it.

The pilot exists to prove that claim on real libraries, on schedule, with every number reported.

The Decision

Begin with ten

The decision in front of a funder is one: commission the Pilot Ten — one million dollars, the first ten libraries delivered within twelve months, and every number in this prospectus tested against the real work before anything larger is asked.

Three requests come with it. First, help name the first ten — the pastors a funder already knows and honors belong at the front of this line. Second, introduce ten families, each to fund one library and meet the pastor whose life work they preserved. Third, a half-day working session to select the cohort and set the reporting rhythm.

Everything else — the schedule, the production, the publishing, the reporting — is ours to carry.

Every generation of the Church has buried more preaching than it has kept. This can be the generation that stops. “One generation commends your works to another; they tell of your mighty acts” (Psalm 145:4, NIV). The works are already preached. The commending is what this project builds.
