The Observation

The song that never leaves the room

In almost every church in America, somebody on the worship team is writing. A voice memo captured in the car. A chorus scratched into a notes app during a lunch break. A bridge that arrived during someone else’s sermon. Once in a while one of those songs makes it to a Sunday morning, and the congregation leans into it in that unmistakable way a room leans into something true. It gets sung once, maybe twice more. And then it goes where church songs go — nowhere. It lives and dies in the room it was written for.

Here is the observation this project rests on: the most under-released resource in the Church is the original worship already being written inside local churches. The industry signs a handful of writers a year and builds the world’s worship around a few well-known rooms. Meanwhile the Church — the whole Church — writes thousands of songs a year that never travel past their own parking lot. Not because they are unworthy, but because no institution exists to receive them, produce them, and release them without taking them.

The last generation proved how far a song can travel: for a season, much of what the global Church sang came from a handful of congregations. That season taught two lessons at once — what a local church’s songwriting can do for the whole body, and what it costs when every stream sings from the same two or three rooms. And it left a gifted young writer with exactly two paths: leave the local church for the industry, or stay and be unheard. Both paths subtract from the local church.

This is a proposal to build the third path, beginning with one hundred churches: their original worship captured, produced, and released — writers keeping ownership, congregations keeping their voice, and free access endowed for the churches that could never afford any of it.

From the Founder

Why I’m building this

I did not begin in the church. I began in Silicon Valley, doing marketing in the years the Macintosh launched — young, in a hurry, and convinced that the world moves when the right idea meets the right moment. I still believe that. Seminary just changed which idea I was willing to give my life to.

At Saddleback I built the small-group ministry, and then spent the Purpose Driven campaign era at the center of something I have never gotten over: tens of thousands of churches taking one journey together in one season. I have been building churchwide campaigns and small-group resources ever since — twenty-five years, more than five hundred church relationships, more than fifty million campaign resources in circulation.

Here is what I noticed in every one of those seasons, though it took me years to say it plainly. The preaching taught the journey, but the singing carried it. A congregation holds its songs longer than it holds its sentences; melody is how the heart memorizes. Whatever we asked churches to study for forty days, the season was won or lost by what those churches sang together on Sunday.

And in twenty-five years of walking hallways between the auditorium and the classroom, I have met the writers everywhere. The nurse who leads on the second Sunday and writes at her kitchen table. The student who put the youth camp’s theme verse to a melody the kids still sing. Almost every voice this generation knows started exactly there — on a volunteer team, in a local church. The ones who make it out get signed. The rest stay faithful and unheard, and their songs are buried with the setlists.

I have spent my life building the systems that multiply the preached word. This project is my attempt to do the same for the sung one — before another generation of songs dies in the rooms they were written for.

— Brett Eastman
Founder · LifeTogether

The Asset

What the Church is already writing

Nobody has ever counted this asset, so count it gently. Suppose only one American congregation in ten has somebody writing, and suppose that writer brings just three songs a year as far as a Sunday morning. Across roughly three hundred thousand congregations, that is on the order of ninety thousand new worship songs a year — written, sung, and lost. The real number is unknowable, which is itself the point: the Church’s largest songwriting community keeps no catalog of its own work.

The asset runs backward as well as forward. Alongside the songs being written this year sit the catalogs already written — the worship pastor retiring with a binder of originals nobody ever charted properly, a church’s twenty years of songs living on one aging hard drive, recordings of a congregation singing something it will never sing again. Like the sermon libraries this project’s sister document describes, these catalogs decay; unlike almost anything else a church produces, nobody is responsible for them.

Scripture’s picture of a singing church is participatory, not performed: “Let the message of Christ dwell among you richly as you teach and admonish one another with all wisdom through psalms, hymns, and songs from the Spirit, singing to God with gratitude in your hearts” (Colossians 3:16, NIV). Songs from the Spirit, arising among the people. That is not a description of a catalog delivered to congregations; it is a description of congregations producing one.

And the rails already exist. The licensing infrastructure the Church uses every week already registers, tracks, and reports songs across hundreds of thousands of congregations worldwide. What is missing is not distribution — it is the institution that carries a song from the voice memo to those rails without costing the writer her ownership or the church its voice. In the language a funding family would use for any other holding: an unbanked asset class — real value, no custodian, no registry, and no path to market that does not cost the Church its writers.

The Work

From voice memo to Sunday

Every church’s catalog moves through the same six steps, whatever condition it arrives in.

1GatherEverything — voice memos, live recordings, chord charts, lyric sheets, half-finished bridges. Nothing has to be polished first, and no format is refused.
2AuditThe full catalog reviewed: what exists, what travels beyond this room, and what the rights situation is — most church originals were never registered at all.
3ProduceThe strongest songs professionally produced — recorded with the church’s own voices wherever possible, so the song keeps its accent.
4PublishCharts in every key, clicks and multitracks a volunteer team can run, registration and rights administration — with ownership staying with the writers.
5ReleaseInto the shared library and onto the licensing rails the Church already uses — with free access endowed for small congregations.
6DevelopThe writers themselves: coaching, co-writing rooms, and a national cohort that connects them without extracting them from their churches.

Two commitments govern every engagement. The writers keep ownership — their songs remain theirs, and the church’s blessing governs everything released in its name. And production serves the song without sanding it down: the goal is not to make a hundred churches sound like one famous room, but to make each room’s true voice strong enough to travel.

The tools moment matters here as it does for the preaching: production, charting, and translation that were recently studio-budget work per song now run at catalog scale, which puts skilled hands where they matter — on the songs worth carrying — and makes one hundred catalogs in three years a schedule instead of a wish.

The Deliverable

What one church’s catalog becomes

A flagship engagement takes one church’s complete catalog through the full work — typically nine to twelve months from the first voice memo to the finished record. Catalogs vary, but every flagship produces the same seven things.

The catalog — gathered, audited, and rights-cleared, most of it registered for the first time.
A produced record — the church’s strongest originals, released under the church’s own name.
Sunday-ready kits — charts in every key, clicks and multitracks a volunteer team of six can run.
Rights administration — registration, licensing, and reporting handled, with ownership retained by the writers.
Writer development — the church’s writers coached, connected, and enrolled in the national cohort.
The circulation covenant — free access for small congregations, forever.
Preservation & succession — the catalog archived so it outlives staff transitions and hard drives.

Selection

Why these hundred churches

The first hundred are chosen by fruit and breadth — never by fame, and never by self-nomination.

FruitOriginal worship already in use — songs a congregation actually asks for, not a catalog that exists only on paper.
BreadthAcross streams, denominations, regions, and languages — rural and urban, liturgical and free, large rooms and living rooms.
HealthThe senior pastor’s blessing and the church’s good standing. This work strengthens a local ministry or it does not happen.
Writers willingParticipation invited, never presumed. Some will decline, and declining will be honored without argument.
NominationNamed by denominations, networks, worship leaders, and funding families — with the funder’s own nominations welcomed first.

Selection runs in rounds. Nominations are gathered, the criteria are applied by a small council drawn from more than one stream of the Church, and no church enters the hundred without its own consent. A congregation’s songs are its testimony; the project’s first act of stewardship is asking permission properly.

Some of the hundred will be churches whose songs have already traveled. Most will be rooms nobody outside their own zip code has heard, and that is by design. The known rooms open the door; the unheard rooms are the reason the door exists. The list will never be ranked — a cohort, not a chart — and no names are attached to this prospectus, because the first ten are chosen with the funder, not before the funder.

After Release

One room, many voices

Release is where this project pays. Every produced song enters a shared library, organized by theme, passage, and season — the same index that organizes the preaching in this project’s sister document — so that a volunteer worship leader planning a Sunday on a Thursday night can find a true song, in her key, with charts her team can run, written by a congregation like hers.

The circulation covenant exists for the churches at the bottom of every budget: free access for small congregations, endowed inside each engagement rather than promised from future revenue. Translation is part of release, not an afterthought — a song crosses languages with its theology checked, not just its rhyme — so a catalog born in one language can serve congregations it will never meet.

The giving church gains as much as it gives. A congregation that hears its own testimony produced and sung back to it learns something about its identity no imported catalog can teach. Its writers stop facing the old choice between leaving and being unheard. And its catalog — archived, charted, and cleared — survives the staff transition that today erases most church songbooks.

One further piece is in development: a public ignition moment for the whole project — a gathered recording in which the country’s most trusted worship voices each sing an unknown church writer’s song, premiered not on the radio but in thousands of sanctuaries on a single Sunday. It is designed, not yet commissioned, and this prospectus does not depend on it; the hundred catalogs are the movement whether or not the moment happens.

One room’s song becomes a thousand rooms’ Sunday. That is the multiplication the first hundred sets in motion.

For the Funder

One family, one catalog

This is not a request to fund a label, and it is not a grant that disappears into a budget. The project is structured so that one gift carries one church’s complete catalog — from the voice memos to the finished record — with the funding family attached to it by name if they wish, or in complete anonymity if they prefer.

The design is deliberately relational. The funding family is introduced to the church and its writers. They receive the first pressing of the record. They are invited to the Sunday the congregation first sings its own produced songs — a room hearing its own voice, at full strength, for the first time. Families who fund this kind of work tend to bring their children and grandchildren to that service, and what those generations watch is a family using its resources to give a congregation its voice. It is the kind of room where a family’s own conversation about legacy tends to start itself.

What the gift is not: it is not a purchase of influence over the church or its worship, it carries no rights over the songs or the writers, and it creates no obligation between family and congregation beyond the honor freely given.

Mechanically, the gift is simple. It works as a direct grant, through a donor-advised fund, or inside a family’s existing annual giving plan; it can be paid on commissioning or against milestones; and it attaches to a named engagement, so a family always knows which church’s voice its generosity carried. Recognition follows the family’s instruction exactly — from a dedication in the record’s liner notes to complete anonymity.

For a family accustomed to giving, the leverage is unusual: the engine already exists and already runs, so the gift buys the work itself, not the construction of a capacity to attempt it. A lead gift can carry the entire pilot cohort and, if desired, carry its name.

Accountability

What will be counted

Every funder receives a written report each quarter and an invitation to an annual gathering of funders, churches, and writers. The report counts seven things, the same seven every quarter, so progress can be judged rather than narrated.

Catalogs completed — against the cohort schedule.
Songs gathered and rights-cleared — the running total across all churches.
Songs produced and released — records finished, kits published.
Congregations singing them — reported through the licensing rails the Church already uses.
Writers developed — enrolled, coached, and co-writing across the cohort.
Congregations served free — churches drawing on the library through the covenant.
The stories — testimony from churches, writers, and families, gathered with permission.

The fourth count deserves a sentence, because it is rare. When a congregation sings a licensed song, the use is reported through infrastructure that already exists — which means circulation is counted by the Church’s own rails, not estimated by us. Few ministry gifts of any size can be verified this plainly.

The pilot carries an additional discipline. Its results will be reported whether they flatter the plan or not: if a catalog proves to cost more than the funded figure, funders hear it first, with revised numbers, before the Cohort of Forty is asked for; if less, the difference extends the circulation endowment. What a song does in a heart will be testified to, not measured, and no report will dress testimony up as data.

Why LifeTogether

Built by the people who build campaigns

LifeTogether has spent twenty-five years helping congregations do one thing together in one season — churchwide campaigns, small-group curriculum, devotionals, and publishing — across more than five hundred church relationships, with more than fifty million campaign resources placed in circulation.

Campaign seasons have always been sung as much as preached, and the disciplines this project requires are the ones LifeTogether already operates at church scale: gathering raw material from congregations, editorial and production judgment, publishing, rights stewardship, church liaison by the hundreds, and delivery on a calendar. Where the work needs the recording industry’s craft — producers, engineers, mastering rooms — it will be hired and partnered openly, not pretended to. The gift funds songs through the machine; none of it builds the machine.

Three refusals define the posture. LifeTogether does not take the songs — ownership stays with the writers, and every agreement is written so this remains true after everyone now involved is gone. It does not produce around the church — the congregation’s blessing gates everything released in its name. And it does not run a talent pipeline for anyone’s label — development serves the local church first, and a writer’s success is measured in what her congregation sings, not in whether she leaves it.

The pilot exists to prove all of it on real catalogs, on schedule, with every number reported — before the second cohort is asked for.

Not in these numbers: the gathered national recording, acquisition of existing published catalogs, and the parallel work in preaching — The Pastor’s Library Project, this document’s companion. One treasury, two doors: the preachers’ hundred preserves what was said; the writers’ hundred releases what will be sung. Neither requires the other to succeed.

The Decision

Begin with ten

The decision in front of a funder is one: commission the Pilot Ten — one million dollars, the first ten catalogs produced and released within twelve months, and every number in this prospectus tested against real churches before anything larger is asked.

Three requests come with it. First, help name the first ten — the churches and writers a funder already knows belong at the front of this line. Second, introduce ten families, each to fund one catalog and stand in the room the Sunday that church first hears its own voice. Third, a half-day working session to select the cohort and set the reporting rhythm.

Everything else — the gathering, the production, the publishing, the reporting — is ours to carry.

Year three, if this works, looks like this: one hundred records under one hundred church names. On the order of a thousand songs produced, cleared, and free to the congregations that could never have paid for one. And a generation of writers who no longer face the choice between leaving the church and staying unheard.

Every generation is commanded to add its verse. “Sing to the LORD a new song; sing to the LORD, all the earth” (Psalm 96:1, NIV). The songs are already being written. The singing to all the earth is what this project builds.
