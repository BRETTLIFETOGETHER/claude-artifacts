An Anthem, a Sunday, and a Movement of New Songs
The world was just told, at historic scale, that He gets us. This white paper proposes the Church’s answer — sung together, premiered in thousands of sanctuaries on one morning, and built to release the next generation of worship from every room it is already being written in.

01
The Idea

The answer back

In 1985, one room changed what music could do for a cause. Forty-five of the most famous voices in the world gathered on a single night, checked their egos at the door, and recorded an anthem that raised tens of millions for a famine the world had been watching helplessly. The song worked because everything around it was engineered: a concrete cause, a trusted convener, a borrowed moment, a room protocol, and a premiere so coordinated that thousands of radio stations played it at once.

Forty years later, the Church has just watched a different kind of campaign spend historic sums to tell a skeptical world about Jesus. Whatever one thinks of its choices, it proved something Kingdom funders should not un-see: there is nine-figure conviction available for putting His name in front of the culture. What no campaign has yet attempted is the response — not the world being told about Him, but the Church answering, together, out loud.

We Worship Him Together is that answer, built from three instruments. An anthem, written and recorded by the most trusted worship voices of every stream in one room. A Sunday, on which thousands of congregations sing it simultaneously — the premiere happening not on the radio but in the sanctuaries. And a movement underneath both: a churchwide campaign season and a permanent library that raises up and releases the worship already being written inside local churches, so the moment funds a generation rather than a headline.

The conviction underneath is simple. The local church is meant to be the light of its town — a city on a hill, a brighter place, a better place — in every community, every family, every life. Preaching carries that light, and a companion effort addresses it. But worship has always been the revelatory edge of renewal: when God moves, His people sing something new. This paper is about making room for the new song — and letting it come from everywhere.

02
The Pattern

A hundred years of new songs

Revival never borrows its soundtrack. It writes one. The pattern is older than the century — Methodism was famously born in song — but the last hundred years make it unmistakable, because every genuine movement of God in living memory arrived with new music nobody had heard the year before.

The Pentecostal outpouring at Azusa Street was reported as much for its singing as its preaching. The Billy Graham era moved millions down the aisle to a hymn — an invitation that was, in practice, a song. The Jesus Movement of the early 1970s took beach-baptized hippies with guitars and produced the entire genre we now call contemporary worship; a single church’s music ministry became a label because the new believers would not stop writing. The Vineyard taught a generation to sing to God rather than about Him. Then an Australian congregation’s songs circled the planet, a college-conference movement in Atlanta wrote much of the canon American churches sing today, a Northern Irish hymn restored theological depth to the modern repertoire, and a multiethnic collective built around gospel and friendship gave this decade its sound. When an outpouring came to a small Kentucky college campus in 2023, the world did not watch sermons on its phones. It watched worship.

Two lessons sit inside that history. First, the songs always came from somewhere local before they went everywhere — a church, a campus, a living room; the movement did not license its music, it grew it. Second, in the most recent era, much of what the global Church sang came from a handful of rooms. That season proved how far a song can travel. The question it left behind is whether the next season’s songs can come from every stream, every denomination, every language — fueling the Great Commission inside each tradition rather than around them.

The next movement needs its own songbook. This proposal exists so the songbook comes from everywhere — and so the local church, not any single platform, is the hero of it.

The Anthem

The shape of the song

The anthem the Church sings must be written by the writers in the room — a movement’s song has to be born, not commissioned. What follows is therefore a draft for the writers’ room: original words, no borrowed melody, demonstrating the architecture — verses anyone can carry, a chorus any congregation can sing, and a bridge built from ten voices, one line per stream.

Verse 1
From the last pew of a chapel where the gravel road ends
To a downtown sanctuary where the streetlight bends,
In a thousand living languages one name is raised
By the tired and the trying — He is worthy of praise.
Pre-Chorus
Every generation adds its verse to the song,
Every tribe, every nation carries it on —
Chorus
We worship Him together,
One voice, a thousand rooms,
The God of every generation,
The King of the empty tomb.
Let the walls come down between us
While the watching world looks on:
We worship Him together
Till the whole earth learns the song.
Verse 2
The nurse sings on a Sunday what she prayed through the night,
The farmer hums the chorus in the first morning light,
The children catch the melody before they can read,
And the song outlives the singer like a harvest from its seed.
Bridge — Ten Voices, One Line Each
From the storefront to the steeple,
From the prison to the pew,
From the kitchen-table writer
To the choir breaking through,
From the elders who first taught us
To the child who learns the name,
Every hidden hallelujah,
Every anthem yet unsung —
Come and add your verse to heaven’s,
For the Kingdom yet to come.
Final Chorus — All, Key Up
We worship Him together… (×2)
Tag — A Cappella
One generation to another,
We will tell what He has done.
Arrangement Map
A♭ major, 4/4, ~70 BPM, gospel-anthem build; modulate to B♭ for the double chorus. Verse 1 solo and piano; choir enters in unison at the first chorus; verse 2 as a duet; bridge passes ten solo lines over a building choir; tag lands a cappella. Melody sits inside a tenth — singable by a volunteer team — with charts in G and A♭ on the Church’s licensing rails.

The Room

Ten voices, one night

Worship has no single convener the way pop had one producer in 1985 — no one voice is trusted by Nashville and gospel and the hymn stream and the global south at once. So the room is convened by a council: ten voices, one per stream, gathered for one night. The slate below is illustrative — no invitations have been extended through this document, and the funder helps shape the real list.

The elder chair — a bridge-generation statesman the whole industry honors, to open the room the way only an elder can.
Gospel — its most trusted producer-arranger, and its most honored matriarch of song.
The congregational canon — the most-sung modern writer, and the voice of the global megachurch songbook.
The hymn stream — the couple who returned theological depth to modern repertoire, carrying denominational and liturgical trust.
The current moment — the writer whose crossover duet just proved a church song can carry the mainstream, plus the multiethnic collective’s leading voice.
The global south — the writer whose song became the most-sung in the world without ever passing through Nashville.

Production runs on two levels. In the booth: a co-production pairing gospel’s master arranger with a first-call Nashville worship producer, so the credits themselves make the unity statement. Above the booth, one honorary seat: the surviving co-writer of the 1985 anthem — raised in a church-choir family in Alabama, once bound for the priesthood before music intervened, author of one of pop’s few unashamed gospel hits, and publicly wrestling this very year with people asking him for another anthem. His presence would not be borrowed fame; it would be the original room blessing the new one. His public theology is broad rather than evangelical, so the seat is honorary and historical — the council carries the theology; he carries the lineage.

The Sunday & the Season

Premiered in the sanctuaries

The premiere is the mechanism 1985 could never have imagined: the anthem debuts in thousands of congregations on one morning — charts in every key, clicks and multitracks a volunteer team of six can run, delivered free through the licensing rails churches already use. They owned the airwaves for a day. The Church can own its own liturgy for a morning, and that morning opens a season.

The season is a 40-day churchwide campaign — the format LifeTogether has run for twenty-five years — with six weekend themes: The God We Worship (worship as response to who He is), A Life That Worships (the whole-life offering of Romans 12), The Song of My Story (testimony — every life adds a verse), Made to Make (creativity as worship), Better Together (one Church across generations and streams), and A Light on the Hill (worship as witness to the watching world). Each week carries the full kit: sermon resources, a small-group study, daily devotionals, and youth and children’s editions.

One mechanic makes this campaign unlike any before it: during the forty days, every participating church writes one original song. A writing circle forms in week one, drafts by week four, and on the final Sunday every congregation sings two songs — the anthem the whole Church shares, and the song only their church could have written. Those songs flow into the Worship Library, which means the campaign does not merely celebrate the movement. It generates the movement’s catalog, church by church, season by season.

The sequence, in one paragraph: form the fund, the rights framework, and the first writer roster; convene the council and record the anthem on a borrowed night; premiere it across the sanctuaries on one Sunday and open the 40-day season; release the album of one hundred discoveries — each trusted voice singing an unknown church writer’s song; let the campaign’s new songs and the library’s first hundred catalogs absorb the momentum; then make it a rhythm — an annual New Song Sunday, global-language editions, and a second hundred already forming before the first is finished.

The Cause & the Money

Where every dollar lands

The 1985 room worked because the foundation existed before the session — the money had somewhere governed to land from the first note. This movement follows the same discipline. Before any voice is invited, three structures exist on paper: the fund that receives, the rights framework that protects, and the covenant that gives away.

The cause is deliberately concrete. Every dollar the anthem and the album earn is committed in advance to two destinations: the circulation covenant — free worship resources for small, rural, bivocational, and global-south congregations that could never pay for them — and writer development, the raising up and releasing of the emerging generation already serving on church worship teams. Not an industry funding itself; the Church’s singing funding the churches that cannot afford a song.

The rights framework is the quiet hero. Writers keep ownership of their songs — always, in every instrument of this movement. The anthem itself is held by the fund, so no artist, label, or ministry ever privately profits from it. And the agreements are drafted before the catalog exists, because the one unforgivable failure would be a movement about releasing writers that ended up owning them.

This paper deliberately carries no budget tables. The movement’s funding architecture lives in two companion prospectuses already prepared — The Pastor’s Library Project and The Worship Library Project, each a one-hundred-engagement program with per-engagement economics, phased cohorts, and quarterly counted reporting. The anthem and its Sunday are the ignition; the libraries are the engine the ignition starts. A funder who wants the detail holds it in the same envelope as this paper.

The Window

Why now has a date on it

The largest Jesus-attention window since 2004 is now on the calendar. The sequel to the most successful faith film ever made arrives in two parts — part one in May 2027 and part two in May 2028 — with a major studio behind it and filming already complete, and the leading episodic Jesus series brings its own crucifixion finale to theaters in the spring of 2027. For two consecutive Easter seasons, the Resurrection will be the biggest story in popular culture.

The precedent for what worship should do with that window was set in 2004, when the original film’s release was accompanied by a companion album of songs inspired by it — artists responding to the story rather than scoring the picture. That is the right posture again: this movement does not need placement inside anyone’s film. It needs to be the Church’s sung response standing beside the films — the anthem premiering across sanctuaries in the Easter season the culture is already watching, the 40-day campaign running through Lent, and a second season waiting when part two arrives a year later. Two films, two springs, two campaign seasons. No announced worship companion to the films has surfaced publicly, which means the lane is, for the moment, open.

Two further pieces belong in the window. A documentary, filmed from day one — the 1985 room became a beloved film four decades later and proved the room itself is the story; this movement should capture its own founding night, its writers, and its first hundred churches as it happens, because the documentary becomes the recruiting engine for everything that follows. And a senior pastoral chair for the campaign wing: the movement will need an elder voice pastors across traditions trust to bless a churchwide season — a role, not yet a name, and a conversation the funder can help shape.

Finally, the teaching. A season of worship without a season of the Word would be half a movement, so every campaign ships with its preaching companion — sermon resources, teaching outlines, and the deep library of the parallel preaching effort — so that a local church receives the whole thing at once: the song, the season, the study, and the teaching its pulpit needs to turn the vision into formation.

The Decision

Fund the foundation, then the room

The 1985 lesson, applied: the room comes last. The decision in front of a funder is to commission the foundation phase — form the fund and the rights framework, seat the council chair, enroll the first writers, and convene a half-day working session to set the calendar against the 2027 window. When the room finally gathers, everything its song earns will already have somewhere holy to go.

Three requests come with it. First, bless the formation of the fund and rights framework — the structures that make every later promise true. Second, help seat the council: the two or three elder voices a funder already knows belong at the front of this line. Third, the working session — funder, LifeTogether, and counsel in one room, with the anthem draft, the campaign architecture, and the two companion prospectuses on the table.

If it works, here is the picture: one night, ten streams of the Church in one room. One morning, thousands of congregations singing one new song. One season, every church writing its own. And underneath it all, a treasury that keeps releasing new voices long after the cameras leave.

“He put a new song in my mouth, a hymn of praise to our God. Many will see and fear the Lord and put their trust in him” (Psalm 40:3, NIV). The new song is already being written, in more rooms than anyone has counted. The seeing — and the trusting — is what this movement is for.
