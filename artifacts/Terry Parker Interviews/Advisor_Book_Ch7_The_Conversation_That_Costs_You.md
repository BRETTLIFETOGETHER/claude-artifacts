# A Different Kind of Advisor

## Chapter Seven: The Conversation That Costs You

*Draft one. Roughly 3,400 words. Written to be cut.*

---

There is a conversation that sits at the center of this work, and almost nobody in our profession has it.

I want to describe it precisely, because the vagueness is part of how it survives.

A client walks into your office. He is sixty-one. He built a distribution company over twenty-eight years and a private equity group has come at him with a number, and the number is around twenty million dollars. He has never had twenty million dollars. He has had a company worth twenty million dollars, which is an entirely different thing, and he is about to learn how different.

He wants to talk about the transaction. What he says is that he needs to think through the tax side. What he means, though he will not say it for another forty minutes and possibly not for another year, is that he is terrified, because the thing he has been for twenty-eight years is about to stop existing, and he does not know what he will be on the other side of it.

You know what you should tell him. Some part of you knows it before he finishes his first sentence.

You should tell him that if he intends to be generous with any portion of this, the decision has to happen now, before there is a binding agreement, because after that the tax code will treat the gift as though he sold the asset himself and gave away what was left. You should tell him that a gift of company stock before the deal closes is worth roughly twice what the same felt sacrifice is worth after it. You should tell him that the moment to decide what this money is for is now, while it is still theoretical, because in ninety days it will be real and he will be a different man about it.

And you should tell him that his children are watching, and that whatever he does in the next six months will teach them more about money than everything he has said to them across their entire lives.

You know all of this. You have known it for years.

Now let me put the other number on the table.

If he gives twenty percent before the sale, four million dollars never arrives on your platform. At a hundred basis points that is forty thousand dollars a year, and it is not forty thousand dollars once. It is forty thousand dollars compounding across the rest of your career and then across the career of whoever buys your book. Run it out twenty years at a reasonable growth assumption and you are looking at something north of a million dollars of firm value that will not exist because you opened your mouth.

Nobody will ever know you didn't. There is no audit for a conversation that did not happen. There is no compliance flag for wisdom withheld. If you simply talk about the tax structure and let the charitable question go unasked, you will have done nothing wrong by any standard your profession recognizes, your client will be satisfied, your revenue will be intact, and you will go home.

That is the conversation. And I have watched a great many good men not have it.

---

### The three ways we avoid it

I want to be careful here, because I am not describing crooks. I have spent my career among people who love God and love their clients, and the avoidance I am describing is almost never a decision. It is a drift. But it has a shape, and the shape repeats, and I have come to recognize three versions of it.

The first is simply **silence**. The subject never comes up. There is always a reason, and the reasons are good ones. He didn't raise it. It wasn't the right meeting. He's not that kind of client. His wife handles the giving. We'll get to it after the transaction settles. Every one of those sentences is true and every one of them is also an exit, and the man who uses them is rarely aware that he has walked through a door.

The second is **lateness**, and it is more troubling than silence because it looks like faithfulness. You raise the charitable question after the letter of intent is signed. You are now, by any observable measure, an advisor who talks to his clients about generosity. You have said the words. But you have said them at the one point in the timeline where they cannot cost you anything, because the window closed before you spoke and the gift will now come out of after-tax proceeds sitting on your platform, where you will continue to manage it and be paid on it while it waits to be granted.

I do not think most men do this deliberately. I think the timing has a way of arranging itself. But I would ask any advisor reading this to go back through the last five liquidity events in his practice and write down the date of the charitable conversation relative to the date of the letter of intent. That is a five-minute exercise and it is not a comfortable one.

The third is **smallness**. You raise it, you raise it early, and you frame it around a tithe on the proceeds. Ten percent of the cash, after tax, given over some years. This is a good and biblical instinct and it is also, in a transaction of this kind, the version of the conversation that costs you the least while sounding the most spiritual. A tithe on twenty million after tax is meaningfully less than a gift of twenty percent of the stock beforehand, and the difference is not small change. It is the difference between funding a building and funding a generation.

I have used all three. That is why I can describe them.

---

### Why the usual defense does not hold

When I have raised this with other advisors, and I have raised it more times than has been good for my popularity, the response comes back in almost identical words.

*I would never let my compensation affect my advice.*

I believe the men who say it. That is precisely the problem.

Bias does not arrive announcing itself. It does not show up as a moment where you weigh forty thousand dollars against your client's soul and choose the money. If it did, we would all pass, because that choice is not hard for anyone who loves God. Bias shows up as the meeting running long. It shows up as your instinct that today is not the day. It shows up as a genuine, sincere, defensible professional judgment that this client is not ready for that conversation, a judgment you have made forty times and which has been correct often enough that you have stopped noticing it is also, every single time, the judgment that protects your revenue.

The conflict does not corrupt your advice. It edits your agenda. And an edited agenda is invisible, including to the man holding it.

There is a reason Scripture treats money as a rival authority rather than a neutral tool. *No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money.* That is Jesus in Matthew 6, and notice what he does not say. He does not say it is difficult. He does not say it requires vigilance. He says it cannot be done, and he says it to people who almost certainly considered themselves men of integrity, because everyone does.

Nine verses later in Luke's version of the same teaching, he adds the line that should be printed on the wall of every firm in this business. *So if you have not been trustworthy in handling worldly wealth, who will trust you with true riches? And if you have not been trustworthy with someone else's property, who will give you property of your own?*

Someone else's property. That is the job description. It is the whole job description.

### The two men who solved it, and the one who didn't

I know of exactly three responses to this problem that hold up, and only two of them are real.

The first man left. He was an attorney, not an advisor, but the structure of his conflict was identical, and when the charitable work began to compete with the billable work he went to his partners and told them he wanted to spend real time on the thing that generated no fees. They cut his draw by a third. He accepted it, and then he spent thirteen more years building something that did not pay him, and for most of those years it did not work.

I want to be honest about what that solution requires, because it is regularly held up as an example in a way that lets the rest of us off. It requires a man to be at a stage of life, and a level of household expense, and a level of marital agreement, where a one-third income reduction is survivable. Most thirty-eight-year-olds with three children and a mortgage do not have that option, and telling them the answer is to leave is not counsel. It is a way of ending the conversation.

The second man restructured. He built a practice where the charitable conversation was not an exception to the business model but the point of it, where the firm's own scorecard counted dollars given alongside dollars managed, and where an advisor who talked a client into a large pre-sale gift was celebrated in the same meeting where the revenue was reported. That is harder than leaving and it is also reproducible, which leaving is not.

The third response is the one almost everyone actually uses, and it is not a solution. It is a hope. The hope is that personal integrity will be sufficient, and that a good man in a bad structure will behave well indefinitely because he intends to. I have never seen that work across a full career. I have seen it work for years at a time, in men I admire, and then I have watched the structure win quietly somewhere around year eleven, usually during a bad market, usually without anyone noticing including the man himself.

Structure beats intention. Not always in a given week. Always over twenty years.

---

### What can actually be built

If you are not going to leave, and you should not have to, then the work is to build something that does not require you to be a hero every Tuesday.

**Separate the planning fee from the assets.** The single most powerful change available to most practices is to charge a real, meaningful, standalone fee for the planning work, so that a substantial portion of your compensation is indifferent to whether the client's assets stay or go. This is not a trick. It is an honest reflection of where the value actually was. The thinking you did about his family and his timeline and his identity after the sale was worth more than the allocation, and you have been giving it away free and recovering it on the spread. Charge for it. You will find that clients pay it without blinking, because they always suspected it was the valuable part.

**Do the charitable work at cost, and do it anyway.** Some firms manage donor-advised assets at reduced or zero basis points. It sounds like a sacrifice and it functions as a magnet, because it removes every ambiguity from the recommendation, and clients can smell an unambiguous recommendation from across a room.

**Put the giving on the scorecard.** Whatever your firm measures in its quarterly meeting is what your firm actually values, regardless of what is on the website. If assets under management is the only number read out loud, then generous counsel is a personal hobby that some advisors happen to have. Read the other number out loud. Dollars given, gifts of non-cash assets completed, families who established a giving plan. Advisors respond to what gets counted, and this is the cheapest structural change on the list.

**Track the date.** Institute one discipline: for every client approaching a liquidity event, the charitable conversation is logged with a date, and that date is compared to the date of the letter of intent. Not as a compliance matter. As a mirror. You cannot manage a bias you cannot see, and this makes it visible in a single column.

---

### The sentence

All of that is structure, and structure matters more than most advisors want to believe. But there is one move available to you tomorrow morning, requiring no firm approval and no change to your fee schedule, and in my experience it does more than all the rest of it combined.

Tell the client about the conflict.

Say it out loud, in plain words, before you make the recommendation. Something close to this:

*Before I say the next part, I want you to know something. If you do what I am about to suggest, four million dollars leaves this firm and does not come back, and I am paid on assets, so this recommendation costs me money every year for the rest of my career. I want you to hear it anyway, because I think it is right, and because you should be able to weigh my advice knowing what it costs me to give it.*

I have watched that sentence change rooms.

Here is why it works, and it is not a technique, which is why it will stop working if you use it as one. Every wealthy man in America has spent his adult life being advised by people with an undisclosed stake in the outcome. He knows it. He has priced it in. He listens to every recommendation through a filter that asks what this person gets out of it, and the filter never turns off, and it is exhausting, and it is one of the loneliest features of having money.

When you name your own stake before he has to go looking for it, you do something almost nobody has ever done for him. You hand him the filter. And the effect is not that he trusts the recommendation. The effect is that he trusts you, permanently, about everything, including things you will not discuss for another decade.

The caution attached to this is severe and I will not soften it. If you say that sentence as a closing move, if you deploy it because you read it here and it sounded effective, he will know. Men who have been sold to for forty years have extraordinarily fine instruments for detecting sincerity, and a manufactured disclosure is worse than no disclosure, because it converts a real conflict into a performance about the conflict. Say it only if it is true, and only if you are prepared for him to take your advice.

---

### Releasing the outcome

There is a pattern running underneath this whole book and this is where it becomes hardest to practice.

The first release was your ideas, and it cost you credit. The second was your people, and it cost you control. This is the third one, and in a practice it takes a specific form.

Your outcome is assets under management. That is the number that tells you whether the year was good. It is the number you compare against last year and against the man down the hall, and it is the number that determines what your practice is worth when you are finished. It is not a vain number. It is how you feed your family and fund your children's education and eventually retire, and it is a legitimate measure of real value delivered to real people.

And it cannot be the thing you are protecting when you sit across from a man who is about to sell his company.

I am not going to tell you that letting go of it pays. I have seen it pay. Generous clients tend to refer generous clients, they tend to stay for a second and third generation, they tend to be less reactive in bad markets because their identity is less fused to the balance, and there is a version of this argument where releasing the outcome is simply better business over a thirty-year horizon.

But I distrust that argument in my own mouth, because it is the prosperity gospel wearing a suit. It says: give up the thing and God will hand it back with interest. That is not what happened to the attorney who took the pay cut, at least not for thirteen years, and it is not what the text promises.

So here is the honest version, and it is the only one I am willing to put on the page.

You may lose money. Some of the counsel you give, if you give it early and give it fully, will permanently reduce what your practice is worth. That is not a risk to be mitigated. It is the actual price, it is knowable in advance, and you should count it before you decide.

Do it anyway.

Do it because you are handling someone else's property, and because the man in front of you has exactly one chance at this and will not get another, and because his children are going to learn what money is for by watching what he does in the next six months, and because you are the only person in his life positioned to say the thing at the moment when saying it still matters.

That is what makes an advisor different. Not the credential, not the Scripture on the wall, and not the intention. The willingness to open your mouth at the one moment when opening it costs you something.

### For reflection

*Go back through your last five liquidity events. When did the charitable conversation happen relative to the letter of intent? Write the dates down before you explain them.*

*What percentage of your revenue is indifferent to whether a client gives an asset away? If the answer is near zero, that is not a moral failing, but it is a structure you did not choose deliberately and can now choose deliberately.*

*Is there a client right now, this month, with whom you have been privately postponing this conversation? Name him. The postponement always has a reason and the reason is always good.*
