**THE WISDOM QUESTION · What Does the Bible Actually Say About Money? (Week 2)**
**Day 9 — Giving Breaks Money's Power: Generosity Loosens the Grip**

GENEROSITY IS NOT WHAT YOU DO AFTER YOU FEEL SECURE. IT IS HOW YOU LEARN THAT GOD, NOT MONEY, IS YOUR SECURITY.

Daily Inspiration | Watch today's short video: [URL]

**Proverbs 11:24-25 (NIV):** "One person gives freely, yet gains even more; another withholds unduly, but comes to poverty. A generous person will prosper; whoever refreshes others will be refreshed."
**Acts 20:35 (NIV):** "In everything I did, I showed you that by this kind of hard work we must help the weak, remembering the words the Lord Jesus himself said: 'It is more blessed to give than to receive.'"

**She Paid It Back With Interest**

A woman whose testimony was shared through our work began giving the moment she came to faith and never stopped. She had grown up in a family of givers, people who wanted others to survive and thrive, and though she did not fully understand the theology of it, she knew from the start that it was all God's, and she wanted to give back to him what he had given her. So from the time she became a Christian, she gave ten percent, and she kept giving it.

Then came a hard stretch. Her marriage was coming apart, and money was leaving the household faster than she could track it, in ways she could not control. There were months when everything in her wanted to hold the tithe back, to use it for the bills that were pressing in from every side. She describes what she did instead in a way I have not been able to forget. When she came up short and could not give the full amount, she would pay God back later, with interest. That tug in her heart to give was stronger than the fear telling her she could not afford to. And her testimony is simple. Even when money was short, God always provided. She got through those seasons, and looking back, she cannot separate the provision from the giving. The two are bound together in her memory, not as a transaction, but as the record of a person who kept trusting God with the very thing she was most afraid to release.

> **A Note on Tithing.** One of the most historic and foundational expressions of giving in the Christian life is the tithe. Different churches may emphasize storehouse giving, or point to the range of percentages Scripture describes for different kinds of tithing. What is consistent from Genesis through Revelation, and from the widow's mite to the challenge to the rich young ruler, is that God asks us to give to his people and to those in need. We encourage you to discover and follow the practice of your own church. For our purposes here, we simply ask that you pray for God's direction about what your consistent giving to your church should be. Further reading and guidance on tithing is provided in the supplemental materials for this module.

**What Giving Breaks**

Generosity is not the final chapter reserved for wealthy people who have taken care of everything else first. It is foundational, and it does something in the human heart that almost nothing else can. Proverbs says it plainly. One person gives freely, yet gains even more. Another withholds unduly, but comes to poverty. That is not a promise that generosity is a strategy for getting rich. It is a description of what happens in the soul. Stinginess shrinks a person. Generosity enlarges them. An advisor I know puts it in exactly those terms, that we break the power of money over our hearts by giving it away. Money makes its strongest claim on us through the quiet lie that security comes from holding on tighter, and every act of open-handed giving is a direct contradiction of that lie.

This is why giving is so central and not peripheral. It is the one financial act that money itself resists most, because giving loosens the grip. When you give, you are declaring, in the most concrete way available to you, that the thing you released was not your true security in the first place. Something in the clenched hand relaxes. The fear that told you there would not be enough is exposed as the fear it always was, and the money returns to its proper size, a useful servant rather than a master with a claim on your peace.

**Giving Is How You Practice Trust**

Here is the shift I most want you to see. Most people assume giving is something you do after you feel secure. You take care of your own needs, build a comfortable margin, arrive at a settled sense that you will be okay, and then, from that place of safety, you give. But that gets the order exactly backward. Giving is not what you do once you feel secure. It is how you learn that God, not money, is your security. It is a way of practicing trust, and trust is a muscle that only develops under load. You do not learn that God provides by first arranging your affairs so that you never need him to. You learn it by giving from a place that still requires faith, and then watching what he does.

That is precisely what the woman in this story discovered. She did not give from surplus after the storm had passed. She gave through the storm, when the math argued against it, and the giving itself became the place where she learned that her security had never been the balance in the account. This is also why generosity must be driven by gratitude rather than guilt. Guilt-driven giving is just another form of anxiety, an attempt to buy peace or to quiet a nagging conscience. Gratitude-driven giving flows from a heart that has already grasped the gospel logic, that everything it holds was a gift in the first place. You do not give to earn what God offers. You give because you have begun to believe that he has already given you himself, and everything else is a response to that.

**The Freedom in Open Hands**

There is a reason this teaching sits inside the larger question of freedom. An open hand can do two things a closed hand cannot. It can give, and it can receive. The hand clenched around what it has is capable of neither. It cannot bless anyone, and it cannot be blessed, because it is entirely occupied with holding. Generosity is the financial expression of the open hand, and the freedom it produces is not primarily the recipient's. It is the giver's. A person who has learned to give freely has, in the process, been freed from the thing that quietly rules most human hearts.

People who walk this road describe a pattern that repeats until it stops surprising them. The more they are able to see a genuine benefit for someone else, the more they sense God leading them to give toward a need or a cause, the more freedom they find in the releasing. And then, not as a payment but as a kindness, they watch God provide again, time after time, until the giving and the provision become a rhythm they trust. That is what Jesus meant in words Paul thought worth preserving. It is more blessed to give than to receive. Not more obligatory. More blessed. The one who gives is the one who ends up freer, lighter, and less afraid.

**Give First, Not Last**

There is a practical dimension to this that determines whether generosity ever actually takes root in a life, and it comes down to order. In a faithful financial life, giving comes first. It is not funded from whatever happens to survive to the end of the month. Most households do the opposite without ever deciding to. Living expenses absorb the income as it arrives, and giving gets whatever remains, which in a month without careful attention is often very little. So the giving quietly shrinks, and the household wonders why generosity never seems to gain any traction. The answer is almost always the order. Giving that comes last comes small, or comes not at all.

First-fruits giving reverses that sequence. You decide on the amount and set it aside first, before the other claims on your income have a chance to reach it. This is not merely a budgeting technique. It is a statement of priority made in the most concrete terms available, because what you fund first reveals what you actually treasure. When giving is first, it is protected from being the thing that gets squeezed out the moment money feels tight, and money always feels tight at some point. That protection matters precisely because the seasons when giving is hardest are the seasons when the trust it builds does the most work in you. The woman whose testimony opened this reading understood that instinctively. She did not give from the leftovers. She gave first, and when she could not, she made it right afterward, because in her mind the giving was not negotiable, it was foundational. That is the posture this principle is teaching, and it is worth building into the structure of your finances rather than leaving to whatever the month allows.

**Think About It**

Think honestly about your own giving. Is it something you do from surplus after your own security feels handled, or something you do as an act of trust while it still requires faith? What would change if you began to see giving as the way you learn that God is your security rather than the reward for having already found it elsewhere?

The teaching here is that generosity should be driven by gratitude, not guilt. When you give, which of those two is usually the engine? What would gratitude-driven giving look like in your life this week, as opposed to the giving that is really just a quieting of conscience?

**Exercise**

Take ten minutes and look honestly at what you gave over the last year, not to judge the amount but to understand the pattern. Then ask two questions in writing. Did I give from surplus, after my own security felt handled, or did any of it require actual trust? And what is one specific, gratitude-driven act of generosity I could take this week, small or large, that would loosen the grip of money on my heart? Then do the one thing you named, and pay attention to what it does in you, not only to the recipient.

**Declaration**

I will not wait to feel secure before I give. Giving is how I learn that God is my security. I open my hands today, to release fear and to receive what He provides.

**Next Steps**

Option A. Give something this week that requires a measure of trust, not merely what is left over and easy. Let the act itself be the declaration that God, not the balance in your account, is your security.

Option B. Identify one person or need God has placed in front of you. Before you calculate whether you can afford it, ask a different question first. Is this something God is inviting me to trust him with? Then respond, and watch what he does.

**Closing Prayer**

Father, You have given first and given everything, and every good thing I hold came from Your hand before it came into mine. Forgive me for the times I have clenched my fist around Your resources out of fear that there would not be enough. Teach me that generosity is not what I do once I feel safe, but the very way I learn that You are my safety. Break the power money has held over my heart, and let me give freely, out of gratitude rather than guilt, until my hands are open enough both to bless others and to receive what You provide. In Jesus' name, amen.

**THE WISDOM QUESTION · What Does the Bible Actually Say About Money? (Week 2)**
**Day 10 — Margin and Direction: Plan Ahead and Aim Somewhere**

MARGIN LETS YOU SURVIVE TODAY. DIRECTION TELLS YOU WHERE TODAY IS HEADED. YOU NEED BOTH.

Daily Inspiration | Watch today's short video: [URL]

**Proverbs 21:5 (NIV):** "The plans of the diligent lead to profit as surely as haste leads to poverty."
**Luke 14:28 (NIV):** "Suppose one of you wants to build a tower. Won't you first sit down and estimate the cost to see if you have enough money to complete it?"

**Two Households, Same Income**

Over nearly fifty years of sitting with people and their money, I have watched a difference play out so consistently that I can almost predict it. Take two households with roughly the same income. One drifts. Money comes in, money goes out, decisions get made in the moment as needs and wants present themselves, and there is never quite a plan, only a series of reactions. The other household, earning no more, has decided a few things in advance. They know roughly where their money is supposed to go before it arrives, and they have some sense of where they are trying to end up. Give those two households a decade, and they will be in entirely different places, not because one earned more, but because one was diligent and the other simply let money happen to it.

That is the whole point of today's reading. The two remaining principles from Ron Blue's framework, planning for margin and setting long-term goals, are really two halves of one idea. Margin lets you survive today. Direction tells you where today is headed. A household with margin but no direction has breathing room and nowhere to go. A household with direction but no margin has a destination and no way to get there. You need both.

**Planning Is Not Control, It Is Stewardship**

People hear the word planning and immediately think of control, as though making a plan were a way of pretending you are sovereign over a future you cannot actually command. That is not what biblical planning is. Planning is stewardship. Scripture calls it diligence, and it commends it without embarrassment. The plans of the diligent lead to profit as surely as haste leads to poverty. Diligence is not anxiety. It is wisdom applied over time, the humble posture of a manager who says, Lord, I am going to be faithful with what You have entrusted to me, and being faithful means thinking ahead rather than lurching from one surprise to the next.

This is where margin does its quiet work. Diligence does not guarantee a perfect life, but it does create stability, and stability creates room to breathe and room to respond when life hits. Margin is what keeps a normal surprise from becoming a crisis. It is the difference between saying that is inconvenient and saying we are in trouble. A household living one surprise away from panic is not free, no matter how it looks from the outside, because everything in it is fragile. Plan ahead simply means do not live on impulse. Build enough margin that the ordinary shocks of a human life, the car repair, the medical bill, the month the income dips, land as inconveniences rather than emergencies. That kind of planning is not a failure to trust God. It is one of the most concrete ways a steward expresses trust, acknowledging reality without surrendering to anxiety.

**Aim Somewhere**

Margin helps you survive today, but survival is not the goal, and this is where the second half comes in. What about direction? Where are you actually headed? If you do not decide where you are going, you will drift, and drifting rarely feels dramatic. It feels normal, right up until the moment it becomes a crisis. Long-term goals give your money a purpose beyond the immediate moment. They help you make decisions today that you will be grateful for later, because a decision made with a destination in view is a different decision than one made purely by impulse.

The goals themselves do not have to be elaborate. In fact, the most useful ones are usually simple questions asked honestly. What kind of stability do we want a year from now? What kind of generosity do we want to be ready for? What kind of impact do we want our financial life to have made over time? When you connect goals like these to God's purposes rather than merely to your own comfort, something shifts in how you carry money. You stop living in reactive mode. You move from anxiety to clarity, from short-term pressure to steady progress. And over time, that is exactly what allows margin to grow rather than leak away, because now the margin has somewhere to go. It is being directed toward something rather than dispersed into whatever the moment happens to want. Jesus pointed at this same wisdom when he asked who would begin building a tower without first sitting down to estimate the cost. He was not discouraging the building. He was commending the sitting down, the honest reckoning of where you are trying to go and what it will take to get there.

**Prepare Because You Are Faithful, Not Because You Are Scared**

I want to draw a distinction here that matters more than it first appears, because two people can build the exact same margin for entirely different reasons, and the reason shapes everything about how the habit holds over time. One person saves because the future feels threatening. The larger the reserve grows, the more temporary the relief, because the fear driving the accumulation is never actually satisfied by a number. There is always a scenario the current amount cannot cover, and the anxiety simply recalibrates upward, chasing the figure that would finally feel like enough, a figure that never arrives. This person is building margin, but the margin is in service of a security that money was never able to provide.

The other person prepares because stewardship requires it. The question driving the behavior is not how much do I need in order to feel safe, but how do I prepare faithfully and stay available to God. Those are different questions, and they produce a different kind of peace. A reserve of a few months of expenses does not guarantee safety, because no amount does. What it does is keep an ordinary disruption from becoming a crisis, which in turn keeps you free to respond when God calls rather than being pinned down by panic. Planning and preparation, held with the right heart, are not enemies of faith. They are expressions of it. You are acknowledging reality without surrendering to anxiety, doing in the calm season what the calm season is for, so that you are ready when the season changes. The behavior looks identical from the outside. The heart behind it is the difference between a person quietly ruled by fear and a person freely practicing trust.

**How Much Is Enough Is a Direction Question**

There is one long-term question that gives shape to all the others, and most people never sit down to answer it. How much is enough? A man whose journey I have followed spent a successful career in hospital leadership, faithfully giving from the beginning of his marriage, and for years he simply kept accumulating the way most successful people do, without a defined destination. Then he encountered the idea that God owns it all and the accompanying question of how much is actually enough, and he describes going through that exercise as the place where he found real freedom. He and his wife did the work of identifying what they genuinely needed to live at a lifestyle they felt was appropriate, and then asked the harder question underneath it: what does God want us to do with the rest? He calls stewardship a wrestling, and he is right. How much do we keep, how much do we give, how much goes to our children, and what is the timing of all of it. But the wrestling only becomes possible once you are willing to name a finish line rather than assuming the goal is simply more, indefinitely, forever.

That is what long-term direction ultimately requires. Not a spreadsheet of retirement projections alone, but an honest answer to where you are trying to end up and why. A household that has never asked how much is enough has no way to know when it has crossed from provision into hoarding, or from prudent saving into anxious accumulation. Naming the destination is what converts a lifetime of financial decisions from aimless drift into stewardship with a purpose. It is the most consequential long-term goal there is, because every other goal takes its meaning from it.

**Pick One Thing**

We have now walked through all five principles across these Wisdom days, and here is where people most often go wrong. They hear all five, feel the weight of how far they have to go, and attempt a complete financial overhaul in a single week. That sounds noble, and it almost always ends in discouragement. So I want to give you the opposite instruction. Do not try to do all five perfectly. Choose one.

Pick the single principle you most often violate, the one you sensed most sharply as we worked through them, and take one concrete step this week that proves you are serious. If it is spending, track every dollar for seven days. If it is debt, list every debt and choose your first payoff target. If it is giving, decide on a first-fruits amount and set it aside first, before anything else has a chance to claim it. If it is planning, set one small margin goal and begin to build toward it. One principle, one step, this week. Because wisdom is not a feeling that arrives all at once. Wisdom is a path, and a path is walked one step at a time. Take the step, and then next week take the next one. Over time, that unremarkable rhythm produces exactly what most people are desperate for and cannot buy: not merely more money, but contentment, stability, and the ability to breathe.

**Think About It**

Between margin and direction, which is more missing in your financial life right now? Do you have breathing room but no real sense of where you are headed, or a destination in mind with no margin to move toward it? What would it take to strengthen the weaker of the two this season?

Of the five principles we have covered, name the one you most consistently violate. What is the single concrete step you could take this week to begin practicing it, and what has kept you from taking that step until now?

**Exercise**

Do two things this week. First, name one long-term direction for your money, phrased as an honest question answered: a year from now, the stability, generosity, or impact I want our finances aimed at is ___. Keep it simple and real. Second, choose the one principle from these Wisdom days that you most need to practice, and commit to a single concrete step toward it before the week ends. Write both down where you will see them. Direction without a next step is only a wish, and a next step without direction is only motion. Put the two together.

**Declaration**

I will not let my money drift. I will build margin to survive today and set direction to know where today is headed, taking one faithful step at a time toward what God is calling me to.

**Next Steps**

Option A. Sit down for fifteen minutes and write one long-term financial goal connected to God's purposes, not just your comfort. Then name the very first step toward it, and put that step on your calendar this week.

Option B. Choose the one principle you most need to practice and take a single concrete action today: track a day of spending, list one debt, set aside a first-fruits gift, or move a small amount into savings. One action. Prove to yourself that the path has begun.

**Closing Prayer**

Father, thank You that planning is not a failure to trust You but a way of being faithful with what You have entrusted to me. Forgive me where I have let money simply happen to me, reacting to each moment without margin and without direction. Give me the diligence to build breathing room into my finances, and the wisdom to aim that margin at goals that reflect Your purposes rather than only my comfort. And keep me from the discouragement of trying to fix everything at once. Show me the one step to take this week, and give me the faith to take it, trusting that You will meet me on the path as I walk it one step at a time. In Jesus' name, amen.

**THE FREEDOM QUESTION · How Do I Have True Financial Freedom? (Week 2)**
**Day 11 — Freedom Is Not Independence From God: Dependence Is the Path to Strength**

TRUE FINANCIAL INDEPENDENCE IS NOT INDEPENDENCE FROM GOD. IT IS DEPENDENCE ON GOD WITH THE COURAGE TO OBEY, ONE STEP AT A TIME.

Daily Inspiration | Watch today's short video: [URL]

**Hebrews 13:5 (NIV):** "Keep your lives free from the love of money and be content with what you have, because God has said, 'Never will I leave you; never will I forsake you.'"
**2 Corinthians 12:9 (NIV):** "But he said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.' Therefore I will boast all the more gladly about my weaknesses, so that Christ's power may rest on me."

**The Musician Who Stopped Balancing**

A man connected to our work has played music since he was ten years old and spent years traveling the world as a professional musician, performing at the highest levels of his field. It was a life his family had made possible through instruments and lessons, and for a long time it defined him. But the life of a performing artist carries a great deal of temptation and uncertainty, and at a certain point he found himself living in compromise with the way he had been raised. He had grown up in the church. He decided he needed to step away from that lifestyle for a while, simply to figure out who he was and where he was going.

That was many years ago now. The turning point, as he tells it, was coming back to really knowing the Lord, and when that happened his whole focus changed. His attention shifted from the next show or the next opportunity toward his family and toward who he might benefit in the years ahead. His work has never offered much security in the ordinary sense. Self-employment in the arts means seasons of work and seasons of waiting, seasons of contentment and seasons of restless wishing you were somewhere else. And yet he describes his entire relationship with money as bound up with his spiritual life, shaped by a single recurring prayer. God, what do You want with my life? It is Yours. He calls it a place of brokenness and dependency, and he does not say that with regret. He says it has been the most helpful thing. The freedom he has found is not in finally arranging enough security to stop needing God. It is in the discovery that the more he senses God leading him to give or to serve, the more freedom he experiences, and the more he watches God provide again. When people ask him how to balance the uncertainty of it all, his answer is disarming. You do not personally balance it. God balances it for you. He leads you. He guides you.

**Freedom Is Not a Number**

When most people hear the word freedom applied to money, they immediately think of a number. Enough saved, enough income, enough distance from worry that they can finally exhale. But that is not what biblical freedom is. Freedom is the condition in which money stops being the thing that quietly controls you, your fear, your relationships, your choices. And Scripture ties that freedom not to an amount but to contentment, and contentment to the presence of God. Keep your lives free from the love of money and be content with what you have, because God has said, Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you. Notice how the verse gets there. The ground of contentment is not a sum in an account. It is a promise of presence. You can be content with what you have because the One who owns it all has said he will never leave.

This is why chasing a number never delivers the freedom people expect. Contentment is never an amount of money. It is a condition of the heart, and a condition of the heart cannot be purchased by any balance, however large. I have watched people cross every financial threshold they once believed would finally make them feel free, only to discover that the same anxiety was waiting on the other side. The freedom they were looking for was never going to come from the number, because it was never a math problem. It was a trust problem, and trust has a different currency entirely.

**Dependence Is the Path to Strength**

Here is the truth the world gets exactly backward. Our culture treats independence as the highest form of freedom, the state of needing no one and answering to nothing. But true financial independence is not independence from God. It is dependence on God, with the courage to obey his wisdom one step at a time. The musician found his freedom not by escaping his need but by surrendering into it, praying it is Yours and then watching God carry what he could not. Paul learned the same paradox and stated it directly. When God told him his grace was sufficient and his power was made perfect in weakness, Paul stopped resenting his weakness and began to boast in it, because it was precisely there that Christ's power rested on him. Dependence is not the opposite of strength. For the believer, it is the path to it.

Fear will tell you the opposite. Fear says you are alone, that if you do not fix this quickly you are doomed, and it makes everything feel urgent, so that you either panic, or avoid, or numb out. But God says something different into that fear. I am with you, right here, while you take the next step. And when God is with you, you do not have to sprint. You just have to walk. That single reframe changes how a person carries financial pressure. The problem does not vanish, but the aloneness does, and the aloneness was always the heaviest part. You are not trying to outrun the future by yourself. You are taking one faithful step at a time alongside the One who has promised never to leave.

**Close to the Brokenhearted**

If you are reading this while feeling behind, or ashamed, or crushed by pressure you do not say out loud, I want you to hear the tenderness in how God speaks to exactly your situation. He is close to the brokenhearted and saves those who are crushed in spirit. God is not standing at a distance, waiting for you to impress him with a turnaround story before he will draw near. He draws close to the discouraged. That means the very condition that makes you want to hide, the sense of being behind or of having already made too many mistakes, is precisely the condition God meets with strength and tenderness rather than judgment.

So do not let shame set the terms. Shame says hide. Wisdom says take the next step. Shame says you are behind. Wisdom says you are on a path. The difference between those two voices determines whether a person moves forward or stays frozen, and the freedom described here belongs to the one who lets wisdom, not shame, do the talking. You do not have to have it all resolved to begin. You only have to take one honest, obedient step, in dependence on the God who is already close, and then the next one, and then the one after that.

**The Shape of the Next Step**

It is worth being concrete about what that next step actually looks like, because freedom does not arrive the way most people expect. Freedom does not happen by one emotional decision, one dramatic turnaround, one great resolution to finally get it all together. Freedom happens by a path, walked one faithful step at a time. And the steps are almost always small and specific. One call. One plan. One expense you pause. One bill you clarify. One automated savings move. One honest conversation. One act of obedience. Not ten of them at once, which is how people set themselves up to fail. One.

The reason this matters is that the size of the step is what makes it possible to take at all. When a person imagines they must leap from where they are to some far-off place of security in a single bound, the distance paralyzes them, and paralysis feels like proof that they are stuck. But God is not asking for the leap. He is asking for the next measurable move, taken today, in the direction you already know is right. And because he has promised to be with you in it, you do not have to make that move alone or in a panic. You name where you are, honestly, without comparing your position to anyone else's, and then you take one step. Then tomorrow you take another. Do that imperfectly but faithfully over time, and you will discover something most people never experience: not merely more control over money, but a deeper kind of freedom, the kind that finally lets you breathe, and sleep, and live at peace, because your trust is anchored in the One who said he will never leave.

**Think About It**

When you imagine financial freedom, is it a number you are picturing or a condition of the heart? Where have you assumed that reaching a certain amount would finally make you free, and what would it mean to locate your freedom in God's presence instead?

The distinction here is between shame, which says hide, and wisdom, which says take the next step. In your financial life right now, which voice tends to win? What is one place where fear or shame has kept you frozen, and what would the next faithful step look like there?

**Exercise**

Take ten minutes and write down the number, spoken or unspoken, that you have believed would finally make you feel financially secure. Then sit honestly with a question. If you reached it tomorrow, do you actually believe the anxiety would be gone, or would it recalibrate to a new number? Underneath what you wrote, write Hebrews 13:5 in your own words as a different foundation for security, and name one financial fear you are choosing today to carry in dependence on God rather than alone.

**Declaration**

My freedom is not a number and my security is not an amount. God has said He will never leave me. I will depend on Him, obey Him one step at a time, and let that be my strength.

**Next Steps**

Option A. Name the one financial worry that most reliably tells you that you are alone. Say it out loud to God, and then say back to it the promise He made: Never will I leave you. Let that be the ground you stand on as you take today's next step.

Option B. If shame has kept you frozen in some area of your finances, break the freeze with one honest action today: look at the number you have avoided, tell a trusted person the truth, or ask for help. Let wisdom, not shame, decide your next move.

**Closing Prayer**

Father, I confess how easily I have believed the lie that freedom means needing no one, including You. I have chased a number, thinking it would finally quiet the fear, when all along You were offering me something the number could never give. Thank You that You have promised never to leave me and never to forsake me, and that Your power is made perfect precisely in my weakness. Teach me that dependence on You is not the opposite of strength but the path to it. Draw close to me where I feel behind or ashamed, and give me the courage to take the next faithful step, not alone and not sprinting, but walking with You. In Jesus' name, amen.
