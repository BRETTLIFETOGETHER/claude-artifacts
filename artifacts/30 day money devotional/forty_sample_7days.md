DAY 1
Day 1 — The Ownership Transfer: Peace Begins When "Mine" Becomes "His"
GOD OWNS IT ALL — SO YOU DON'T HAVE TO CARRY IT ALL.
1 Chronicles 29:11–12 (NIV) — "Yours, Lord, is the greatness and the power and the glory and the majesty and the splendor, for everything in heaven and earth is yours. Yours, Lord, is the kingdom; you are exalted as head over all. Wealth and honor come from you; you are the ruler of all things."
Haggai 2:8 (NIV) — "'The silver is mine and the gold is mine,' declares the Lord Almighty."
What Nobody Told Him It Could Be
A young financial advisor connected to our work told me something that has stayed with me. When he was in college, studying finance alongside biblical literature, people pushed back. Not strangers, but people from church. He would explain what he was studying and the response was almost always some version of the same objection: "Doesn't the Bible say money is evil? Isn't it contradictory to think seriously about both?" He remembers being surprised that it kept coming from churchgoing Christians. People who believed Scripture but had quietly decided that the financial parts of life belonged to a different category, a secular one, operating under its own rules, separate from faith.
What he kept finding as he went deeper into his Bible courses was that the divide people assumed was not in Scripture at all. Jesus talked about money more than almost any other subject, not because accumulation is the goal of life, but because he understood what money does to the human heart when it isn't held correctly. The more this young man read, the more the wall between sacred life and financial life kept collapsing. He eventually found his way into a career doing exactly what everyone had told him was impossible: merging genuine faith with daily financial counsel, praying with clients, discipling people through their financial lives, helping them find clarity and peace at the same time. And what he keeps running into, working with people at every income level, is the same root problem regardless of the numbers. The divide people carry in their heads between money and God costs them more than any bad investment ever could.
That divide is the first thing this journey wants to address.
The Most Fundamental Decision a Christian Can Make
I have been saying for nearly fifty years that the most fundamental decision a Christian can make financially is to settle the question of ownership. Not budgeting. Not debt payoff. Not investing. Ownership. Because until that question is settled, every financial decision carries hidden weight, the weight of believing that you are ultimately responsible for outcomes that belong to God.
Scripture doesn't negotiate with you on this point. Haggai 2:8 doesn't hedge — the silver is mine and the gold is mine, declares the Lord Almighty. In 1 Chronicles 29, David stacks up word after word, greatness, power, glory, majesty, splendor, not to make you feel small but to paint a picture of an ownership so complete that nothing falls outside it. And in Matthew 25, in the parable of the talents, Jesus sets the scene with a single quiet phrase that reframes everything: a man going on a journey called his servants and entrusted his property to them. Not their property. His. They were managers of what belonged to someone else, and they would one day give an account of how they handled it.
When I came to understand this, really understood it rather than just assenting to it theologically, my own thinking shifted. I had spent years on Wall Street and building a CPA firm, operating in environments where money was simply assumed to be yours: you earned it, you managed it, you protected it, and more of it meant more security. Coming to faith changed the frame entirely. What I discovered was that the best principles I had encountered in professional finance all had roots in Scripture. The wall I had lived inside turned out to have been built on a false assumption.
Owners and Stewards Carry Different Things
The distinction matters practically because owners and stewards carry fundamentally different burdens. An owner has rights, and along with those rights comes responsibility for outcomes. A steward has responsibilities, but the owner retains the rights and, with those rights, ultimate accountability for what happens. When my oldest daughter reached driving age, she was eager to use my car. I gave her the keys. But there was never any question that I could take that car back at any time for any reason. She had responsibilities. I retained the rights. I literally possessed much but owned nothing that was truly mine, because everything I had came from somewhere outside me, entrusted for a season.
That is the position every one of us is actually in, whether we feel it or not. The title to your house reflects your right to possess it temporarily, not to own it forever. Only God owns it forever. If you walk around your property and think about how long that ground was there before you arrived and how long it will be there after you leave, the temporary nature of your claim on it becomes hard to ignore.
This matters because of what ownership-thinking produces in the soul. When you believe what you have is fundamentally yours, earned by you, secured by you, guaranteed by you, then every threat to financial stability feels like a threat to your identity. A surprise expense isn't just inconvenient; it is a crack in the foundation you are responsible for holding together. A job change isn't just a transition; it feels like a crisis, because you are the one who is supposed to have this under control. Underneath every budget conversation and every savings plan is a deeper question that almost everyone carries: will I be okay? And if everything is mine, the honest answer is that there is never quite enough to be secure, because security that depends on accumulation is always one unexpected event away from feeling threatened.
Stewardship changes the assignment. A steward is fully responsible for faithfulness, wisdom, and care. But the steward is not responsible for guaranteeing the future. That belongs to the owner. When you accept that you are a manager rather than an owner, the weight that has been quietly pressing on you has somewhere to go.
You Can't Fake It
There is one more thing about ownership worth saying plainly: you cannot fake stewardship. This is both the most uncomfortable truth and the most freeing one. Your checkbook reveals what you actually believe about whose money this is. Not what you say on Sunday morning, but how you actually live Monday through Saturday, what you spend on without thinking, what you protect most fiercely, what you avoid looking at because clarity feels uncomfortable. These are not just financial habits. They are ownership habits. They show where the real belief lives.
Very few Christians would argue with the principle that God owns it all. But following that principle to its natural conclusion has three revolutionary implications. First, God has the right to whatever he wants whenever he wants it, including, if he chooses, the resources you have been entrusted with. Second, every spending decision is a spiritual decision, because there is nothing more spiritual about giving to your church than buying a car or paying off debt or taking a vacation. These are all responsible uses of his resources. And third, because of the first two, you cannot compartmentalize. There is no secular financial life running on one track while your spiritual life runs on another. They are one track, and the question underneath every decision is the same: how do I faithfully manage what he has entrusted to me?
That question is what the next forty days are built around. Not how to optimize your portfolio or build the perfect budget, though there is practical work ahead. The question is who is actually holding the deed, and what that means for everything else.
The young advisor I mentioned carries this conviction into every client meeting. He starts in prayer and ends in prayer, because he has seen what happens when people genuinely transfer ownership, when they stop treating God's resources as theirs to secure and start treating them as a trust. Not necessarily more money, but a settled confidence that they are not carrying this alone. That settling, small as it may feel on the first morning of a forty-day journey, is where everything else begins.
Think About It
Ron writes in Master Your Money that the title to your home reflects your right to possess it temporarily, not forever, because only God owns it forever. What does it do to the way you think about your home, your savings, or your income to sit with that honestly for a moment?
The book identifies three implications of God owning it all: he has the right to whatever he wants whenever he wants it; every spending decision is a spiritual decision; and you cannot fake stewardship. Which of the three sits most uncomfortably with you right now, and why?
Exercise
Take ten minutes with a pen and paper. Write one sentence for each:
The area of my financial life where I am most clearly acting as an owner rather than a steward is: ___
The fear underneath that grip is: ___
If God truly holds the deed on this, my role as steward here might look like: ___
Then read all three sentences slowly, out loud if you can, as an honest prayer. You are not solving anything today. You are naming it, and naming it before God is where the transfer begins.
Declaration
Everything I have is Yours. I receive it as a gift, hold it as a trust, and release the outcome back to You.
Next Steps
Option A — Before any financial decision today, pause and ask honestly: am I acting as an owner or a steward right now? Let that single question slow you down just enough to answer from a different place.
Option B — Find five minutes of quiet and name the financial burden you have been carrying as if the outcome depended entirely on you. Say it out loud to God: "This is Yours. Show me my next faithful step."
Closing Prayer
Father, the earth is Yours, and everything in it, including the things I have been gripping as if they were mine to secure and protect and guarantee. Forgive me for the weight I have been carrying that was never mine to carry. Today I want to begin where this has to begin, with the simple honest acknowledgment that You are the owner and I am the steward. Not an owner with rights, but a manager with responsibilities, and the freedom that comes from trusting the one who holds the deed. Give me the grace to hold with open hands what You have entrusted to me, and teach me over these forty days to manage it in a way that honors You. In Jesus' name, amen.

DAY 4
Day 4 — Faithfulness First: God Measures What's In Your Heart, Not Your Hands
YOU DON'T HAVE TO HAVE IT ALL TOGETHER TO START. YOU JUST HAVE TO START.
Luke 16:10 (NIV) — "Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much."
Mark 12:43–44 (NIV) — "Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put more into the treasury than all the others. They all gave out of their wealth; but she, out of her poverty, put in everything — all she had to live on."
Small Changes and a Slow Joy
A woman who came to these principles through our work arrived at them later in life than most. She had spent years in the military as a single mother, moving from station to station, raising her daughter largely on her own, building the kind of independent toughness that comes from having no other choice. She had grown up in foster homes in Puerto Rico, learned early that no one was coming to rescue her, and carried that lesson into adulthood in ways she was still unlearning when she first read through this material.
What struck her, she said, was how the world had always told her that financial freedom meant accumulating enough to feel secure. She had been measuring herself against that standard for years, always slightly behind it, always feeling like she had to do more to close the gap. Then she read that God owns it all, and something shifted. Not dramatically, not overnight, but the way a room changes when someone opens a window. The question was no longer how much she had accumulated. The question was whether she was being faithful with what she had been given right now, in this season, at this income, with these circumstances.
She started making small changes. Not a complete financial overhaul, nothing that required a perfectly aligned household. Just small movements toward what she now understood to be true. And she described the result with a word I have heard many times over the decades when people take their first faithful steps: joy. Not the relief of having everything solved. Something quieter and steadier than that. The particular joy that comes from acting in alignment with what you believe, from handling what God has entrusted to you in a way that actually reflects who you say you trust.
Her husband was not fully on the same page yet. She was honest about that. But she said she believed that watching her make these changes was having an impact on him, and she was praying that they would eventually join forces and walk the path together. She was not waiting for perfect conditions. She was being faithful with where she was.
The Servant Who Did Nothing
In the parable of the talents, there are three servants. Two of them take what they have been given and put it to work. One of them, the servant with a single talent, buries it. When the master returns, this servant explains his reasoning: he was afraid. He knew the master expected results and he was not confident he could produce them, so he protected himself from failure by doing nothing at all.
The master's response is one of the sharpest things Jesus ever said about money. He does not rebuke the servant for producing too little. He rebukes him for producing nothing. "You wicked, lazy servant." To know what you ought to do and delay is, in the language of this parable, a kind of disobedience. It would suggest we have emotional faith or intellectual faith but not active faith. We know, but we wait. We agree, but we postpone.
I have spent nearly fifty years helping people work through this particular paralysis, and it almost always has the same root. People believe they need to be in a better situation before they start. They need more income first, less debt first, a more cooperative spouse first, a clearer plan first. They turn the starting line into a finish line and wonder why they never feel ready to begin. What the parable teaches is that the master does not evaluate the servants by comparing their results to each other. He evaluates them by asking what each one did with what each one was given. The servant with five talents and the servant with two talents receive identical praise: "Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things." The amount was different. The commendation was the same.
The amount is not important. What you do with it is.
Faithful With Little, Trusted With Much
Jesus points to the poor widow at the treasury and says she has given more than everyone else. The wealthy donors gave larger amounts, but they gave from surplus. She gave from scarcity, and what she gave cost her everything she had. Jesus is not making a point about giving strategies. He is making a point about the heart. The measure of faithfulness is not the size of the offering. It is the proportion of trust.
This is genuinely freeing if you let it be, because it means that wherever you are in your financial journey right now, the path forward is not waiting for better circumstances. The path forward is faithfulness with the circumstances you have. If you are carrying debt, faithfulness looks like one honest step toward a plan. If you are in a season of barely making it month to month, faithfulness looks like giving something, however small, from what you have. If your spouse is not yet on the same page, faithfulness looks like making the changes you can make without demanding that everything align before you move.
The woman I described did not transform her finances in a season. She made small changes and found that those small changes had an impact on her life that surprised her. That is almost always how it works. Not a dramatic reversal but a slow accumulation of faithful moments, each one small, each one building something the larger moments alone could never construct.
God works with people who take the next faithful step. Not the perfect step. Not the final step. Just the next one.
Think About It
The wicked servant buried his talent because he was afraid of failing. In your own financial life right now, is there an area where fear of getting it wrong has kept you from doing anything at all? What would one small faithful step look like in that area, even if the conditions are not yet ideal?
The woman in this story started making changes while her husband was not yet fully aligned with her. She did not wait for perfect agreement before taking any steps. Is there a financial decision or habit you have been postponing until someone else is ready or until conditions are better? What would it mean to be faithful right now, in the situation as it actually is?
Exercise
Write down one financial area where you have been waiting for better conditions before you start. It might be giving, saving, paying off debt, having a budget conversation, or simply looking honestly at where your money goes.
Then write one sentence: "The smallest faithful step I could take this week in this area, right now, with what I have, is ___."
Do not plan the whole journey. Just name the next step. Then take it before this week is over.
Declaration
I do not need perfect conditions to be faithful. I need only to take the next step with what I have been given, right now, in this season.
Next Steps
Option A — Today, take the one small step you wrote down in the exercise. Not the whole plan. Just the one step. Tell someone you trust that you did it.
Option B — If your financial situation involves a spouse or partner who is not yet fully on the same page, pray specifically for them today. Then make one change you can make regardless, and let it speak for itself.
Closing Prayer
Father, I confess that I have sometimes waited for better conditions before I was willing to be faithful with the conditions I have. I have looked at what I don't have rather than at what You have already entrusted to me. Today I want to hear the parable differently. Not as a warning to achieve more, but as an invitation to stop burying what You have given me out of fear. You do not measure me against what someone else was given. You measure me by what I did with what was in my hand. Show me the next faithful step. Give me the courage to take it today, even if conditions are not perfect, even if not everyone around me is ready, even if the amount feels too small to matter. You are the one who turns small faithfulness into large fruit. I trust You with that. In Jesus' name, amen.

DAY 5
Day 5 — Faith Requires Action: Obedience Is the First Financial Step
YOU WILL NEVER HAVE ENOUGH CERTAINTY TO START. FAITH IS THE DECISION TO ACT BEFORE YOU DO.
James 2:17 (NIV) — "In the same way, faith by itself, if it is not accompanied by action, is dead."
Hebrews 11:8 (NIV) — "By faith Abraham, when called to go to a place he would later receive as his inheritance, obeyed and went, even though he did not know where he was going."
She Tithed Before He Was Ready
A couple whose story was shared with me had been married a few years when the question of tithing became a real point of tension between them. The wife had grown up in a home where tithing was simply what you did. Her mother had modeled it through a difficult season, raising two children largely on her own after a divorce, living frugally on a single income, paying off the house in three years, and continuing to give generously through all of it. Decades later, that faithfulness had made possible the very lifestyle her daughter and son-in-law were now building. The wife had watched all of this growing up and it had settled something in her heart early: obedience to God with money comes before the conditions feel right.
Her husband did not share that settled conviction, at least not at the start. He had not grown up in church, had come to faith as an adult, and when he heard that giving ten percent of their income was what Christians were supposed to do, his analytical mind went to work on the math. They were carrying real debt at the time, underwater on a house, just starting their financial lives together. The money they would give, he reasoned, could go toward the mortgage, toward paying down what they owed, toward the principles that on paper made obvious sense. Why send it to a church when the numbers clearly pointed elsewhere? He was not resistant to God in some dramatic spiritual sense. He simply wanted to be rational about it. The numbers had to add up before he could move.
His wife prayed and waited. She did not pressure him or argue the theology at him. She believed what she believed and she trusted that God could do more in his heart than she could through force. And over time, the resistance softened. He began to give, tentatively at first, and then with more conviction. Within three months of making that decision, he received two raises at work. He tells this story himself now with a kind of wonder he cannot quite attribute to coincidence, because the same thing happened again years later after a stretch when they had been between churches and had let the giving lapse. The moment regular tithing resumed, a significant bonus arrived within a month. He acknowledges freely that correlation is not causation. But he also knows what he has seen, and what he has seen has made him far less interested in waiting for the numbers to line up before he obeys.
By the time they were in their early thirties, they had paid off all their debt, every student loan and car payment, and purchased their home in Indiana outright, with cash. They were thirty-two and thirty-four years old. The wife traces the thread directly back to her mother's faithfulness and to the early decision to tithe before the math felt certain.
The Problem with Waiting for Certainty
I have counseled thousands of people over nearly five decades, and the single most common thing that prevents people from taking their first faithful financial step is not ignorance of the principles. They know what to do. The problem is that they are waiting for conditions to improve before they act on what they already know. They will give when the debt is paid off. They will budget when the income is more stable. They will have the financial conversation with their spouse when the timing is better. They will start saving when there is a little more margin. They tell themselves this is responsible, but what it actually is, most of the time, is the substitution of circumstances for obedience.
The book of Hebrews gives us a long gallery of people who acted before the conditions were right. Noah built an ark without having seen rain. Abraham left his homeland for a destination he did not know. Sarah received the promise of a child when she was far too old to conceive. What is remarkable about every one of these stories is not the dramatic outcome. It is the first step. Noah did not wait until he understood flooding. Abraham did not wait until he could see the destination. They moved on the basis of what God had said, without the full picture, without adequate resources visible on paper, without certainty about how it would work out. And God showed them the second step after they had taken the first.
In Master Your Money, I write about faith goals having three consistent characteristics. First, the means of accomplishment may not be evident. You may not be able to see how it will happen. Second, a faith goal is often set with inadequate resources — the math does not obviously support the objective. And third, a faith goal requires action without full understanding. You do not need to see the whole staircase to take the first step. You need only to believe that the one calling you forward can see what you cannot.
The man whose story I described earlier was waiting for the numbers to make sense before he tithed. But giving, in the biblical framework, is not a decision you make after you have determined you can afford it. It is a declaration that you trust God more than you trust the math. The first step of giving is always an act of faith, not an act of calculation. That is precisely why it has the power to shift something in the soul that no financial strategy alone can reach.
Obedience Precedes Understanding
There is a scene in Mark 6 that I have returned to many times because it captures something essential about financial obedience. Jesus tells his disciples to have the crowd of five thousand sit down on the grass. There is no food at this point. The disciples do not have a plan. The instruction makes no logistical sense and they know it. But they obey anyway, and then Jesus takes what is available, gives thanks, and the miracle happens.
I draw four principles from that scene that apply directly to financial obedience. First, the world's advice and God's instruction will often point in different directions. The disciples' rational response was to send the crowd away to find their own food. Jesus said sit them down. The financially rational response when you are in debt is to redirect every available dollar toward payoff. God says honor him first with what you have. These are not always the same instruction, and choosing between them requires deciding whose counsel you trust. Second, obedience does not wait for the plan to make sense. The disciples obeyed before the food appeared, not after. Third, when God says to give, you give. When he says to pay down debt, you do it. When he says to create margin or stop spending beyond your means, the issue is not whether you feel ready. The issue is obedience. Fourth, the miracle follows obedience. Not the planning. Not the strategy. The willingness to take the step before you can see the outcome.
I want to say something here that may be uncomfortable: to delay financial obedience is to disobey. That sounds harsh, but I believe it is true. Many of us have intellectual faith about money, the kind that says, "Yes, God owns it all, yes, I should give, yes, I should get out of debt." But intellectual faith that produces no action is not the faith the Bible describes. James is blunt about this: faith without action is dead. The wicked servant in the parable of the talents knew what his master expected. He simply chose not to act on it. And what he knew but refused to do cost him everything.
The good news is that the first step does not have to be large. The husband in this story started tithing when the math was against it, from a position of real debt and genuine financial pressure. The wife's mother had tithed through a divorce and a single income and two kids and came out the other side with her house paid off and her retirement secured and her daughter's future made possible. Neither of them started from a position of surplus. They started from a position of trust, and they took one step without seeing the next one.
That is all that is required of you today. Not a complete financial overhaul. Not perfect clarity about the future. Just the willingness to take one obedient step, in the direction you already know you should go, before the conditions feel certain. God will show you the step after that when you need it.
Think About It
The husband in this story was waiting for the numbers to support obedience before he was willing to act. In what area of your financial life are you currently doing the same thing — waiting for better conditions before you take a step you already know you should take? What is the condition you are waiting for, and how long have you been waiting?
The wife in this story chose faithfulness before she and her husband were fully aligned, and she did it through patience and prayer rather than pressure. If you are in a household where financial values are not yet fully shared, what would it look like to be obedient with what you can control without forcing alignment on what you cannot?
Exercise
Hebrews 11 lists a series of people who acted before they could see the outcome. Take ten minutes and write a brief answer to this question: What is the financial step you have known for some time that you should take but have postponed, and what is the story you have told yourself about why the timing isn't right yet?
Then write one sentence that begins: "The first action I can take this week, before conditions are perfect, is ___."
The goal is not to plan the whole journey. It is to identify and name the first step, because first steps are what God works with.
Declaration
I will not wait for certainty before I obey. God has shown me the first step. I will take it today and trust him with the second.
Next Steps
Option A — If you have been postponing giving until your financial situation improves, give something today. Even a small amount. Let the act be the declaration that God is first before the conditions are right.
Option B — If the financial step you need to take involves your spouse or someone else in your household, choose today not to pressure but to pray. Then make the one change within your own control and let your faithfulness speak on your behalf.
Closing Prayer
Father, I confess that I have often treated obedience as something that comes after the conditions are right. I have told myself I was being responsible when really I was waiting for certainty You never promised me. Today I want to believe that You are the God of Noah, who acted before rain existed, and Abraham, who left for a place he could not see, and the disciples, who sat a crowd of thousands down before there was any food. You work with first steps, not finished plans. Show me the one step I have been delaying, give me the courage to take it today, and let me trust You with what comes next. You have never failed the people who stepped out before they could see the outcome. I am choosing to be one of them. In Jesus' name, amen.

DAY 12
Day 12 — Plan for Margin: Preparing for the Future Is a Faith Practice
THE ANT DOES NOT STORE PROVISIONS BECAUSE IT IS AFRAID OF WINTER. IT STORES BECAUSE IT UNDERSTANDS THAT SEASONS CHANGE AND WISDOM ACTS BEFORE THE NEED ARRIVES.
Proverbs 27:23–24 (NIV) — "Be sure you know the condition of your flocks, give careful attention to your herds; for riches do not endure forever, and a crown is not secure for all generations."
Proverbs 6:6–8 (NIV) — "Go to the ant, you sluggard; consider its ways and be wise! It has no commander, no overseer or ruler, yet it stores its provisions in summer and gathers its food at harvest."
What He Learned to Do Before the Crisis
A young coach in our financial discipleship program describes the role margin played in his own financial life with a clarity that comes from having learned it the hard way. He did not grow up in a household where planning and preparation were modeled. He came to these principles as an adult, through the kind of slow awakening that happens when the absence of margin makes itself felt — through the recurring anxiety of a financial life that had no buffer between its income and its obligations, no reserve against the unexpected, no room to breathe when something normal went wrong.
What changed first was not his bank account. It was his understanding of what margin was actually for. He had thought of savings as a defensive measure — protection against worst-case scenarios, a hedge against fear. What he came to understand was that margin is not primarily about fear management. It is about availability. The prepared household can respond when God calls. It can help when a need presents itself. It can say yes to an opportunity without first calculating whether the answer will cause a financial crisis. The margin is not a wall built against the outside world. It is the room that makes obedience possible.
He describes it in terms of hands — open versus closed. When your hands are open you can both give and receive. When they are clenched around what you have, you can do neither. Margin is the financial expression of open hands: not everything consumed, something held in reserve, available to be deployed when the moment comes. That is not a defensive posture. It is a generous one.
The Ant and the Wisdom It Does Not Announce
Proverbs 6 directs the sluggard to the ant — one of the most pointed teaching illustrations in the wisdom literature. The ant has no commander. No overseer. No external system enforcing the behavior. And yet it stores provisions in summer and gathers food at harvest. Not because winter is threatening it right now. Because winter is coming, it is not here yet, and the ant understands the rhythm of seasons well enough to act before the need arrives.
This is the wisdom at the heart of planning for margin. It is not reactive. The reactive version waits for the crisis and then improvises. The wise version sees that seasons change — income will fluctuate, expenses will arrive unexpectedly, life will not maintain its current comfortable equilibrium indefinitely — and acts before those realities materialize. The three-to-six month emergency fund that feels unnecessary when nothing has gone wrong is what keeps a job change from becoming a catastrophe and a medical bill from becoming a debt spiral. It is what the ant knows that the sluggard does not: you do not wait for winter to decide you should have stored something in summer.
This is not anxiety dressed up in wisdom language. Anxiety says: I will never have enough, something will always go wrong, the future is threatening and I must protect against it constantly. Wisdom says: I know that seasons change, I understand the financial rhythms of a human life, and I will prepare appropriately before the need arrives rather than scrambling after it has. The ant is not trembling. It is simply doing, in summer, what summer is for.
Know the Condition of Your Flocks
Proverbs 27:23 is one of the more direct financial instructions in all of Scripture, and its agricultural frame should not obscure how practical it is. Be sure you know the condition of your flocks. Give careful attention to your herds. For riches do not endure forever.
In the ancient world, your flock was your wealth. Knowing its condition — how many animals you had, which were healthy, which were vulnerable, whether the herd was growing or shrinking — was not optional for anyone who wanted to manage faithfully. An inattentive shepherd did not know that a portion of his flock had gone missing until the losses had become too large to recover. Careful attention, maintained consistently, was the difference between a flock that remained productive and one that quietly declined while its owner assumed everything was fine.
The application to modern financial life is direct. Most people have an approximate awareness of their financial position. They know roughly what they earn and roughly what they spend. They do not know the condition of their flocks — the exact state of their accounts, the precise flow of cash through their household, the specific margin between what comes in and what goes out. They assume things are fine until a disruption arrives and the absence of careful attention becomes suddenly expensive.
Margin begins with this kind of attention. Not obsessive monitoring, but the regular, honest practice of knowing where things actually stand. What are the accounts? What is the cash flow? Where is the buffer? How many months of living expenses could the household sustain if the income stopped tomorrow? These are not fearful questions. They are the questions a faithful steward asks the same way a shepherd checks the flock — not because disaster is imminent, but because care requires current awareness, and current awareness makes care possible.
Prepare Because You Are Faithful, Not Because You Are Scared
I want to draw a distinction that I believe matters practically for how the margin-building habit is formed and maintained over time. The difference between saving because you are scared and preparing because you are faithful is not primarily a difference in behavior — both produce a savings account. It is a difference in the orientation of the heart behind the behavior.
The person saving from fear is doing so because the future feels threatening. The larger the reserve, the more temporary the relief — because the fear that drives the accumulation is not satisfied by numbers. There will always be a scenario that the current amount cannot handle, and the anxiety recalibrates upward to chase the number that would finally feel like enough. This person is building margin, but the margin is in service of a security that financial resources alone cannot provide.
The person preparing from faithfulness is doing so because stewardship requires it. The question driving the behavior is not: how much do I need to feel safe? The question is: how do I prepare faithfully and stay available to God? Margin is the answer to that question. A margin of three to six months of living expenses is not a number that guarantees safety — no number does. It is the preparation that keeps a normal disruption from becoming a crisis, and that therefore keeps obedience possible when God calls.
Planning and preparation are not enemies of faith. When the heart is right, they are expressions of it. You are acknowledging reality without surrendering to anxiety. You are being the ant — acting in summer with the awareness that harvest seasons are followed by winter seasons, and that wisdom prepares accordingly.
Where to Start
The margin-building process in Master Your Money follows a simple sequence that I have taught for decades and found consistently effective for households at every income level.
Step one is the elimination of consumer debt — credit cards, personal loans, and any high-interest obligations. Paying off a credit card with an eighteen to twenty-two percent interest rate is not a conservative financial decision. It is the highest guaranteed return available to almost any investor. Until consumer debt is eliminated, every other margin-building effort is competing against an ongoing guaranteed loss.
Step two is the establishment of an emergency fund — three to six months of living expenses in a liquid, interest-bearing account. This is the foundational reserve that converts a normal disruption into an inconvenience rather than a crisis. If this account is tapped for an emergency, the first priority is to replenish it. This account is not an investment. It is a buffer — the financial equivalent of the ant's summer stores — and its purpose is not to grow but to be there when seasons change.
These two steps, done in sequence rather than simultaneously, provide the foundation on which all further financial planning becomes possible. Until they are in place, every other financial goal is structurally vulnerable to disruption. When they are in place, the household can make decisions from a position of preparation rather than panic.

Day 24 — Cash Flow Clarity: Money Leaks Kill Momentum
IF THE NET WORTH STATEMENT IS A SNAPSHOT OF YOUR FINANCIAL HEALTH, THE CASH FLOW SUMMARY IS THE FILM — AND THE FILM SHOWS WHETHER THINGS ARE GETTING BETTER OR WORSE.
Proverbs 27:23–24 (NIV) — "Be sure you know the condition of your flocks, give careful attention to your herds; for riches do not endure forever, and a crown is not secure for all generations."
Ephesians 5:15–16 (NIV) — "Be very careful, then, how you live — not as unwise but as wise, making the most of every opportunity, because the days are evil."
The Leak They Did Not Know Was There
The composite couple I introduced in Day 23 — twenty years of marriage, two children, a home, and a net worth of fifty-five thousand dollars — came back for the second part of their financial analysis with a problem that would have been invisible to them without the work we had started together. After projecting their income and walking through each category of outflow, the picture assembled itself. Their gross income was just over seventy-nine thousand dollars a year. After giving, taxes, and debt payments, their net spendable income was forty-six thousand dollars. Their total annual living expenses — housing, food, clothing, transportation, entertainment, medical, insurance, children's expenses, gifts, and miscellaneous — came to fifty-six thousand dollars.
Their cash flow margin was negative nine thousand eight hundred and fifty dollars.
That number meant they were spending nearly ten thousand dollars more each year than remained after their obligations were met. Not intentionally. Not dramatically. It had crept up month by month — a little more here, a little more there — until the gap between what was coming in and what was going out had opened wide enough to require one of four responses: increase their income, reduce their expenses, dip into savings, or borrow. They were, in fact, doing the last two, and in doing so, were compounding the problem without knowing its source.
They almost certainly did not know any of this before the analysis. That is not unusual. In my experience, the majority of households running a negative cash flow have no clear picture of it. They feel the pressure — the tightness at the end of the month, the credit card balance that never quite clears, the savings that never quite builds — but they cannot name the number or the category where the money is going. The cash flow summary names it. And named problems can be solved.
The Snapshot and the Film
In Master Your Money, I describe the difference between the net worth statement and the cash flow summary with a simple image. If the net worth statement is a snapshot — a photograph of everything you own and owe at a specific moment — then the cash flow summary is a film. It captures not a point in time but a period of time: what came in, what went out, and whether what went out exceeded what came in.
These two tools together give you the full picture. The net worth statement shows where you stand. The cash flow summary shows whether you are moving forward or backward. A household with a positive net worth that is running a negative cash flow is quietly consuming its own position — assets being depleted, debt being accumulated, the net worth number that looked solid on Day 23 slowly eroding in the film that Day 24 reveals.
Proverbs 27:23 does not merely say to know what you have — it says to give careful attention to your herds. The word is deliberate. Knowing in general is not enough. Careful attention means knowing which animals are producing, which are consuming resources, and whether the flock is growing or shrinking. The same attentiveness applied to a household's cash flow is what Paul describes in Ephesians 5 as living wisely and making the most of every opportunity. Every dollar that leaks from a household without intention is an opportunity that was present and passed unnoticed. The careful steward tracks where the money actually goes.
The Cookie Jar and What It Knew
Many people tell me stories about the cookie jar where grandparents used to keep their cash. As income came in, it went into the jar. As needs arose, money came out. When the jar was empty, spending stopped until more came in. Simple. Visible. Effective.
When expenses grew more complex, the cookie jar gave way to the envelope system — one envelope for food, one for clothing, one for giving, one for insurance. The same principle applied. The income was allocated, placed into envelopes for specific purposes, and when an envelope was empty, spending in that category stopped until the next period's allocation arrived.
I return to this image regularly because it captures three principles that no modern budgeting technology has actually improved on. First, every dollar was allocated. There was no pool of unassigned money that could quietly absorb whatever came along. Every dollar had a destination before it was spent. Second, spending stopped when the allocation was exhausted. Not because of willpower or discipline in the moment, but because the system made the limit visible and concrete. Third, there was always a current awareness of where things stood. You could look at the envelope and know exactly how much remained and whether you were on track.
These three principles — allocation, limits, and awareness — are what any effective cash flow system must produce, whether the tool is envelopes or a spreadsheet or a financial planning app. The technology has changed. The principles have not. And the households that maintain awareness of their cash flow in real time are the ones that stop the leaks before they become floods.
The Priority Order That Changes Everything
The most important structural insight in the cash flow analysis is one that seems simple but reverses the way most households actually operate. The net spendable income concept establishes that the first three uses of incoming money are giving, taxes, and debt repayment — in that order. What remains after those three obligations is the net spendable income, and living expenses are funded from that remainder.
Most households do exactly the opposite. Living expenses come first, absorbing the income as it arrives. Giving, debt payments, and savings get whatever remains at the end of the month — which in a household without careful cash flow tracking is often very little. The giving gets reduced. The debt payment gets made because there is no choice. The savings never accumulates. And the household wonders why financial faithfulness never gains traction.
The cash flow summary makes this priority disorder visible. When you lay out all six elements of the cash flow — income, giving, taxes, debt, living expenses, and savings — and you assign them in the order they actually receive your income, the priority structure becomes concrete and correctable. Giving moves from last to first. Living expenses stop being the first call on every paycheck and become what they are meant to be: the fourth priority, managed within what the first three have left.
For the couple in this illustration, the path from a negative nine-thousand-dollar cash flow to a positive four-hundred-dollar cash flow did not require a dramatic change in income. It required a set of specific decisions across their living expense categories — a hundred dollars less on housing, twenty-five less on food, fifty less on transportation, ninety less on children's expenses. Added together with the restructuring of their debt, it produced the margin they needed. The problem was not unsolvable. It was simply unnamed — invisible until the summary made it concrete.
Think About It
The couple did not know they were running a nearly ten-thousand-dollar annual deficit. They felt the pressure but could not name its source. In your own financial life, is there a gap between what you think you are spending and what you are actually spending? What would it reveal if you tracked every dollar of outflow for the next thirty days?
Ephesians 5:16 calls the steward to make the most of every opportunity. A dollar that leaks without intention is an opportunity that passed unnoticed. Which category of your monthly spending do you suspect is the most significant source of unintentional leaking — the place where money exits without clear purpose? What would it cost you in annual terms if that pattern continued for ten more years?
Exercise
Prepare your own cash flow summary in five steps.
Start with your gross annual income from all sources.
Subtract your giving — what you actually give, not what you intend to give.
Subtract your taxes — federal, state, Social Security.
Subtract your debt payments — every monthly payment except the mortgage, which belongs under housing in living expenses.
The number that remains is your net spendable income. That is what your lifestyle must be funded from.
Now estimate your annual living expenses across the major categories: housing, food, clothing, transportation, entertainment and recreation, medical, insurance, children, gifts, and miscellaneous. Use actuals where you have them and honest estimates where you do not.
Subtract your total living expenses from your net spendable income. The resulting number is your cash flow margin. If it is positive, you have room to work with. If it is negative, you have just named the problem that has been creating pressure without a clear source. Either result is useful. Both are more useful than not knowing.
Declaration
I will know where my money goes. Every dollar has a purpose, and I will give it one — on purpose, before it finds one by accident.
Next Steps
Option A — For the next thirty days, record every dollar that leaves your household. Not to judge yourself but to see clearly. At the end of thirty days, compare what you assumed you were spending to what the record shows. The gap between those two numbers, in any category, is the first place to look for margin.
Option B — If you already track your spending, review the last three months and identify the single category where actual spending most consistently exceeds your intended amount. Name it specifically. Then decide whether that category reflects your stated priorities. If it does not, decide what a more intentional allocation would look like — and set it before next month begins.
Closing Prayer

Day 33 — Your Investments Advance a Cause: Faith-Based Investing Starts With Awareness

The Question He Had Never Thought to Ask

What You Root For
Robin John, in his book The Good Investor, makes an observation that I have found both simple and difficult to shake. When you invest in something, you begin to want it to succeed. The investment creates an emotional stake. You check the performance. You hope the quarter goes well. You root for the company the way a sports fan roots for a team. The investment is not emotionally neutral — it never was — and the question is whether what you are rooting for is something you would be willing to put your name on in daylight.
Jesus said in Matthew 6:21 that where your treasure is, there your heart will be also. This verse is usually applied to giving, and rightly so. But it applies with equal precision to investing. Where you place your investment capital, your heart follows. You become, over time, a stakeholder in whatever your portfolio contains — not just financially but emotionally and spiritually. You develop a rooting interest in the companies and funds you own. And the companies and funds you own are doing things in the world, funding things, advancing things, sometimes things that align with your values and sometimes things that directly contradict them.
Most Christians who have never examined their portfolios through this lens are not investing in harmful things on purpose. They are investing without awareness, which is a different and more correctable problem. The issue is not intention. It is the absence of a question that most people have simply never thought to ask: what does my investment portfolio actually fund, and is that something I want to advance?
Everything in the Lord's Name
Colossians 3:17 is one of the most sweeping instructions in the New Testament. Whatever you do — in word or deed — do it all in the name of the Lord Jesus. The word "whatever" is not qualifying or limiting. It is comprehensive. It covers the obvious categories: how you speak, how you treat people, how you work, how you give. And it covers the less-examined ones: how you spend, how you save, and how you invest.
This does not make investing a religious exercise in a narrow sense. It makes it a stewardship exercise in the fullest sense — bringing the same intentionality to portfolio decisions that a Christian brings, at best, to giving decisions. The question is not whether faith belongs in investment conversations. Faith belongs in all conversations. The question is whether we have been bringing it.
Psalm 24:1 establishes the foundation that makes Colossians 3:17 make sense. The earth is the Lord's, and everything in it. If that is true — if the ownership question settled in Day 1 is genuinely settled — then there is no corner of the financial life, including the investment portfolio, where it does not apply. The steward who manages an employer's assets does not get to say that a portion of those assets falls outside the employer's interest. Everything is in trust. Everything is subject to the owner's values. That includes the portion of God's resources currently allocated to equity markets.
From Ten Percent to a Hundred

It is a statement about coherence. The Christian who gives generously from one account and invests without awareness from another has divided the financial life into sacred and secular sections, which is exactly the wall that this devotional has been working to collapse since Day 1.

When the heart starts there, he says, investing becomes an act of trust. It is no longer about timing the market or chasing returns. It is about multiplying what the Master has entrusted, for his glory rather than for its own sake.
That shift does not make investment analysis irrelevant. Returns still matter. Risk still matters. The sequential investment strategy from Day 32 still applies. What changes is the framework inside which those questions are asked. The portfolio is not a separate domain operating under different rules than the rest of the financial life. It is an extension of the same stewardship, subject to the same owner, managed in the name of the same Lord.
Starting With Awareness
The subtitle of today's devotional is faith-based investing starts with awareness, and I want to be precise about what that means practically. I am not suggesting that everyone restructure their portfolios immediately or that there is one correct way to invest as a Christian. I am suggesting that most people have never asked the question, and that asking it is the first step.
What does your portfolio currently fund? Not in broad market terms, but specifically — the industries, the companies, the causes that benefit from your investment dollars. You may find that the answer is entirely consistent with your values. You may find gaps you did not know were there. You may find that you are actively funding things you would never fund deliberately. Any of these discoveries is more useful than the comfortable vagueness of not looking.
The move from not looking to looking does not require becoming an expert in ESG frameworks or faith-driven investment vehicles. It requires the same thing that every other part of this devotional has required: honesty, awareness, and the willingness to bring your financial life into alignment with what you actually believe. The question is not whether your investments can advance a cause. They already are. The question is which cause, and whether you know.

Day 41 — The Family Conversation: Money and Faith Belong in the Same Talk

The Table or the Coffin
I ask this question in Master Your Money because I have found nothing sharper for cutting through the procrastination that surrounds family financial conversations: is it easier to train your children in financial principles around the coffee table or from your coffin? The answer is obvious. The behavior is not. Most parents wait until it is too late, passing on assets they managed faithfully without ever transferring the convictions that made the management faithful.
There are two purposes for bringing the family together around the topic of money, and both are essential. The first is communication — the honest, timely, age-appropriate sharing of the financial philosophy, beliefs, and values that are shaping how the household operates. Not necessarily the specific numbers, though sometimes that is appropriate. The convictions behind the numbers: what you believe about ownership, about generosity, about spending and saving, about what money is actually for. These things should not be a mystery to the people who will one day inherit what you built.
The second purpose is training. Children who watch their parents plan are more likely to plan. Children who see their parents give first and live below their means are more likely to do the same. The financial habits of the next generation are not formed primarily in classrooms or through books. They are formed at dinner tables, in conversations about why a purchase was made or not made, in the ordinary rhythm of a household where money is handled in a particular way for reasons that are named out loud.
Money can cause tremendous conflict within families without anyone being aware that money is the underlying issue. The tension over inheritance. The unspoken resentments about who received what. The children who never understood why the estate plan was structured the way it was and feel, after their parents are gone, that the choices communicated something about how they were valued. None of these fractures have to happen. Most of them are the product of conversations that were deferred until they could no longer be had.

That is the first lesson, and it is delivered not by lecture but by repeated practice.
They talk about what the Bible says. They talk about their own finances — not in full detail, but with enough honesty that the children understand that financial decisions are being made consciously and for reasons. Yes, we could buy that. We are choosing something different. Here is why. This is the kind of training Deuteronomy 6 is describing. When you sit at home. When you walk along the road. When you lie down and get up. Not a formal curriculum with a syllabus, but a posture of ongoing conversation — the financial principles woven into the daily texture of family life so that children absorb them the way children absorb everything formative: through repetition, through watching, through having the reasons explained in language they can hold onto.

Does it create the conditions for faith, faithfulness, and generosity — or does it create the conditions for complacency, entitlement, and drift? The answer should shape the plan.
This is what Psalm 78 is after. The psalmist is not describing a transfer of wealth from one generation to the next. The financial piece is real and should be handled well. Day 40 addressed that directly. But what finances alone cannot transfer is the why — the convictions, the faith, the framework for understanding why any of this matters and what it is ultimately for. That transfer happens in conversation. It happens at the dinner table, in the car, in the moments when a child asks why and the parent gives the honest theological answer rather than the convenient one. It happens when a parent who has stumbled, as the man in this story did, is willing to sit down and tell the real story rather than the polished one.

Plan one family financial conversation this week. Not a budget review or an estate planning session — a values conversation. Choose one of the following as the starting point and bring it to the dinner table, to a car ride, or to any setting where it can be discussed honestly.
Option one — Tell your financial story. Where did you start? What did you get wrong? What has changed and why? Let the people closest to you hear the real version, not the cleaned-up one.
Option two — Explain the why behind one of your household's current financial practices. Why do you give what you give, and where does it go? Why did you make the housing decision you made? Why does the spending plan look the way it does? Name the conviction behind the practice.
Option three — Ask a question and listen. What do your children or your spouse believe about money? Where did those beliefs come from? Are they the beliefs you would hope they hold? The conversation starts with curiosity as much as teaching.
Declaration
I will not let money remain a subject the people I love have to figure out without me. I will talk about it — honestly, faithfully, and in a way that points toward God as the owner of it all.
Next Steps
Option A — If you have children at home, start the 10/10/10 conversation this week. Not necessarily the exact percentages — the principle. Before your children spend any of their money, introduce the question: who does this belong to? What should happen to it first? The practice forms the belief over time.
Option B — If you have adult children or grandchildren with whom you have never had a direct conversation about your financial values, your giving, your estate intentions, or what you believe about money and faith — consider writing them a letter this week. Not a formal document. A personal one. What do you want them to know? What do you hope they carry forward? The letter does not have to be long. It has to be honest.
Closing Prayer
Father, forgive us for the conversations we have deferred — the ones where faith and money were supposed to meet at the dinner table and somehow never did. We have often managed our finances with conviction in private and silence in community, never naming for the people we love most what we actually believe about why money matters, where it comes from, and what it is for. Give us the courage to talk about it — with our children, our spouses, the people who will one day manage what we have been managing. Let the praiseworthy deeds you have done in our financial lives not be hidden from our descendants. Let them know the story. Let it be enough to point them toward you. In Jesus' name, amen.
