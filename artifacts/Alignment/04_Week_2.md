# Day 6
## How Everything Works Together as a System
### From a collection of parts to a coordinated whole

*Watch the Day 6 video before you read.*

**"Just as a body, though one, has many parts, but all its many parts form one body, so it is with Christ."**
1 Corinthians 12:12

When you think about assets under management, what are you really helping a family understand?

You're helping them to understand how everything works together as a system.

Most families have assets spread among different places, sometimes with different advisors managing their money, different strategies, different structures. So the goal is to move from a collection of parts to a coordinated whole.

I want to sit on the phrase collection of parts, because it describes almost every substantial family I have ever met and none of them would use it about themselves.

They did not assemble a collection on purpose. A position got taken in one decade. An entity got formed because a transaction required it. An advisor got added because somebody made an introduction at the right moment. Every one of those decisions was defensible when it was made, by a competent person, for a real reason. Twenty years later the family owns a collection rather than a plan, and there was never a day when the collection was chosen.

That is what makes it hard to see. Nothing went wrong. Things simply accumulated.

Once you bring it all together, people begin to see differently. You can align strategies with goals, simplify where needed, and clarify how each piece fits into the overall plan.

And that changes how decisions are made going forward. Instead of reacting to individual opportunities, you begin evaluating everything in the context of the whole.

That sentence deserves more attention than it usually gets, because it describes a change in the family's operating mode rather than a change in their portfolio.

An investor who evaluates opportunities one at a time is permanently reactive. Each opportunity arrives on its own merits. Each one can be argued for. There is no principled basis for declining anything that looks good in isolation, which means the answer to a well-presented idea is almost always yes, and the collection grows.

An investor evaluating against a whole has a standard. A standard is the only thing that lets a person decline something good because it does not fit.

Integration is the key. You're helping the family understand how assets, how strategies, and structures relate to each other and to their overall purpose, because without that, it's very easy to lose direction.

Most families don't naturally step back to do this.

The reason is not laziness, and it is worth being fair about. They are managing pieces, making decisions, and responding to what's in front of them, and they're not always seeing the system. That is a full job. Stepping back requires somebody to stop responding for long enough to look, and there is never a convenient week for that.

Your advantage is that you can schedule it. A family cannot easily give themselves permission to spend an afternoon asking how their holdings relate to each other. They will give you that afternoon if you ask for it, because coming from you it is a meeting rather than an indulgence.

Paul is describing a body with many parts, and the argument he is making is that the parts are not in competition and are not interchangeable. Each one is doing something the others cannot, and the whole thing only functions because they are coordinated rather than merely adjacent.

Adjacent is what most portfolios are. Several good parts, in the same place, doing unrelated things, with nothing connecting them but ownership.

Where does the family want to go, and what do we need to do to align it with where they want to go?

That is the question the system has to answer. Not what did it return. What is it for.

# Day 7
## Why Is It There?
### Each piece could have a purpose within the larger picture

*Watch the Day 7 video before you read.*

**"Steady plodding brings prosperity; hasty speculation brings poverty."**
Proverbs 21:5 (TLB)

You start by mapping it out. What assets exist? How are they being managed, and what role is each one playing?

Then you ask, does this make sense together? And does it align with what the family is trying to accomplish?

You're not just reviewing performance, because quite honestly, you're not reviewing performance. You're reviewing the asset itself, and you're evaluating it from an alignment standpoint, not from how much it's growing in value.

That is a bigger departure than it sounds like, and it is worth stating plainly to a client so they understand what kind of meeting they are in.

Performance is easy to focus on and frankly, it's measurable. That is exactly why it crowds out everything else. Your reporting produces it automatically. Your quarterly meetings are already built around it. A client can look at a performance figure and feel that a review has occurred, and in a limited sense one has.

Alignment requires stepping back and looking at how everything connects, how the pieces of the puzzle come together. There is no report that generates it. Somebody has to sit down and ask, of each holding, what job this is doing for this family.

Each piece could have a purpose within the larger picture. If it doesn't, then you have to ask, why is it there?

I like that question because it cannot be answered with a number, and almost every other question in this business can be.

Sometimes the answer is good. It is generating income the family needs. It is holding a position for a reason that still applies. It is hedging something real. Fine. Move on.

Sometimes there is no answer at all. The holding is there because it was there last year. Somebody bought it in 2011 for a reason that expired in 2015, nobody has revisited it, and it has been quietly performing acceptably ever since.

That is the best camouflage available. An asset that performs poorly gets noticed and gets questions. An asset that performs acceptably and serves no purpose can sit undisturbed for a decade, because there is nothing about it that triggers a review.

So the question has to be asked deliberately, of every holding, whether or not anything looks wrong. That is the only way this category of problem ever surfaces.

Now, what it feels like to do.

It is tedious. You are going down a list of forty positions asking the same question forty times, and thirty-five of the answers are fine. There is no way to make it sound impressive at a conference and there is no way to shorten it.

Solomon's word is plodding, which nobody would put in a brochure. Steady, unglamorous, repetitive work produces the result. Speculation, which is far more interesting to do and much more interesting to talk about, produces the other one.

Asking why is it there of forty holdings is plodding.

It is also the difference between managing a family's assets and stewarding a family's plan, and the family can feel the difference even when they cannot name it. They have had performance reviews before. They have not had anybody ask what a holding is for.

Ask it of one holding today.

# Day 8
## There's Fragmentation Going On
### Decisions are being made in isolation

*Watch the Day 8 video before you read.*

**"Do two walk together unless they have agreed to do so?"**
Amos 3:3

You have got a family with multiple advisors. One is managing investments, one is handling insurance, another one is working on estate planning. Each one is doing their job well. They know it. They're in each of their lanes. Everybody's being paid, everybody's having their meetings, and performance looks fine.

Nothing feels broken, but no one has stepped back to look at how all these strategies fit together.

So over time, you start to see overlap in some of these areas and gaps in others, and people start speaking in lanes that they really aren't in charge of. The family feels like something isn't quite working, but they can't clearly explain why.

What's really going on?

Well, there's fragmentation going on. Each piece is being managed independently, but there's no coordination across the whole, so decisions are being made in isolation instead of in alignment.

Fragmentation is not visible from inside a lane. That is the whole difficulty. Every professional at that table is doing competent work and none of them is positioned to see the problem, because the problem exists in the space between them and nobody was hired to occupy that space.

Here is the most expensive version I encounter, and it happens repeatedly.

They might buy an insurance policy and think this is just what the family needs to pay estate taxes. Well, they should have put that insurance outside of the estate so that it's not counted in the value of the estate.

Follow what happened there. The family bought a policy specifically to fund an estate tax liability. It was placed inside the estate. So the death benefit arrives, it increases the size of the estate, and it inflates the very liability it was purchased to solve.

The insurance professional did good work. The estate attorney did good work. Neither one was in the room when the other was working, and there was no mechanism anywhere in that family's life for the two decisions to meet.

And over time, this creates inefficiency and confusion.

Confusion is the part that wears on people, and it is why families eventually go looking even though they cannot articulate what is wrong. Nothing has failed. Accounts are performing. Advisors are attentive. And underneath it the family senses friction they cannot locate, so they do not raise it, and because they do not raise it nobody looks.

That is how a condition persists for a decade inside a group of competent professionals who would fix it in an afternoon if anyone described it out loud.

A lot of times we can save millions of dollars just by putting things in the right places in order to get where they want to go.

Now, the instruction that keeps you from wrecking the relationship.

The solution is not to replace what's there, but it's to bring it together and to put it in the right situation. To map it out, understand how it fits, and begin aligning those pieces so they function as a system.

Do not replace people. That instinct is common and it is expensive, because you will spend your credibility fighting incumbents rather than fixing outcomes, and the family will experience the whole thing as a turf fight rather than as help. Most of the value here comes from relocating things, not from changing who manages them.

Amos asks whether two people walk together without having agreed to. The question is rhetorical and the answer is obvious, and the reason it belongs here is that four advisors serving one family are walking in the same direction without ever having agreed on anything, because they have never spoken.

They are not in conflict. They are simply uncoordinated, which produces a similar result more slowly.

Get the client's permission to talk to the others. That is the whole first move.

# Day 9
## He Doesn't Care About the Business
### But we care about the business

*Watch the Day 9 video before you read.*

**"So in everything, do to others what you would have them do to you."**
Matthew 7:12

A big question with families is often, is that in your estate or is that outside of your estate? You want stuff outside of your estate, because they tax all the stuff that's in the estate.

But also, the average advisor, all he cares about is the money under management that he manages. He forgets that this guy owns a company. He doesn't care about the business, because he doesn't get anything off of that.

But we care about the business, because it's part of the big picture of what the family has, and we're just trying to help them steward it well.

I want to be fair to the advisor in that description, because I am not accusing anybody of negligence.

He is doing exactly what his compensation trained him to do. He has a scope, the scope is defined by what he is paid on, and everything outside it is genuinely invisible to him. Not ignored. Invisible. It does not appear in his reporting, it does not come up in his review meetings, and no part of his process would ever surface it.

That is the most dangerous kind of blind spot, because the man cannot detect it by trying harder or caring more.

The only cure is a decision, made once, in advance. I look at the whole balance sheet regardless of what I am compensated on. That decision costs you hours and it will occasionally produce a finding that leads to work you do not perform and are not paid for.

Take it anyway. And here is the second reason, which sits alongside the first rather than replacing it.

There's so many different ways for advisors to come into this, whether it's fractional, or part-time, or the few of their clients that are some of the larger ones that have businesses that have some complexities, of which they'd see this as a very valuable service in addition to whatever they're doing under management.

It's a huge opportunity for the advisor to get to know the family better, to build relationships multi-generationally in the family, and to help the family to do everything they want to do.

Notice that you are not being asked to convert your practice. This works fractionally. It works with the handful of clients in your book who own operating businesses and carry genuine complexity, which is probably fewer than a dozen relationships and almost certainly your largest ones.

Those are also the relationships where a competitor with a wider view can take everything.

The Golden Rule is usually read as a general instruction about kindness. It is more precise than that. It says to do what you would want done, which requires you to first put yourself in the position of the person across the table and imagine wanting something.

So imagine it. You own a business worth fifteen million dollars, which is most of what you have. You are paying a competent professional to advise you on the rest. Would you want him to have noticed that the largest thing you own creates a tax problem for your family?

Of course you would. And you would be astonished, and probably not forgiving, to learn that he never once looked at it because he was not paid to.

That is the whole argument, and it does not require any change to how you are compensated. It requires you to look at the parts of a family's life that your fee schedule does not cover, and then to say something about what you find.

Go look at what you are not paid on.

# Day 10
## The Advisor Is Either Not a Part of It or He Is a Part of It
### When a person dies, there's a whole lot of work that needs to be done

*Watch the Day 10 video before you read.*

**"A good person leaves an inheritance for their children's children."**
Proverbs 13:22

When a person dies, there's a whole lot of work that needs to be done. And the advisor is either not a part of it or he is a part of it.

And if he's there, supporting the wife, in this case, then she's probably going to hang around with him and be there with him. And then the kids, if they're being brought into the situation as well, they're probably going to keep the assets there as well.

It's a win-win situation.

I am going to make the commercial case once, here, plainly, and then this book will not raise it again.

You are practicing through the largest generational wealth transfer in history. When one passes, usually the patriarch, the matriarch gets resources, and many times they shift advisors because there's not a relationship there. Same thing is true with the children.

That is the mechanism. Not a service failure, not a competitor with a better product. Simply the absence of a relationship with the person who ends up holding the assets.

The surviving spouse frequently has never had her own conversation with you. She came to some meetings. She was in the room. She was not the client, and both of you understood that, and neither of you thought it mattered because there was time. Then there is no time, and she is making a decision about who to trust while she is grieving, and you are one of several people her husband used to talk to.

The children are worse, because usually they have never met you at all. Their first exposure to you is at a moment of maximum emotion and minimum trust.

But this Family Legacy Coaching gives you the opportunity to be there, be in there, and to have those relationships cultivated. And now you have a sustainable and scalable business practice, especially with those with businesses and things like that.

Notice that the fix is not a retention strategy. It is doing the work in a way that naturally puts you in the room with the people who inherit, years before they inherit, for reasons that have nothing to do with keeping the account.

That is the honest version of win-win. Not that you get something out of helping people, though you do. That the thing which serves the family best and the thing which sustains your practice happen to be the same activity, and that is worth noticing rather than being coy about.

Now the part that will decide this over the next decade.

These guys, they don't know what they don't know in some of these things. And now with the combination of AI and other things, they need to lean in to the relational side with the clients that they have, or they're going to be clients of somebody else.

I am not going to make a prediction about technology. What I will say is that the portion of this business that consists of information, analysis, and reporting is getting cheaper every year, and the portion that consists of sitting with a widow in the worst month of her life is not.

Solomon's picture reaches two generations forward, to children's children, which means the thing being described is not a transaction. It is a chain. A chain fails at whichever link nobody inspected.

The link nobody inspects is the relationship with the people who have not inherited yet.

Go meet somebody's children.
