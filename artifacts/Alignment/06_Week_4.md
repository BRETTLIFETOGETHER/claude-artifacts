# Day 16
## That's a Real Bad Decision
### A lot of people buy insurance inside of a company

*Watch the Day 16 video before you read.*

**"Anyone who does not provide for their relatives, and especially for their own household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever."**
1 Timothy 5:8

As we got into their situation and realized the business that they had, and the size of the business, and what it was worth, we knew that at some point when they pass away, this business is going to be taxed. There's going to be a huge estate tax liability.

So we wanted to protect the family, so we went ahead and bought some insurance for them so that their family would be protected in the event that something happened to them.

And then also, we basically bought the insurance outside of their estate. We created an irrevocable life insurance trust, put the insurance in that trust, so therefore that money is not in their estate and won't be counted.

Two decisions there, not one, and they are frequently confused. The first was to buy coverage, which addressed a real liability. The second was where to put it, which determined whether the coverage would do the job it was bought for.

A great many families get the first right and the second wrong, and the second is invisible.

A lot of people buy insurance inside of a company. And they don't know the difference. That's a real bad decision, because the value of that insurance will get added to the value of the business, and the business value will go up.

If you have ten million dollars of book equity, you'll pay forty percent tax on that ten million. This way you move it outside of the business, therefore it's not taxed when that person dies and it protects the family.

*[Rate figure is Tom's on tape. Rates are indexed and subject to change. Print current values with an as-of date, or present illustratively.]*

Follow the arithmetic, because it is the part that persuades. A policy owned by the company pays into the company. The company is now worth that much more. The estate includes the company. So the death benefit, purchased to protect a family from an estate tax, has increased the estate and therefore increased the tax.

The instrument works perfectly. It is simply pointed at the wrong target.

So insurance is a very important product. But you need to make sure. Some people are insurance salesmen. They'll sell you insurance whether you need it or not. They'll tell you you need it, but you may or may not need it.

I am not making a general accusation about an industry. I hold no insurance license myself and I rely on a man who does. What I am describing is structural. Some people are compensated for placing coverage and not compensated for declining to place it, and that shapes advice whether or not anyone intends it to.

So that's why, in our holistic approach, we look at what is the potential estate tax liability. And if they do have an estate tax liability, how are they going to pay for that estate tax liability?

And there are a lot of ways to pay for it. But one of it is insurance.

Read that sequence again, because the order is the whole protection.

Liability first. Then the question of how it gets paid. Then insurance considered as one of several answers rather than as the answer. An advisor who works in that order cannot accidentally become a salesman, because the recommendation arrives at the end of an analysis rather than at the beginning of one.

An advisor who works in the other order has already decided, and everything after that is justification.

Paul writes about the obligation to provide and puts it in unusually severe terms. Failing to provide is not called an oversight. It is called a denial of faith.

What that does to this session is remove the option of treating placement as a technicality. If providing is a matter of faith and character, then so is whether the provision actually arrives where it was aimed.

Size the liability for one client before you discuss any policy with anybody.

# Day 17
## Who Owns the Policy?
### Who controls the policy?

*Watch the Day 17 video before you read.*

**"Now it is required that those who have been given a trust must prove faithful."**
1 Corinthians 4:2

Sometimes it becomes a question of ownership and responsibility.

Who owns the policy? If it's owned in the estate, or is it owned outside the estate? And then who controls the policy? Is it controlled by the company? Is it controlled by the individual? And then who benefits from the policy? And what responsibilities does that create across the family?

Because all those things are really important.

So the ownership. The insured, who's the person that's insured? Who's the beneficiary, who's going to get it? What are the premiums on the policy? Who's paying the premiums? What's the cash value? Is it creating any cash value? And if it is, is it inside the estate? Is it outside the estate?

That is not one question with several parts. Those are separate questions with separate answers, and they can each point at a different person.

Most people carry a single mental category called our life insurance, which contains a face amount and a vague sense that the family is taken care of. Underneath that category are six or seven distinct facts, and a policy can be entirely correct on five of them and produce an outcome nobody wanted because of the sixth.

Because those decisions don't just affect today, they affect how things play out over time and across generations.

Now, what happens when nobody has ever asked.

We might have a situation where a family might say, well, we have life insurance, and feel like they've kind of checked that box.

Checked the box is exactly right, and it is the most common condition I encounter. The box got checked years ago by somebody competent, and checking it produced a genuine feeling of having handled the matter, and that feeling has been doing the work of a review ever since.

But if you step back and ask what would actually happen if something changed tomorrow, that's when clarity begins.

Who would be affected? What responsibilities would still exist? What would need to continue?

Those three questions are worth more than any policy analysis, and you do not need a license to ask them. They are not about coverage. They are about consequences, and consequences are where a family can actually think clearly, because they involve people they know rather than instruments they do not understand.

And once you start asking those questions, you realize it's not about coverage, it's about whether the right things are actually protected, and whether the responsibility for that protection is clearly understood.

Read that twice. Not whether coverage exists. Whether the right things are protected, and whether anybody knows who is responsible for keeping that true.

Paul writes that those given a trust must prove faithful, and the word given is doing the work. A trust is not something a man takes on by ambition. It is handed to him, usually without ceremony, and the faithfulness required is mostly administrative. Knowing what you hold. Knowing what it is for. Checking that it still does what it was meant to do.

A man who has been handed responsibility for a family's protection and has never read the ownership line on the policy has not been unfaithful in any dramatic sense. He has simply not looked.

Read down the ownership column on one client's policies this week. It takes ten minutes and it is where the money is.

# Day 18
## The Coverage Made Sense Then
### But over time, things change

*Watch the Day 18 video before you read.*

**"The prudent see danger and take refuge, but the simple keep going and pay the penalty."**
Proverbs 22:3

If you actually ask most families to walk through whatever their insurance plan is and what they have and what it covers and how it fits together, they'd probably pause and say, I don't really know what the deductible is. I don't know what our monthly or annual is. I just felt like five years ago we got it taken care of.

Five years ago we got it taken care of. That sentence is the entire diagnosis, and it is said with complete sincerity by capable people.

It may need to be updated, not because nothing exists, but because it hasn't been looked at as a whole in light of all the other things, because the company's value and all the other assets may have gone way up.

The guy who runs the company, he's busy, he's got a lot of things going on. He's not thinking about whether he has enough insurance.

I want to be generous about that because it is accurate. He is not careless. He is running a company, and the number of things demanding his attention on any given Tuesday does not include his liability limits. Coverage decisions have no natural review trigger. Nothing arrives in the mail to tell a man his limits are now inadequate.

But somebody comes along and says, have you thought about how much you have?

That is the whole method, and it is available to you this week with no preparation. There is no diagnostic subtlety required. The question itself does the work, because a business owner who hears it can generally do the arithmetic faster than you can present it.

Especially based upon what happens when trailer truck accidents happen, and they're usually very serious accidents. People are really messed up after an accident.

Becomes something you do because you're supposed to, not something you think through intentionally. And when that happens, coverage can be incomplete. It could be outdated, or not aligned with the family's actual situation.

Now the pattern, which is the same one you have seen in the documents week and will see again.

A family might have put coverage in place when their situation looked very different. At that time, their income was at one level, their responsibilities were simpler, and the number of people depending on them may have been smaller. So the coverage made sense then.

But over time, things change. The company is now worth a whole lot more. They have a whole lot more that they could lose. Income has grown, assets have grown, responsibilities have increased. Maybe there's a business now that they didn't have before. Maybe there are more people depending upon that income than there were at that time.

Nobody made an error at any point in that story. A decision that was correct in one year stayed correct-looking while the facts underneath it moved.

That is the failure mode of this entire module, and here it appears in its clearest form. The document did not go wrong. The policy did not go wrong. The world moved and nothing followed it.

Which means the remedy is not intelligence. It is a review interval. A crude annual look catches drift. A brilliant analysis performed once in 2011 does not.

Solomon says the prudent see danger and take refuge, and the contrast is not with the reckless. It is with the simple, who keep going. That is not a description of a fool. It is a description of a man proceeding normally, at speed, without stopping to look up.

Every business owner you serve is the simple man in that proverb about at least one thing.

Ask one of them this week what liability coverage the operation carries.

# Day 20
## You Have It in the Wrong Place
### We need to put it in the right place

*Watch the Day 20 video before you read.*

**"Each of you should use whatever gift you have received to serve others, as faithful stewards of God's grace in its various forms."**
1 Peter 4:10

For a lot of families, that's where the disconnect is. They know they have something, but they don't really understand how it functions over time, because it gets more complex as things grow or change and families emerge.

Here's another important layer. If the policy is inside the estate or outside of it, because that affects how it's treated, how it's transferred, and how it interacts with taxes and the broader plan.

Think about it this way. If a policy is inside the estate, it may increase the size of the estate and create additional tax exposure. If it's outside of the estate, it may pass differently and serve a different role in the overall plan.

So where it sits really does matter, not just that it exists.

That last line is the whole week compressed into one sentence, and it is worth handing to a client verbatim.

A lot of people think, oh, we have life insurance, we're in great shape.

No, you're not in great shape. You have insurance, but you have it in the wrong place. We need to put it in the right place, and then it will be great if you have it.

Notice the structure, because it is why the sentence works in a room rather than wounding somebody.

The first half takes something away. The second half gives it back. What is being corrected is not the decision to buy the policy, which was sound, and not the man who bought it, who was doing right by his family. What is being corrected is an administrative fact about where the thing sits.

Get that distinction wrong and you have told a client his judgment was poor. Get it right and you have told him his judgment was fine and his paperwork is in the wrong drawer. Same finding, entirely different conversation.

And we talk about ownership, and we talk about responsibility, but the truth as well, especially for a Christian family, it's stewardship. It's ownership and it's responsibility of stewardship and managing the limited or the significant resources that God has entrusted.

Keyword, entrusted.

That word changes the arithmetic in a way worth naming. If the resources are yours, placement is optimization and optimization is optional. If they were entrusted, placement is faithfulness with something belonging to someone else, and faithfulness is not optional.

So now you're not looking at just coverage. There's a lot more complexity to it. It's not as simple as a phone call and a product. You're looking at placement. You're looking at the dynamics that can help further, both provide safety and leverage for the family in the future, especially as it relates to a business.

So, in conclusion, a healthy approach is to review coverage regularly. Ask what are you protecting, what coverage exists, and whether it still matches your situation today.

It's not a one-time decision. So much of this stuff is not a one-time decision. It needs to be revisited as your life changes, and it needs to be adjusted as situations change.

Peter writes about stewards of grace in its various forms, and the phrase various forms is the one to notice. Stewardship is not one activity. It takes the shape of whatever has been entrusted, which means for some people it looks like teaching and for others it looks like reading an ownership line on an insurance policy so that a family receives what was intended for them.

That is not a lesser form of it. It is the form yours has taken.

Read the ownership line. Then put the review on a calendar, because nothing here survives good intentions.
