# Day 6
## All the Money Managers All Use the Same Strategy
### They could have had just one guy

**"Invest in seven ventures, yes, in eight; you do not know what disaster may come upon the land."**
Ecclesiastes 11:2

Sometimes people hire three different money managers, but all the money managers all use the same strategy. So basically, they could have had just one guy that put a third of their money into different things.

That family did what careful people do. They did not want everything sitting with one firm, so they spread it across three. It felt prudent. It looked like diversification. It came with three sets of reports, three relationships, and three fee schedules.

Then somebody laid the three portfolios side by side and they were running essentially the same strategy. The same thinking, the same tilt, the same exposures, arrived at independently by three competent professionals who had never spoken to each other and had no reason to.

Nobody did anything wrong. Each manager did the job he was hired to do. The failure sat in the space between them, which is exactly the space nobody was being paid to look at.

Most families have assets spread among different places, sometimes with different advisors managing their money, different strategies, different structures. Each piece may make sense on its own. They have not stepped back to look at how it all fits together.

Here is the version of this I see more often, and it is worse because it is invisible for longer.

You have one part of the assets trying to grow aggressively. Another part trying to generate income for the family. A third part built to minimize taxes. Every one of those makes sense on its own, and every one of them was somebody's good idea at the time. If they are not coordinated, it is three different strategies pulling in three different directions, and nobody is asking whether they actually work together.

The result is not usually loss. It is friction. Inefficiency in some places, redundancy in others, and a family that feels something is not connected and cannot tell you what.

There is also the tax layer sitting underneath all of it, which almost never enters the conversation between managers. You have tax-paying assets and you have non-tax assets like retirement accounts. What kind of assets you put in each one of those is a little bit different. If nobody is deciding which bucket holds which asset, the buckets get filled by accident.

Solomon's instruction is genuinely about spreading risk. Seven ventures, then eight, because the future is unknown. The families I am describing believe they have obeyed it. They have not. They have one venture wearing three names, and they are paying three times to hold it.

The distinction is between the number of managers and the number of ideas. Nobody counts the second one. It does not appear on any statement, and there is no report that shows it, and the only way to see it is to put the holdings on one page and look down the columns.

That is not sophisticated work. It is an afternoon.

What makes it rare is that it requires somebody willing to look at accounts he is not paid on and ask a question that could embarrass a professional the family likes.

Do it anyway. The family is not paying you for tact.

# Day 7
## It's a Clarity Problem
### So the assets go the way mom and dad want them to

**"By wisdom a house is built, and through understanding it is established."**
Proverbs 24:3

You may have a parent who assumes everything is going to pass in a certain way. Some of the assets are in a trust. Some have beneficiaries. Some are owned inside an entity. And when you actually map it out, it doesn't go the way that they thought it was going to go.

That's not a technical problem, it's a clarity problem.

I want to sit on that sentence because it changes how you handle the conversation entirely.

If it were a technical problem, somebody would have made a mistake and the job would be to find it and correct it and probably to replace whoever made it. That is not what happened here. Every individual decision was made correctly. A beneficiary was named when an account was opened, and it was the right person that day. An asset went into a trust for good reasons. An interest sat inside an entity because a transaction required it.

What was never done is the assembly. And assembly is where the mismatch lives.

Part of creating that clarity starts with understanding ownership. Who owns what. Which accounts are owned by whom. Assets may be held individually, held jointly between a husband and a wife, held in a trust, or held inside entities. If that is not clearly understood, it becomes difficult to see how everything really fits together.

So we are just trying to get that clarity so that we can make sure the assets go the way mom and dad want them to.

That is the whole objective and it is worth saying plainly to the client, because it is not what they expect you to say. They expect optimization. What you are offering is congruence between what they have decided and what will actually occur, which sounds smaller and is worth considerably more.

Now, the practical consequence.

Because this is a clarity problem rather than a technical one, you do not open by correcting anybody. You open by letting the client describe what he believes will happen, in his own words, and you write it down. That description is the standard. Then you pull the actual designations and the trust terms and the entity documents, and you map where each significant asset really goes.

Then you put the two versions on one page. What he told you. What the documents do.

I have never had that comparison go badly. Clients respond to it with recognition rather than defensiveness, and the reason is that the page does not accuse anybody. Nobody made a mistake. The page simply shows what happens when eleven correct decisions get made in eleven different rooms across twenty years.

Solomon puts building and establishing in sequence, and they are not the same act. A house gets built by wisdom, which is the making of good individual decisions. It gets established through understanding, which is something else, and it comes second.

A great many of your clients have built. Almost none of them have established.

The work of establishing is unglamorous. It is reading documents somebody else drafted and making a chart.

Nobody else in that family's life is going to do it.

# Day 8
## A Life Insurance Policy Doesn't Go Through a Will
### You don't see that until it's too late

**"A good person leaves an inheritance for their children's children."**
Proverbs 13:22

Sometimes people think that everything passes through their will, and many of their assets don't go through their will at all. They go because of beneficiary designation. A life insurance policy doesn't go through a will. A retirement fund doesn't go through a will.

That is the single most common false belief I encounter in this work, and it is held by intelligent people with good counsel.

They all go as the beneficiary has been named. So the result is not what everybody expected.

Different assets pass in different ways. Some go through a will. Some go through a trust. Some go through a beneficiary designation. If those are not coordinated, you end up with outcomes that do not match what the family intended. One asset goes one direction through a will while another goes somewhere else through a beneficiary designation, and those two decisions were not made together, and in most cases they were not made in the same decade.

And the challenge is you don't see that until it's too late.

I want to be exact about what too late means, because it is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Too late does not mean expensive. It means unavailable. A beneficiary designation is trivial to change while the account holder is breathing and impossible to change afterward. There is no negotiation, no cure period, and no sympathetic reading of what the man clearly would have wanted. The form says what it says, and the form was probably filled out at an onboarding meeting by somebody who was mostly thinking about the paperwork ending.

That is the whole risk profile. Nearly zero cost to fix today. Total and permanent cost tomorrow.

Which is why I do not treat this as a review item. I treat it as the thing to do first, before the interesting work, because the interesting work is recoverable and this is not.

The people who get hurt by this are almost never the account holder. He is gone. The people who get hurt are a second spouse who receives nothing, or a first spouse who receives everything, or three children who find out that one of them was named on the largest account in 2004 and nobody ever went back.

Then the family has to decide whether to believe that the outcome was intended.

That is the real damage, and it does not show up in any tax calculation. A family that suspects a document said what their father meant will grieve. A family that suspects it did not will litigate, or worse, will not litigate and will carry it for thirty years.

Solomon's picture reaches two generations forward, to children's children, which means the thing being described is not a transaction. It is a chain. A chain fails at whichever link nobody inspected, and this is the link nobody inspects, because it is a form in a file rather than a document with a lawyer attached to it.

Go pull the designations on your five largest relationships this week.

You will find something. I have never once done this exercise with a family and found nothing.

# Day 9
## Why Is It There?
### A purpose within the larger picture

**"Steady plodding brings prosperity; hasty speculation brings poverty."**
Proverbs 21:5 (TLB)

You start by mapping it out. What assets exist, how are they being managed, and what role is each one playing. Then you ask, does this make sense together, and does it align with what the family is trying to accomplish.

You are not just reviewing performance. Quite honestly, you are not reviewing performance at all. You are reviewing the asset itself, and you are evaluating it from an alignment standpoint rather than from how much it is growing in value.

Each piece could have a purpose within the larger picture. If it doesn't, then you have to ask, why is it there?

I like that question because it cannot be answered with a number, and almost every other question in this business can be.

Performance is easy to focus on and it is measurable, which is exactly why it crowds out everything else. Your quarterly meetings are already built around it. Your reporting produces it automatically. A client can look at a performance figure and feel that a review has occurred.

Alignment requires stepping back and looking at how everything connects, and there is no report that generates it. Somebody has to sit down and ask, of each holding, what job this is doing for this family.

Sometimes the answer is good. It is generating income the family needs, or it is holding a position for a reason that still applies. Fine. Move on.

Sometimes there is no answer. The holding is there because it was there last year. Somebody bought it in 2011 for a reason that expired in 2015 and nobody has revisited it, and it has been quietly performing adequately ever since, which is the best possible camouflage.

An asset that performs poorly gets noticed. An asset that performs acceptably and serves no purpose can sit undisturbed for a decade.

Once you bring it all together, people begin to see differently. You can align strategies with goals, simplify where needed, and clarify how each piece fits into the overall plan. And that changes how decisions get made going forward. Instead of reacting to individual opportunities, you begin evaluating everything in the context of the whole.

Notice what that sentence does to the sales cycle in your own practice. An advisor evaluating opportunities one at a time is permanently reactive, because each opportunity arrives on its own merits and each one can be argued for. An advisor evaluating against a whole has a standard, and a standard lets you decline things.

Solomon's word is plodding, which nobody would choose for a brochure. Steady, unglamorous, repetitive work produces the result. Speculation, which is more interesting to do and much more interesting to talk about, produces the other one.

Asking why is it there of forty holdings is plodding. It is genuinely tedious and it takes an afternoon and there is no way to make it sound impressive at a conference.

It is also the difference between managing a family's assets and stewarding a family's plan.

Ask it of one holding today.

# Day 10
## He Doesn't Get Paid on That $15 Million
### But we care about the business

**"So in everything, do to others what you would have them do to you."**
Matthew 7:12

The average advisor, all he cares about is the money under management that he manages. He forgets that this guy owns a company. That company is worth fifteen million dollars. But he does not manage it. So he does not get paid on that fifteen million.

Add the fifteen million to all the other assets, including the life insurance the man owns personally, and all of a sudden the guy has an estate tax problem. And the advisor is not aware of it, and he is not thinking about it, because he does not care about the business, because he does not get anything off of it.

But we care about the business, because it's part of the big picture of what the family has.

That is the whole argument and it takes one sentence.

I want to be fair to the advisor in that story, because he is not a bad man and I am not describing negligence. He is doing precisely what his compensation trained him to do. He has a scope, the scope is defined by what he is paid on, and everything outside the scope is genuinely invisible to him. Not ignored. Invisible. It does not appear in his reporting, it does not come up in his review meetings, and no part of his process would ever surface it.

That is the most dangerous kind of blind spot, because the man cannot detect it by trying harder.

The only cure is a decision, made once, in advance: I look at the whole balance sheet regardless of what I am compensated on. That decision costs you some hours and it will occasionally produce a finding that leads to work you do not perform and are not paid for.

Take it anyway, and here is the practical reason on top of the ethical one.

It is a huge opportunity for the advisor to get to know the family better, to build relationships multi-generationally in the family, and to help the family do everything they want to do.

Then think about what happens when the patriarch dies. There is a whole lot of work that needs to be done, and the advisor is either a part of it or he is not. If he is there, supporting the wife, she is probably going to stay with him. And if the children have been brought into the situation as well, they are probably going to keep the assets there.

It is a win-win situation.

I do not say that to make the ethical case commercial. I say it because both are true and pretending otherwise is dishonest. Looking at assets you are not paid on is right, and it is also the single best business decision available to you, and the fact that those two point the same direction is not something to be embarrassed about.

The Golden Rule is usually read as a rule about kindness. It is more precise than that. It says to do what you would want done, which requires you to first imagine yourself in the position of the person across the table.

So imagine it. You own a business worth fifteen million dollars, which is most of what you have. You are paying a competent professional to advise you on the rest. Would you want him to have noticed that the largest thing you own creates a tax problem for your family?

Of course you would. And you would be astonished to learn he never looked.

Go look at what you are not paid on.
