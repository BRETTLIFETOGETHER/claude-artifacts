# EMAIL 0 · SEVEN DAYS OUT

**Subject line** — *Something we're doing for six weeks* · alt: *Nobody is going to ask you for anything*

---

This Sunday we start something called 40 Days of Generosity. Six weeks, and I want to tell you up front what it is and what it isn't.

It isn't a campaign for anything. There's no building, no budget gap, no pledge card at the end. Nobody from the church is going to call you, and I'm not going to ask you for money in week six. I've asked our whole team to hold that line, and they've agreed.

Here's what it is. Six weeks working through what the Bible actually says about ownership, contentment, fear, what we can see, our families, and joy. Most of us only ever hear about money at church when the church needs some, which trains all of us to brace. I'd like to spend six weeks not doing that.

Three ways to be part of it. Come on Sundays. Pick up a daily reader — five minutes a morning, forty days. And if you want, join a group: six weeks, ninety minutes, in someone's home.

Groups are the part I'd nudge you toward. Something happens in a living room that doesn't happen in a row.

# EMAIL 1 · AFTER WEEK ONE

**Subject line** — *Who owns it?* · alt: *One sentence*

---

If you were with us Sunday, you left with a card and one sentence to finish: *God owns ______, and my job is ______.*

I want to tell you that I found mine harder to write than I expected. Not the theology — I've believed that for thirty years. The specific blank. Naming one actual account, one actual thing, and saying out loud that it isn't mine.

David does this in 1 Chronicles 29 at the peak of the most generous day of his life. He's just gathered the largest offering in Israel's history, and instead of thanking anyone he says: everything comes from you, and we've given you only what came from your hand.

He doesn't congratulate them. He tells them they never owned it.

Here's the whole thing in one question: *how much should I give* is subtraction, and subtraction always hurts. *What does the owner want done with this* is allocation. Nobody in history has ever given joyfully while doing subtraction.

If you haven't written the sentence, write it this week. Put it somewhere you'll see it for the next thirty-nine days.

And if you didn't write it — that's genuinely fine. Nobody's checking.

# EMAIL 2 · AFTER WEEK TWO

**Subject line** — *How much is enough?* · alt: *The number nobody writes*

---

Sunday we talked about the question almost nobody asks.

Most of us learned a floor — ten percent, give first, be faithful. Good, and it's done enormous good. But a floor only answers the smallest question and leaves every other one open. Almost none of us was ever handed a ceiling.

Here's what a ceiling does. Once a household names the annual number at which it has enough, everything above the line has an obvious destination, and the argument you've been having every December quietly ends.

Agur prays for it in Proverbs 30 — neither poverty nor riches — and he gives his reasons. Too much and I may forget you. Too little and I may steal. He isn't being humble. He's being careful about his own soul.

This week: write the number. And if you can't, write down what makes it hard. That answer is honestly worth more.

One thing I want to say clearly, because this is the week it could be misheard. I'm not asking you to give more. I'm asking you to decide what's enough, and I have no idea what you'll do with the answer. Those are different things, and I meant what I said in week one.

# EMAIL 3 · AFTER WEEK THREE

**Subject line** — *What are you actually afraid of?* · alt: *Open hands*

---

This was the hardest Sunday of the six, and I think it was the truest.

Almost nobody holds back because they love money. We hold back because we're afraid — of running out, of a downturn, of a child's future, of needing help someday and having nobody. Greed is something you repent of. Fear is something somebody has to walk you out of, slowly.

There's a widow in 1 Kings 17 gathering sticks to cook a last meal for herself and her son, and a stranger asks her to make him something first. From outside it looks reckless. From inside it's trust — she wasn't careless with her son's life, she was betting on the character of the God who sent the man.

And the promise underneath all of this, in Hebrews, isn't what we usually assume. It isn't *you'll always have enough.* It's *never will I leave you.* The comfort is a person, not a balance.

No assignment this week. Just name the fear specifically — not "the future," the actual scenario you rehearse at three in the morning — and tell it to God in those words.

If Sunday surfaced something heavier than money, please reach out. I mean that, and I'll answer.

# EMAIL 4 · AFTER WEEK FOUR

**Subject line** — *Go and look* · alt: *What do you see?*

---

When God calls the prophets, he doesn't ask what they're going to do. He asks what they see. Jeremiah gets shown a branch. Amos gets shown a plumb line. The assignment always comes after the sight.

Nehemiah does the ordinary version. He hears the walls are down, and before he says a word to anyone he rides out at night and looks at them himself.

We fund what we can picture. That's not shallowness, it's how attention works. Which means one of the most spiritually significant things any of us could do this year is go somewhere and stand there.

So that's this week. Pick one thing you already support and go see it. If you can't think of anything you've seen firsthand, that's the finding, and it's worth sitting with.

Put a date on the calendar before Sunday. Not "sometime." A date. It's the only thing from this week that won't happen on its own.

If you'd like a suggestion of somewhere to go, reply and I'll give you three.

# EMAIL 5 · AFTER WEEK FIVE

**Subject line** — *Twelve stones* · alt: *Who's watching*

---

After Israel crossed the Jordan, Joshua had them pull twelve stones out of the riverbed and stack them where everybody could see. The reason he gives is oddly practical: so that when children ask what these mean, somebody will have to tell the story.

Most families have no stones. No object, no date, no repeated telling. Just decisions that get made privately and assets that show up at the end with no explanation attached.

Nobody in this church was argued into generosity. Every generous person I know caught it from somebody — a parent, a friend, a neighbor whose choices didn't quite make sense.

Two things this week.

Build a stone. A framed photograph, an annual meal, an object on a shelf — anything a child would ask about.

And have one conversation about a gift you'll decide together. A spouse, a child, a friend, a roommate. Let them have a real vote.

And if you're single, or your kids are grown, or your family is far away — this is still for you. Somebody is watching how you handle money. That's all it takes.

# EMAIL 6 · AFTER WEEK SIX

**Subject line** — *Nobody asked* · alt: *One sentence to finish*

---

Six weeks ago I told you nobody would ask you for anything.

Nobody did.

I want to name that, because I know some of you have been waiting for the other shoe. There wasn't one. There isn't a card in the mail this week either.

What I hope happened instead is that a few hundred of us settled something. Who owns it. What enough looks like. What we're actually afraid of. Where we've stopped looking. Who's watching.

Sunday we ended on one sentence, and it's the only assignment left: *when our work is done, ______ will be true.* One sentence. No qualifiers. Write it and put it with the first card, the one from week one.

Almost everyone in your life will offer you a need. Almost nobody will offer you a finish. A family that adopts an ending stops asking how much to give this year and starts asking when the work could be done — and that question changes more than any sermon I could preach.

Thank you for six weeks. It's been one of the better seasons I've had here.

If you want to keep going, reply and tell me. We'll figure out what's next together.

# THE HOST TRACK

Sent Sunday evening. Eighty words. From whoever runs groups, not the senior pastor.

**Week 1** — You're on this week. Two things only: read the covenant out loud exactly as written, and after your first question, count to fifteen before you say anything. It'll feel unbearable. Somebody always starts talking around eleven. You will feel like you did it badly. Every host does. The group won't notice.

**Week 2** — Tonight is the number. Somebody in your room may get quiet or uncomfortable — that's normal and it's usually fear, not resistance. Don't chase it in the room. Text them tomorrow instead.

**Week 3** — This is the pastoral one. Some of what comes up won't be about money. Receive it, thank them, don't solve it, and follow up during the week. Keep my number handy.

**Week 4** — Get the visit on a calendar before people leave. Not "sometime" — an actual date. It's the only thing from this week that won't happen on its own.

**Week 5** — Say at the top, not the end, that "family" means whoever they share life with. Otherwise the single people in your room spend ninety minutes as spectators.

**Week 6** — Last one. Don't skip the guardrail — the return is real and it isn't a promise of wealth. And when you close, pray and stop. No announcements, no next steps. Somebody has waited six weeks to see if we meant it.

# NOTES FOR WHOEVER SENDS THESE

**One idea per email.** The temptation is to add the youth retreat and the parking reminder. Don't. Six weeks of single-subject email from the pastor is itself part of the message.

**Reply-to a person.** Emails 3, 4, and 6 all invite a reply. Somebody has to actually answer them or the invitation was a device.

**Do not report giving numbers** during or after these six weeks. Not in an email, not from the platform, not in the annual report as a campaign result.

**Segment if you can.** People in groups can get a two-line addition at the bottom about their session. People who aren't should never be told what they're missing.

**Keep every send under 250 words.** Every word past that is a word somebody didn't read.
