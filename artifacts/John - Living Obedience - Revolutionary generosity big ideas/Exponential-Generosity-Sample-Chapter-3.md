# We're Counting the Wrong Things

---

---

I sat through a conference a few years ago where eleven organizations presented in a single afternoon, and every one of them opened with a number.

Dollars raised. Assets under management. Total granted since inception. Some of the numbers were enormous and some were modest, and the modest ones were presented apologetically, the way a man apologizes for a small house. The slides were well made. The people were sincere. Several of them do work I would fund tomorrow.

And somewhere in the seventh presentation it occurred to me that I had now watched three hours of reporting and could not tell you what any of it had accomplished.

Not one presenter said: here is what changed. Here is what exists now that did not exist before. Here is a thing that multiplied. They told us how much money had moved and how much money was held, and we all wrote it down, because those are the numbers our field has agreed to keep.

I want to be careful, because I am not standing outside this. My organization keeps those numbers too. We publish them. I have used them in a room to establish that we are worth taking seriously, and I will probably do it again next year, because that is the currency the field trades in and I have not yet found a way to opt out of it without sounding evasive.

But I have come to believe that the numbers we keep are quietly shaping what we do, and not in the direction any of us intended.

---

## What each number actually measures

Take the three that dominate this field and ask what each one is really counting.

**Dollars raised** measures the persuasiveness of the person asking. That is all it measures. An organization that raises forty million dollars has a superb development department, which may or may not correlate with doing superb work — and in my experience the correlation is weaker than anyone wants to admit. Some of the most effective people I have ever funded are terrible at explaining themselves. Some of the most polished presentations I have sat through were describing work that was already failing.

**Assets under management** measures accumulation. It is the metric every financial institution in the world reports, for the obvious reason that it is also the basis on which most of them are paid. I understand why it exists. I would simply point out that a Kingdom organization reporting how much it holds is reporting the same number a bank reports, and expecting it to mean something different.

**Percentage given** measures the giver's discipline. This one is closest to the heart and still misses. It tells you what left your hand. It says nothing whatsoever about what happened next.

Three numbers, and all three of them are inputs. Not one of them is a measure of fruit. We have built an entire field on a dashboard of things that happen before anything happens.

---

## The number I would rather report

There is a better one available, and it is not complicated.

**How fast does the money leave?**

Not how much is held. How much moved, as a proportion of what was held, in the last twelve months. Call it velocity, which is the word economists use for exactly this — they long ago worked out that counting the currency in circulation tells you very little, and that the important question is how many times each unit changes hands. The same dollar, moving quickly, does the work of five sitting still.

I am fond of this metric partly because my own organization performs well on it, and I want to name that bias out loud rather than pretend I arrived at it neutrally. Our community grants at a rate well above the industry average. Every time I say that in public I am aware that I am reporting a number that flatters us.

So let me say the harder thing. Velocity is a better metric than balance, and it is still an input. It tells you the money moved. It does not tell you that anything multiplied. A fund can grant quickly into ground that produces nothing, and the dashboard will look excellent.

If our field ever gets serious about this, velocity is the second step, not the destination.

## The objection, which is a good one

Someone reading this is already forming a sensible response: you cannot measure Kingdom fruit.

That is largely true, and I want to concede it fully rather than argue around it.

The parable of the growing seed says the man scatters, sleeps, rises night and day, and the seed sprouts and grows — and he does not know how. Paul plants, Apollos waters, God gives the growth, and neither of the men is anything. A translator's work may produce nothing measurable for a decade and then produce a church movement forty years after he dies. There is no dashboard that captures that, and any attempt to build one will distort the thing it is measuring.

Worse than that: the demand for measurement has its own pathology. Funders who require reportable outcomes end up funding the organizations best at producing reports, which is a different skill from doing the work, and often a competing one. I have watched good ministries hire a communications director before they hired a second field worker, because they had learned what donors reward. We taught them that. We should own it.

So the honest position is narrower than the one this chapter might seem to be arguing. You cannot measure whether it bore fruit.

You can measure whether you tried.

That distinction carries more weight than it looks like. A family cannot know what its giving produced. It can know, with complete precision, whether it ever asked the question, whether it went and looked, whether it funded the year nobody photographs, and whether the money left the account or sat in it. Those are all knowable. Almost nobody tracks any of them.

---

## Four numbers for a household

Here is what I would rather a family measure. None of these requires a consultant, and all four can be calculated on a Saturday morning.

**One. Your outflow rate.** Dollars granted last year divided by dollars held in charitable accounts. If you have a donor advised fund, a foundation, or a designated giving account, you already have both numbers. Write down the percentage. Then decide what you want it to be, and tell someone.

**Two. Your asset ratio.** What proportion of last year's giving came from something other than cash? For most families the honest answer is zero, which is not a moral failure — it is a strong indication that nobody ever explained the alternative. This is the number with the most room in it. It is frequently the difference between giving what you can spare and giving what you actually have.

**Three. Your speed.** Days from the moment you were convinced to the moment the transfer settled. Measure your last significant gift. Most families have never timed this and are startled by the answer. A household that takes eleven months to say yes will miss most of what it is asked to do, and the failure will feel like circumstance rather than design.

**Four. Givers produced.** How many people in your household made an independent giving decision this year — researched something, defended it, and moved real money? Include yourself. For a great many families the answer is one, or one and a half.

That fourth number is the one I would put at the top if I were building the dashboard from scratch. Everything else in this book is about multiplication, and the most durable multiplication available to any family is not a larger gift. It is another giver.

## What the shrewd manager was commended for

There is a parable in Luke 16 that has embarrassed commentators for two thousand years, because a dishonest manager is held up as an example. Jesus is not commending the fraud. He is commending the alertness — the man understood his situation, understood the clock, and acted decisively while he still could. And then comes the line that has never quite left me: use worldly wealth to gain friends for yourselves, so that when it is gone you will be welcomed into eternal dwellings.

Whatever else that passage is doing, it is treating money as something to be deployed rather than held, on a timeline that is running out, toward relationships that outlast the money itself.

The manager kept exactly one metric. How fast can I move this, and toward whom?

I have never met a foundation that reports that number. I have met a great many that report how much they hold.

---

## The work

**Calculate your outflow rate this week.** Two numbers you already have, one division. Write it on a card and put it where you keep your giving records.

**Calculate your asset ratio for last year.** If it is zero, that is the finding, and chapter four is written for you.

**Time your last significant gift.** Conviction to settlement, in days. Write down what slowed it.

**Count your givers.** Not your dependents. The people in your household who made a real giving decision this year and could defend it. If the number is one, that is the most important number in this chapter.

## For reflection

1. Which numbers have you been quietly proud of, and what do they actually measure?

2. Where has your giving been shaped by what is easy to report rather than by what you believe is true?

3. If you could keep only one number about your household's generosity, what would you want it to be — and are you keeping it?

## A prayer

Father, you have never once asked me for a report. You know what I gave and where it went and whether any of it grew, and none of that is hidden from you. Keep me from measuring the wrong things because they are easy to count, and from refusing to measure anything because the truth is inconvenient. Make me alert, like the manager in the story — awake to the clock, quick with what is not mine, and more interested in what it produces than in what it proves about me. Amen.
