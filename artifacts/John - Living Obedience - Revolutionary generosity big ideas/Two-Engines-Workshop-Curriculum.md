# TWO ENGINES
### A Workshop on Vision, Obedience, and the Generosity That Follows

**Co-branded curriculum — The Signatry with LifeTogether**

---

**Status: developing.** Curriculum draft for review. Co-branding arrangement not yet formalized; partner logos, legal lines, and any third-party marks are placeholders pending agreement.

**Format:** Four sessions, 75 minutes each. One long day, or two half-days, or four monthly meetings.
**Group size:** 8–16. Below eight the room goes quiet; above sixteen the honest conversation stops.
**Participants:** Families with assets, business owners, and — where the host chooses — their advisors.
**Facilitators:** Two. One carries the teaching, one runs the room.

---

## THE GOVERNING RULE

**Nobody asks for anything.**

No solicitation, no pledge card, no follow-up call from a development officer, no vehicle presentation, no "if you'd like to learn more about opening a fund." This rule is not politeness — it is the reason the room tells the truth. Every participant has spent years being handled, and they can smell an ask through a wall. The moment they detect one, the workshop becomes a sales meeting and everything after it is theater.

The Signatry's own mechanics are off the table in the room. They belong in a separate, clearly-labeled conversation that a participant initiates, on a different day. Facilitators should say this out loud in the first three minutes of Session One and then honor it without exception.

---

## THE ARGUMENT THE WORKSHOP MAKES

Two engines drive generosity. **Vision** is the pull — what a person can see, and how big it is. **Obedience** is the yes — the willingness to act when told, before the outcome is visible.

Vision without obedience produces talk: grand plans, deferred giving, a foundation that admires its own balance sheet. Obedience without vision produces faithfulness that never becomes catalytic — a lifetime of good, small, reactive gifts from someone who could have funded a movement and never saw one worth funding.

Most people in the room are running strong on one engine. The workshop's job is to help each person find out which one, and to start the other.

# SESSION ONE — WHAT DO YOU SEE?

## Participant Guide

**Opening question:** Think of the most meaningful gift you have ever made. Not the largest — the most meaningful. What made it different?

**The two engines.** Generosity runs on vision and obedience. Vision is what you can see. Obedience is your willingness to act on what you're told. Neither works alone.

- Strong heart, small vision → faithful, scattered giving. You give to what crosses your desk.
- Big vision, unsettled heart → announced intentions, delayed gifts, projects that carry your name.
- Both running → the people whose giving changed something.

**The diagnostic.** Mark the statement closer to true for you.

1. I give consistently and could not tell you what most of it produced. / I can describe exactly what I am funding and why.
2. I have talked about a major gift for more than two years without making it. / When I decide, I move within weeks.
3. My giving decisions are mostly responses to requests. / My giving decisions mostly originate with me.
4. I have visited the work I fund in the last three years. / I fund from a distance.
5. My family could not describe what we are trying to accomplish. / My family could state it in a sentence.

**God's question to the prophets.** When God commissions Jeremiah, Amos, and Zechariah, the question is not *what will you do*. It is *what do you see* (Jeremiah 1:11; Amos 7:8; Zechariah 4:2). Sight comes before assignment. A person who cannot see anything specific will give generically — which is how enormous capacity ends up funding whatever asks loudest.

**Humphrey Monmouth.** A London cloth merchant who saw an English Bible before one existed. He gave William Tyndale a room in his own house, funded the translation, and used his merchant ships to move the printed New Testaments back into England. He served a year in the Tower of London for it. History remembers Tyndale. Almost nobody remembers the man who paid.

**Exercise — the sight inventory.** Write down three things you have seen with your own eyes in the last five years that you could not stop thinking about. Not causes. Not categories. Things you saw.

**Take home.** Ask one person who knows you well: what do you think I actually care about? Compare their answer to your list.

**Reflection**
1. Which engine is running strong in you, and which one is idling?
2. When was the last time you saw something that changed what you wanted to do with your money?
3. What have you been giving to out of habit rather than sight?

---

## Session One — Teaching Script

**[0:00–0:05] Frame and the rule.**
Welcome, introductions kept short — name, what you do, nothing about capacity. Then state the rule plainly: *Nobody in this room is going to ask you for anything. Not today, not next week. No one will call you. If that changes how you listen, good — that's the point.* Do not soften it or make a joke of it. Say it and move.

**[0:05–0:15] Opening question, in pairs.**
Most meaningful gift, not largest. Pairs for six minutes, then three or four report out. Do not editorialize on what they say. The room needs to hear each other before it hears you.

*Facilitator note:* People almost always describe a gift that was small, personal, and connected to something they had seen. That's the whole session, discovered rather than taught. Let them find it.

**[0:15–0:30] Teach the two engines.**
Draw them. Vision on one side, obedience on the other. Work the two failure modes hard, because the room contains both.

Key language: *Vision without obedience produces talk. Obedience without vision produces faithfulness that never becomes catalytic.* Say it slowly. Then: *Most of you are strong on one engine. Almost nobody is strong on both, and nobody has ever told you they were separate things.*

**[0:30–0:40] The diagnostic.**
Silent, individual, five minutes. Then: *Don't share your answers.* Instead ask one question aloud — *which engine is idling?* — and let a few answer. The point is self-knowledge, not disclosure.

**[0:40–0:55] What do you see?**
Teach the prophetic question. Jeremiah is shown an almond branch and a boiling pot before he is given a word. Amos is shown a plumb line. Sight precedes assignment, every time.

Then the turn: *If you cannot see anything specific, you will give generically. And generic giving is how the largest capacity in the American church ends up funding whoever writes the best appeal letter.*

**[0:55–1:05] Monmouth.**
Tell it as a story, not a lesson. Merchant, room in his own house, ships, the Tower. Land on the contrast: every English Bible traces back to Tyndale, and Tyndale traces back to a businessman nobody has heard of.

*Facilitator note:* Resist applying it. The room will apply it faster than you can.

**[1:05–1:15] Sight inventory and close.**
Silent writing, six minutes. Do not collect it. Give the take-home. Close with the reflection questions read aloud, slowly, without commentary.

# SESSION TWO — THE SETTLED YES

## Participant Guide

**Opening question:** What is the largest gift you have seriously considered and not made? What stopped it?

**Obedience is not compliance.** The word comes from *ob-audire* — to listen toward. Obedience is the posture of someone close enough to be told something specific. It is a relationship, not a rule.

**The formula problem.** A percentage answers the question in advance so you never have to ask it again. That is useful early and anesthetic later. The tithe is a floor. Most people never leave the floor because nobody told them there was a building above it.

**Three decisions that settle the yes.**

1. **Ownership, in writing.** Not a feeling — a document, signed and dated. *God owns this. My role is steward.*
2. **A finish line.** A number for your own lifestyle, set deliberately and revisited rarely. Everything above it has an obvious destination, and the annual argument ends.
3. **Cap the lifestyle, let the giving float.** Fix consumption rather than budgeting a giving percentage, and every future increase becomes a giving decision automatically.

**Alan and Katherine Barnhart.** Alan was twenty-four when he read Matthew 19 — the camel, the needle — and spent roughly two years working through hundreds of passages before changing anything. Then he and his brother capped their own incomes at the level of a ministry worker, in the first year of the business, when there was almost nothing to cap. Half of annual profits went out; half went back into the company. Fifty thousand dollars in year one, more than Alan's salary. Decades later, a million dollars a month. In 2007 they gave the company itself away.

Alan has said the first thing they settled was ownership: God owns the business, and his job is to be a steward.

**Exercise — name the number.** Write down the annual figure at which your household has enough. If you cannot write it, write down what makes it hard.

**Take home.** Tell one person the number, and the date you will revisit it. Not your advisor. A friend.

**Reflection**
1. What are you actually afraid of? Name it specifically — most people find it is not greed.
2. If your lifestyle number were fixed today, what would happen to every increase from here?
3. Who in your life is allowed to tell you no about money?

---

## Session Two — Teaching Script

**[0:00–0:10] Opening question in the full circle.**
Largest gift considered and not made. This one goes to the whole room, not pairs — the shared admission does the work. Expect silence for twenty seconds. Wait it out. Do not rescue it.

*Facilitator note:* The answers are almost always *timing*, *my spouse*, or *I wasn't sure*. Write the words on the board as they come. You will use them later.

**[0:10–0:20] Reclaim the word.**
*Obedience* lands badly on this room. Get ahead of it: *If obedience sounds like a demotion to you, that's fair — you've spent forty years being the person who decides.* Then the etymology: to listen toward. Obedience is proximity, not compliance.

**[0:20–0:35] The formula problem.**
Teach the tithe as a floor honestly and respectfully — it has done enormous good. Then the turn: *A percentage answers the question so you never have to ask again. That's useful at thirty and anesthetic at sixty.*

**[0:35–0:55] The three decisions.**
Teach each, briefly, with the practice attached. Ownership in writing. The finish line. Cap the lifestyle, float the giving.

Spend the most time on the finish line, because it is the practice with the strongest track record and the highest resistance. Frame it as freedom, never as restriction: *A ceiling is what ends the argument. A floor never has.*

**[0:55–1:05] Barnhart.**
Two years of study before any decision. The cap set when there was nothing to cap. Fifty thousand in year one. Give half, reinvest half — so the giving grows the company that grows the giving. Then 2007.

Land it here: *That's not a bigger heart than yours. That's a decision about ownership and a cap on consumption, made early, and then twenty-five years of arithmetic.*

**[1:05–1:15] Name the number.**
Silent, individual. Some will not be able to do it; say in advance that this is fine and instructive. Give the take-home. Close on the reflection questions.

*Facilitator note:* If a participant becomes visibly agitated during this exercise, do not pursue it in the room. Note it and make yourself available afterward. Fear surfaces here more than in any other session.

# SESSION THREE — WHERE THE CAPACITY IS

## Participant Guide

**Opening question:** What is the largest thing you own that you have never once considered giving?

**The checkbook is the smallest room in the house.** Roughly nine-tenths of American wealth sits outside cash — businesses, real estate, appreciated securities, equipment, land, intellectual property. Roughly all of the giving comes out of the checking account. That mismatch is not a character problem. It is an information problem, and it caps the size of every yes you will ever say.

**Four multipliers you control.**

- **What you give.** Gift the asset rather than the proceeds. Families who learn this frequently give on the order of ten times what they otherwise would have — same conviction, same year.
- **When you give.** Capital deployed at fifty-five works thirty years longer than capital deployed at eighty-five. A gift at death is a transaction. A gift during life is a partnership.
- **What you fund.** Some good work is consumed; some reproduces. A meal is eaten. A translator trained produces Scripture that produces churches that produce translators. Both are worth funding. Only one multiplies.
- **How fast you can move.** A family that needs eighteen months and three advisors to make a gift will miss most of what it is asked to do. Friction is not neutral — it quietly converts generosity into accumulation.

**Brent Haverkamp.** A family wanted to fund a college ministry project. The husband did the math carefully: to net the gift and cover the tax on the sale, he would need to sell two rental properties. Then he learned he could give one property to a charitable fund before selling it. The ministry received more than the two-property plan would have produced. He did not become more generous that afternoon. He became better informed.

**The sequence is the gift.** Give-then-sell and sell-then-give produce dramatically different outcomes from the same asset. The window closes earlier than most owners think — often before a letter of intent is signed.

**Exercise — the asset inventory.** List everything you own that is not cash. Circle what has appreciated. That circled list is your real giving capacity.

**Take home.** If you are within five years of any liquidity event, ask your advisor this week what could be given before rather than after.

**Reflection**
1. What did you circle that surprised you?
2. Where has your giving been sown into ground you have never actually seen?
3. How long does it currently take your household to say yes? Time it.

---

## Session Three — Teaching Script

**[0:00–0:08] Opening question, pairs.**
Largest thing never considered as a gift. Quick, energetic, no reporting out. This session runs faster than the other three by design — it is the practical one, and the energy should shift.

**[0:08–0:20] The mismatch.**
Teach the nine-tenths problem. Be concrete about the friction: valuations, appraisals, board approvals, an attorney who has never done this. Say plainly: *You are not less generous than you think. You have been operating with one instrument.*

**[0:20–0:50] The four multipliers.**
Roughly seven minutes each. Keep the arithmetic visible — this room believes numbers and distrusts adjectives.

On *what you fund*, use annuals and perennials rather than a hierarchy of worthiness. Both get planted. Only one comes back.

On *how fast*, ask the room how long their last significant gift took from conviction to transfer. The number embarrasses people usefully.

**[0:50–1:00] Haverkamp.**
Tell it with the actual math on the board — two properties versus one. This is the moment several people in the room will physically lean forward. Do not rush past it.

*Facilitator note:* This is where the no-ask rule earns its keep. Someone will ask how to do it. The answer is: *That's a real conversation and I'd be glad to have it — not here, and not today. Find me after and we'll set a time.* Then move on.

**[1:00–1:15] Asset inventory and close.**
Silent, eight minutes, individually — spouses may work together. Do not collect. Give the take-home about the advisor conversation. Close on the reflection questions.

# SESSION FOUR — THE ASSIGNMENT

## Participant Guide

**Opening question:** If your family finished something in your lifetime, what would you want it to be?

**Aim at an end, not at a curve.** Almost every organization offers you perpetual need. Almost none offers you a finish. A donor facing endless need gives defensively. A family with a finishable assignment gives like people with a deadline.

The Signatry states its own vision as an ending rather than a growth curve — the last check written for the last missionary sent to the last unreached people group, so the last person hears the gospel. Whatever you make of the scale of that, notice its shape. It has a finish.

**Name the soil.** In the parable of the sower the seed is identical in every case. What varies is the ground — and only the good ground returns thirty, sixty, or a hundredfold. The multiple depends on where you sow. Which means the central question was never *how much*. It was *where*.

**The family that sees together.** Children inherit what they helped decide. A family whose next generation has never researched a cause, argued about a grant, or met a recipient will inherit assets without the vision that made them meaningful. The annual meeting with real dollars the next generation allocates and defends is the mechanism, and it is not complicated. It is just rarely done.

**Exercise — the end-state sentence.** One sentence. No qualifiers. *When our family's work is done, ______ will be true.*

**Exercise — the ninety-day commitment.** Name one action with a date on it. Not a resolution. An action.

**Take home.** Put the family meeting on the calendar before you leave the room.

**Reflection**
1. What would have to be true for you to describe your giving as an assignment rather than a duty?
2. Who in your family should be in the room next time, and what has kept them out?
3. What is the first thing you will do, and when?

---

## Session Four — Teaching Script

**[0:00–0:10] Opening question, full circle.**
What would you want your family to finish? Let it run long. This is the emotional center of the day and it should not be hurried.

**[0:10–0:25] The finish.**
Teach the difference between perpetual need and a finishable assignment. Use The Signatry's vision as an example of the *shape*, and say explicitly that you are not recruiting anyone to it — you are showing them what an ending looks like.

*Facilitator note:* Some participants will find the scale of that vision implausible. Do not defend it. Say: *You may not believe it's achievable. Notice what it does to a family that believes it is.*

**[0:25–0:40] Name the soil.**
Read Matthew 13:1–9 aloud. Then make the turn most people have never heard: the seed is identical, the soil varies, and the multiple is a function of the ground. So the question moves from *how much* to *where*.

Connect it back to Session One: *This is why we started with what you can see. You cannot choose soil you have never visited.*

**[0:40–0:55] The family that sees together.**
Teach the annual meeting, the shared fund, and real authority for the next generation. Be specific enough to copy. Handle the "my kids aren't interested" objection directly — in almost every case it means they have never been given a real decision.

**[0:55–1:10] The two exercises.**
End-state sentence first, silently. Then the ninety-day commitment. Then, if the room is willing, invite three or four to read only their end-state sentence aloud. No discussion, no response, no applause. Just the sentences, one after another.

*Facilitator note:* This is the most powerful two minutes of the workshop when it works, and it fails badly if anyone comments on someone else's sentence. Say so before you start.

**[1:10–1:15] Close.**
No summary, no recap, no ask. Read the reflection questions. Then close in prayer, briefly — thanking God for what he owns and asking for the nerve to plant where he points.

# FACILITATOR NOTES

**Room design.** Round tables, no head table, no stage, no lectern. Nametags with first names only. No titles, no organizational affiliations, no capacity indicators of any kind.

**Who is in the room.** Advisors change the dynamic. If a participant's own advisor is present, most people will not name their number honestly. Either run an advisors-only cohort or seat advisors at separate tables and say so openly.

**What to do with silence.** Wait. This group is accustomed to filling air and will fill it if you don't. The best material in the workshop surfaces at second twenty-five.

**What to do if someone opens something heavy.** Money conversations reach grief, family estrangement, and shame faster than facilitators expect. Do not counsel in the room. Acknowledge, thank them, move gently on, and follow up privately.

**The one thing that ruins it.** Any hint of an ask. Including a soft one. Including a well-meant offer to help. Including a table tent with a QR code.

# EDITIONS

**Church edition.** Same four sessions, adapted for a congregation's high-capacity families with the senior pastor present but not teaching. Pairs naturally with a Journey of Generosity and should be positioned as formation rather than as a campaign precursor — if it precedes a capital campaign, participants will read the whole thing backward.

**Advisor cohort edition.** Same architecture, second-person shifted: the advisor is learning to raise these questions with clients rather than answering them for himself. Session Three carries the technical weight and expands.

**Family office edition.** Two half-days rather than four sessions, with the next generation present for Sessions One and Four and the whole family for all four where possible.

**Overnight edition.** Sessions One and Two on an evening, Three and Four the following morning — the format closest to a Journey of Generosity, and the one with the strongest track record for producing actual decisions.

**Extension.** The four sessions map directly onto the book's four parts, which means the workshop is the book's front door and the book is the workshop's follow-through. Neither needs to wait on the other to launch.

# OPEN ITEMS

1. **Co-branding.** Partner selection, logo lock-up, and who owns the curriculum IP. Recommend LifeTogether retains the curriculum and licenses it, with The Signatry as named co-publisher.
2. **Which title.** The workshop currently teaches the two-engine argument, which is the *Visionary Generosity* frame. If the book consolidates under a different title, the workshop name should follow it rather than compete with it.
3. **Facilitator certification.** Whether facilitators are trained and certified, or the guide ships open. Certification protects the no-ask rule; open shipping scales faster. Recommend certification for the first eighteen months.
4. **The story gap.** Three of the four anchor stories are American business owners. Source at least one at ordinary income and one from outside the United States, and place the ordinary-income story in Session One.
5. **Permissions.** Barnhart material through NCF and Generous Giving; Haverkamp through The Signatry. Both needed before any public facilitation.
