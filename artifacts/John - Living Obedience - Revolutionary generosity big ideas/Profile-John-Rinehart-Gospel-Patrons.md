# PROFILE: JOHN RINEHART AND *GOSPEL PATRONS*
### The closest competitor, and the most useful ally

**Prepared for the Visionary Generosity project · August 2026**

---

## Why this one first

Of the five competing books and ministries, Rinehart is the only one who is simultaneously your nearest rival and your most natural foreword. He occupies the exact narrative territory the book wants — the person behind the person — and he got there twelve years earlier. Every acquisitions editor who reads your proposal will ask about him, and the answer needs to be settled before the manuscript is drafted rather than after.

Handled badly, he is the reason a reviewer writes "*Gospel Patrons* with more chapters." Handled well, he is the reason the book reads as the next step in a conversation he started.

---

## What he actually published

*Gospel Patrons: People Whose Generosity Changed the World* came out in 2013, <cite index="131-1">self-published, running about 170 pages.</cite> It funded through Kickstarter, which tells you something useful about how he built his audience.

<cite index="133-1">The structure is six chapters: an opening, then Tyndale and Humphrey Monmouth, then Whitefield and Lady Huntingdon, then Newton and John Thornton with Wilberforce appearing alongside, then a chapter on what motivated historical patrons and how a reader becomes one, and a closing chapter on the calling itself.</cite>

The three pairings are chosen well and researched properly. <cite index="139-1">He worked through letters, long biographies, and academic articles, then retold the stories in plain modern prose.</cite> <cite index="139-1">Monmouth believed in Tyndale's work and put his money, his trade networks, and his influence behind it — at a cost of a year in the Tower of London on more than twenty charges.</cite> <cite index="135-1">Thornton placed Newton in an influential church and pushed him to publish the hymns, one of which was "Amazing Grace."</cite> <cite index="132-1">Huntingdon assembled her wealthy friends to hear Whitefield preach, because they would accept an invitation to her home when they would never stand in a field with commoners.</cite>

<cite index="131-1">His thesis extends past wealth: anyone can be a gospel patron, and the invitation is to find gospel leaders and come alongside them with whatever resources you have.</cite> <cite index="139-1">He grounds that in the parable of the talents — servants entrusted with a little as well as with much.</cite>

<cite index="138-1">He has since built gospelpatrons.org into an ongoing ministry with free articles and videos,</cite> <cite index="134-1">and describes his subject as the integral role business leaders have always played in God's kingdom.</cite> <cite index="134-1">He and his wife Renée have a son and a daughter from Ethiopia.</cite>

---

## What the reception says

Worth knowing honestly, because it maps the opening.

The praise is consistent: the stories are persuasive, the history holds up, and the reframing genuinely lands. <cite index="136-1">One reviewer working in eighteenth-century British history called the examples historically accurate and tied closely to the documented cycles of decline and revival.</cite>

The criticism is also consistent, and it is the more useful information. <cite index="137-1">Readers describe it as a fast, shallow read that whets the appetite without satisfying it — good stories that feel rushed, and less depth than the subject deserves.</cite> <cite index="136-1">A recurring complaint is that he spends significant space late in the book explaining the gospel itself, as though addressing readers who do not believe it,</cite> which several people found a change of register that lost them.

Read that as a map. The gap in his book is depth, mechanism, and contemporary evidence. Those are precisely the three things your book supplies.

---

## Where you overlap and where you don't

**The overlap is real and unavoidable.** Monmouth, Huntingdon, and Thornton currently anchor four chapters of the Visionary Generosity outline. Those are his three. Using them without acknowledgment would be both discourteous and strategically foolish, since anyone who has read him will notice in the first fifty pages.

**Three things your book does that his does not.**

*It pairs the historical with the living.* His patrons are all dead, which makes the stories inspiring and safely distant. Yours sit beside Barnhart, Green, and families whose numbers can be examined. That converts admiration into precedent.

*It supplies the mechanism.* His book tells a reader to become a patron. It does not tell a reader with nine-tenths of his wealth in a closely held business how. The asset chapters, the timing chapter, and the pre-exit checklist are the entire practical apparatus he leaves out — and without it, patronage is available only to people who already have liquidity.

*It argues a tension rather than a virtue.* His thesis is that patrons matter. Yours is that vision and obedience fail separately, which gives the book somewhere to go for three hundred pages and produces the empire chapter he has no reason to write.

## How I would approach him

**Lead with his mission, not yours.** He has spent a decade arguing that the church has forgotten the people who funded it. Your book extends that argument into a generation of families who have the capacity and no framework. Open there. Nothing about your platform, your co-author, or your publisher until he asks.

**Be specific about the debt.** Name the three pairings you use and say plainly that his book is why they are known at all. Offer to credit him in the introduction, not the endnotes.

**Bring the differentiation, unprompted.** Tell him what your book does that his doesn't before he has to wonder. It signals you have read him carefully and are not planning to reissue him.

**Ask for counsel before you ask for a foreword.** The first conversation should be about the historical material — what he learned, what he would have done differently, what he wishes someone would write next. A foreword offered after that conversation is a different thing than one requested in a cold email.

**Route it through a relationship.** He moves in overlapping circles with Generous Giving, NCF, and the faith-driven entrepreneur networks. Someone in your existing set almost certainly knows him. A warm introduction is worth more here than a good letter.

---

## Risks worth naming

**He may decline, and that is survivable.** A foreword from him is a strong signal, not a requirement. April Chapman remains the first choice on other grounds — she leads a competitor organization, which makes her endorsement the clearest possible evidence that this is a movement book rather than a house book.

**He may be writing something adjacent.** Twelve years and an active ministry is a long runway. Find out early rather than at manuscript delivery.

**Do not let him become the book's frame.** The temptation after a good conversation is to lean into the patron language and let the two-engine argument recede. The patron material is Part III of your book. It is the whole of his. Keeping that proportion is what makes yours a different book rather than a longer one.

---

## The one-line positioning

When an editor asks how this differs from *Gospel Patrons*: **he wrote the history; this is the argument, the mechanism, and the living evidence.** Say it in that order, and credit him first.
