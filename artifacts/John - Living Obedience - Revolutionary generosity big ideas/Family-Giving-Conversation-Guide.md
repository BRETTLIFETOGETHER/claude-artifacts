# THE FAMILY GIVING CONVERSATION
### A guide for the meeting most families never hold

**Companion to *Visionary Generosity* and the Two Engines workshop**
The Signatry with LifeTogether

---

## Why this is hard

Money conversations inside a family are rarely about money. They reach fairness, control, grief, and old comparisons faster than anyone expects, which is why most families avoid them and then hold the conversation for the first time in a lawyer's office after a funeral. By then the participants are grieving, the decisions are already made, and nobody can ask the person who made them why.

The alternative is not a difficult conversation. It is an ordinary one, held annually, about something concrete — real dollars, allocated by people who have to defend their reasoning. Children inherit what they helped decide. That is the entire principle, and everything below is machinery for it.

Expect the first meeting to be awkward. Expect the third to be the one that matters.

---

## Five ground rules

**One. This meeting is about giving, not about inheritance.** If inheritance comes up, note it and schedule a separate conversation. Blending them turns a formation exercise into a negotiation.

**Two. Everyone present has a real vote on real money.** Not an advisory opinion. If the next generation cannot actually move dollars, they will correctly identify the meeting as theater.

**Three. No one has to disclose their own finances.** Parents lead with their reasoning, not their balance sheet. Adult children are never asked what they earn.

**Four. Questions are allowed. All of them.** Including "why do we give to that?" and "why don't we just keep it?" A family that cannot ask those out loud has not actually had the conversation.

**Five. What is said here stays here.** Including from spouses who are not present, unless the family agrees otherwise.

---

## The first meeting — 90 minutes

**[0:00–0:10] Why we're doing this.** One parent, briefly, in their own words. Not a speech. Two or three minutes about why this matters and what you hope for. Then say plainly: *We may be bad at this the first time.*

**[0:10–0:25] The story.** Where the family's resources came from. The lean years, the risk, the person who worked a job nobody remembers. Tell the hard parts. Sanitized origin stories transmit nothing, and the next generation already suspects the sanitized version.

**[0:25–0:40] What we've been giving to, and why.** Walk through last year honestly, including the gifts made out of obligation, guilt, or a friendship. Naming those is what makes the meeting credible.

**[0:40–1:00] The allocation.** A defined amount — meaningful enough to matter, small enough that a mistake is survivable — divided among the participants. Each person researches and brings a recommendation to the next meeting. Assign it now.

**[1:00–1:15] What are we for?** Attempt one sentence together. You will not finish it today. Write down the best three attempts.

**[1:15–1:30] Close.** Set the next date before anyone leaves the room. Read a psalm or pray briefly. Do not summarize.

## Questions by seat

**For parents to answer out loud**
- What did you believe about money when you had none?
- What is the most generous thing you ever saw one of your parents do?
- What have you given that you regret, and what did it teach you?
- What are you afraid of financially, right now, that we don't know about?

**For adult children**
- What did you learn about money in this house that we never said?
- What do you think we care about? Are you sure?
- What would you fund if it were entirely your call?
- What do you wish we'd stop funding?

**For teenagers**
- Who around you has less than you, and does it bother you?
- If you had to give away a thousand dollars this month, where would it go, and how would you find out whether it worked?
- What's the difference between helping someone and fixing them?

**For spouses, privately, before the meeting**
- Are we actually agreed on the lifestyle number?
- Is there a gift you've wanted to make and haven't raised?
- Which of our children do we treat differently about money, and why?

## Giving real authority

The allocation exercise is the mechanism. Everything else is conversation.

Set an amount. Divide it. Each participant investigates one organization or one person, and brings back four things: what problem it addresses, who is doing the work, what would break if the funding stopped, and why this rather than something else. Fifteen minutes each at the next meeting, questions from the room, then the money moves.

Two rules make it work. The parents' recommendation gets questioned like everyone else's — if it doesn't, the meeting is a performance. And a recommendation the family declines still gets discussed respectfully; the person who researched it did real work, and how you treat a rejected proposal determines whether anyone brings a bold one next year.

Raise the amount as the family gets better at it. That escalation is itself formative.

## When it goes badly

**The disengaged child.** Almost always a child who has never been given a real decision. Give them one, alone, outside the meeting, with a smaller amount and no audience. Disengagement is usually a reasonable response to being consulted about something they cannot influence.

**The angry child.** Anger in these meetings is generally about something other than the giving — a comparison, an old decision, a sense that the family's money shaped their life without their consent. Do not defend. Ask what they mean and listen without correcting. The conversation may need to leave this room.

**The spouse who disagrees.** Stop. A gift made over a spouse's genuine objection costs more than it gives, every time. Disagreement between spouses on a large gift is information about timing, and it is worth taking seriously rather than overriding.

**In-laws.** Decide in advance and say it out loud rather than leaving it to be inferred. Either they are in the room or they are not, and both are defensible — but an unstated policy will be read as a verdict on a marriage.

**Nothing happens.** The most common outcome of a first meeting. Hold the second one anyway.

---

## The annual rhythm

**Once a year, same month.** Ninety minutes. Review what last year's allocations produced — including what didn't work, discussed without blame. Then a new allocation, larger than the last.

**Once every three years, longer.** Half a day. Revisit what the family is for. Revisit the lifestyle number. Bring in someone from outside to facilitate if the room has gotten polite.

**Once a decade, take a trip.** Go and stand where the money goes. Nothing you do in a conference room will accomplish what one visit does, and the person it changes most is usually the one who complained about going.

## The stones

After Israel crossed the Jordan, Joshua had them take twelve stones out of the riverbed and stack them where everyone could see. The reason given is startlingly practical: so that when children ask what these mean, someone will have to tell the story (Joshua 4:20–24).

Most families have no stones. There is no object, no date, no repeated telling — just assets that appear at the end without explanation.

Build something a child will ask about. A framed page. An annual meal on the anniversary of a decision. A photograph of a place the family funded. Then wait for the question, and answer it fully.
