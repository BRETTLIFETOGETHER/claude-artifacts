Section five · the moment

The pledge that changed philanthropy has a gap in it

In 2010 Bill Gates, Melinda French Gates and Warren Buffett invited the wealthiest families in the world to commit at least half of their wealth to charity. Roughly two hundred and fifty families across some thirty countries have signed. Each one writes a letter explaining why.

It is a moral commitment rather than a legal one, and it changed the conversation permanently. But fifteen years in, the honest reporting on it says two things. Most signatories are wealthier than when they signed. And the majority of the money that has moved has moved into private foundations and donor-advised funds rather than into the hands of the work.

The world's most successful philanthropic pledge asks families what they will give eventually. Almost nobody has asked them what they will live now.

What that leaves open

A commitment measured at death rather than during life. A promise about a percentage rather than a pattern. A signature from one person rather than a decision made by a family. And a structure in which the generosity is real but the formation never happens.

Why this house in particular

The Signatry holds the infrastructure that the parked money already sits in — which means it is uniquely positioned to be the organisation that helps families deploy rather than merely commit. Nobody else can make that argument from the inside.

Stated carefully: this is not a criticism of the Giving Pledge, which has done enormous good and moved the conversation further than anything before it. It is an observation about what it was never designed to do.

Section six · the idea underneath all of it

The Signatry means
the one who signs

Your vision is to write the last check. But the last check is only possible because somebody signed first — and this organisation has never asked anyone to sign anything.

The Living Pledge is not primarily a promise about how much a family gives. It is a commitment to intentionally live its faith, its generosity, its values, its purpose and its legacy now — while the generations are still together. Giving remains the catalytic behaviour. Family flourishing becomes the larger promise.

That distinction matters commercially as well as theologically. A pledge built on a percentage tells families who aren't ready that they don't belong, and tells families already there that you have nothing left to offer. A pledge built on a question includes everyone and never expires.

Live your faith.
Live your generosity.
Live your legacy.

The question the pledge actually asks

What do we want to live while we are living? What should our children learn? Which values should survive us? What is our wealth for? How much is enough? What story do we want the next generation to tell?

What the family receives

A private profile: here is where you are flourishing, here is where you may be vulnerable, here is where the generations do not agree, and here is the journey we would recommend next. That last line is what turns one book into a decade of engagement.

What The Signatry receives

The personalisation engine behind the entire library — and, over years, an anonymised body of data on how Christian families of significant means actually hand down what they hold. Nobody publishes that. It would be worth publishing.

One discipline built in from the start: never let the score become the goal. An assessment inside a book about surrender is one careless sentence away from turning obedience into a performance metric.

Section eight · where this sits

Complementary, not competitive

The field is full of good work. This fills a gap rather than duplicating one.
Formation
What is generosity doing in our hearts?

Generous Giving has spent twenty-five years proving that a room where nobody asks for money produces honesty no solicitation ever has. That work is the ground everything here stands on, and this proposal borrows its discipline rather than competing with it.

Collaboration
What happens when generous people deploy together?

Maclellan's Giving Together model reported 518 pledges and roughly $6.55 million mobilised across eight countries in 2025 alone — evidence that a pledge structure works, and that families will sign something when the something is worth signing.

Infrastructure
Where does the money actually go?

The Signatry, the National Christian Foundation and the wider donor-advised world hold the mechanism. That part is solved, and solved well.

The gap
Who is our family becoming?

Nobody owns the multigenerational formation experience that happens before, around and after the charitable transaction. That is what Living Obedience and the Living Pledge are for — and it is the only one of these four that is genuinely open.

What if The Signatry could help families not only give more effectively — but become stronger families because of their generosity?
