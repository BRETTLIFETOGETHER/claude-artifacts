# EXPONENTIAL GENEROSITY
### Expanded Chapter Architecture

**Status: developing.** Working title, unclaimed as of August 2026. 16 chapters, 4 parts, 65,000–75,000 words.

**The thesis in one sentence:** most Christian giving is linear — a dollar given is a dollar spent — and exponential generosity asks a different question of every gift: does this produce more?

**How to read this document.** Each chapter carries three to four movements. Every movement has a *why* — the reason it earns its place, not just its content. Each chapter then has an illustration bank: the anchor story, an alternate if permissions fail, an image or metaphor drawn from the reader's own world, the Scripture, the objection the chapter must answer, and the exercise.

**A note on the metaphors.** This reader allocates capital for a living. The images below borrow from that world deliberately — compounding, reinvestment rate, terminal value, dry powder, velocity. Used once each and never strained, they carry the argument further than another farming metaphor will. Used carelessly they turn the book into a business seminar. Rule of thumb: one financial image per chapter, and it must clarify rather than decorate.

# PART I — THE MATH WE NEVER DID

## 1. A Dollar Spent Is Not a Dollar Multiplied

**Premise:** Generous people plateau not because their hearts cool but because nobody ever showed them the difference between a gift that is consumed and a gift that reproduces.

**Movement one — The plateau is real and it is not a character flaw.**
*Why:* The reader has to be met without accusation in the first five pages or he closes the book. Opening with "you're not giving enough" loses him; opening with "you've been faithful and it stopped growing, and here's the mechanical reason" keeps him.

**Movement two — Consumed and reproduced are both good, but only one multiplies.**
*Why:* This is the distinction the whole book rests on, and it must be introduced without disparaging relief work. A meal eaten is a good thing. It is not the same kind of thing as a translator trained.

**Movement three — Most of us were taught to measure the input.**
*Why:* Percentage giving measures what leaves your hand. It says nothing about what happened next. Naming that gap early makes every later chapter feel like relief rather than rebuke.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* The Barnhart first year — fifty thousand dollars given away, more than Alan's own salary, out of a business run from two bedrooms. Start small on purpose; it disarms the reader who thinks this book is for billionaires.
- *Alternate:* A Signatry family whose first non-cash gift was physical goods rather than money.
- *Image:* Opex versus capex. Every owner knows the difference between money that keeps the lights on and money that builds capacity. Most giving is opex.
- *Scripture:* Matthew 25:14–30 — the servants are judged on what the money did, not on what they received.
- *Objection to answer:* "Are you saying feeding hungry people doesn't matter?" No. Say so directly, in this chapter, in plain words.
- *Exercise:* List last year's ten largest gifts. Mark each C or R.

## 2. The Hundredfold

**Premise:** Jesus talks about giving in the language of return more often than in the language of loss, and the return schedule in the parable of the sower is doing something we have missed.

**Movement one — The seed is identical; the soil varies.**
*Why:* This is the book's original exegetical contribution. Thirty, sixty, and a hundredfold are not degrees of piety — they are a function of where the seed lands. Which means the giving question shifts from *how much* to *where*.

**Movement two — Return language is the Bible's own, not a marketing softening.**
*Why:* Christian readers are rightly suspicious of anything that sounds like investment. Establish the vocabulary from the text before applying it, or the chapter reads as accommodation.

**Movement three — Bob Buford built a life on this and it cost him everything it could cost a man.**
*Why:* Buford is both the strongest claim to this material and the necessary corrective — his hundredfold conviction coexisted with the loss of his only son. Pairing them here inoculates the whole book against prosperity drift.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* Buford, and the decision to fund leaders rather than programs — the choice that made Leadership Network a multiplier rather than a recipient.
- *Alternate:* Humphrey Monmouth funding Tyndale — one merchant's money behind every English Bible since.
- *Image:* The reinvestment rate. Two businesses with identical revenue and different reinvestment rates diverge violently over twenty years.
- *Scripture:* Matthew 13:1–23; Luke 8:11–15.
- *Objection:* "This is prosperity teaching in a suit." Answer it in the chapter, not the appendix.
- *Exercise:* Name one gift from the last five years and try to trace what it produced. Most readers can't. That's the point.

## 3. We're Counting the Wrong Things

**Premise:** Every institution in this field reports what it holds and what it raised. Almost none reports what multiplied.

**Movement one — Dollars raised is a fundraising metric masquerading as a Kingdom metric.**
*Why:* It measures the persuasiveness of the asker, not the fruitfulness of the gift. Say it plainly and the reader will recognize a discomfort he's felt for years.

**Movement two — Velocity is a better number than balance.**
*Why:* A charitable fund that grows while granting slowly has optimized for the wrong variable. The Signatry publishing a grant rate rather than assets under management is the working example, and it lets the authors make the argument against their own industry.

**Movement three — What a household could measure instead.**
*Why:* Critique without a replacement metric is just complaining. Give the reader four numbers he can actually track.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* The industry's own reporting conventions, examined honestly — including where The Signatry's numbers help the argument and where they don't.
- *Image:* Velocity of money. Economists don't just count the currency, they count how fast it moves. The same dollar can do the work of five.
- *Scripture:* Luke 16:1–13 — commendation for shrewd deployment, not for accumulation.
- *Objection:* "You can't measure Kingdom fruit." Partly true. Answer: you can measure whether you tried.
- *Exercise:* Calculate your household's outflow rate — dollars granted divided by dollars held — and write it down.

# PART II — THE SEVEN MULTIPLIERS

## 4. Give the Thing, Not the Proceeds *(the asset multiplier)*

**Premise:** Roughly nine-tenths of wealth sits outside cash, and roughly all giving comes out of the checking account.

**Movement one — The bottleneck is competence, not character.**
*Why:* Absolves the reader of guilt he doesn't owe and reframes the problem as solvable this quarter.

**Movement two — The sequence is the entire gift.**
*Why:* Give-then-sell and sell-then-give produce dramatically different outcomes from identical assets. Teach it once, mechanically, with real numbers.

**Movement three — What blocks it: valuations, boards, attorneys who've never done this.**
*Why:* Naming the friction is what makes the reader believe the authors have actually done this rather than read about it.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* Brent Haverkamp — needing to sell two rental properties to net the gift plus cover the tax, then giving one property before the sale instead.
- *Alternate:* David and Casey Trogden gifting private business stock ahead of a sale.
- *Image:* A leak in the pipe. The water leaving the reservoir and the water arriving at the field are different volumes, and the difference is pure loss.
- *Scripture:* Deuteronomy 26:1–11 — firstfruits are the crop itself, not currency converted from it.
- *Objection:* "This is just tax strategy dressed up." Answer: the tax outcome is real and secondary; the point is the size of the yes it makes possible.
- *Exercise:* The asset inventory. List everything illiquid. Circle what has appreciated.

## 5. Thirty More Years *(the timing multiplier)*

**Premise:** Capital deployed at fifty-five works for three decades longer than capital deployed at eighty-five.

**Movement one — A gift at death is a transaction; a gift during life is a partnership.**
*Why:* Establishes that timing is not a technicality but a difference in kind. You get to correct mistakes, learn what you fund well, and be present for the outcome.

**Movement two — Your children learn from what they watch, not from what they inherit.**
*Why:* The strongest emotional argument in the chapter and the bridge to Part III.

**Movement three — Why people wait, honestly examined.**
*Why:* They wait out of fear of running out, not out of greed. Treating the delay as a fear problem rather than a stinginess problem is what makes the chapter land.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* David Green's decision to move ownership during his lifetime rather than leaving the question to an estate plan.
- *Alternate:* A family that gave at death and a family that gave at sixty, told side by side.
- *Image:* The compounding table every reader has seen — the one where starting ten years earlier beats contributing twice as much.
- *Scripture:* Luke 12:16–21; Psalm 90:12.
- *Objection:* "I need the assets for my own security." Take it seriously; answer with the finish line rather than with shame.
- *Exercise:* Model one planned bequest as if it were given this year. Just look at the number.

## 6. Return Measured in Givers *(the generational multiplier)*

**Premise:** The highest return on a gift is not more dollars. It is more givers.

**Movement one — One generous household can produce four, or none.**
*Why:* Reframes succession as the central generosity question rather than a legal one.

**Movement two — Transmission requires participation, not instruction.**
*Why:* Children inherit what they helped decide. A lecture at Thanksgiving transmits nothing.

**Movement three — The fourth-generation problem.**
*Why:* Every wealthy family knows the shirtsleeves proverb. Show that generosity is the most reliable known antidote, and the chapter becomes urgent rather than aspirational.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* A family practicing generosity across four generations since 1965, with the specific practices that carried it.
- *Alternate:* The Phillips family's four-generation account.
- *Image:* Reinvestment rate again, applied to people — what percentage of your giving produces another giver?
- *Scripture:* Psalm 78:4–7; 2 Timothy 1:5.
- *Objection:* "My kids aren't interested." Usually means they've never been given authority over real money.
- *Exercise:* Give each adult child a real allocation this year and require them to defend it.

## 7. Fund What Reproduces *(the reproduction multiplier)*

**Premise:** Some good work is consumed and some good work reproduces, and the difference is the largest available lever.

**Movement one — Annuals and perennials.**
*Why:* Gives the reader a usable sorting instinct rather than a rule. Both get planted; only one comes back.

**Movement two — Fund the person who trains the trainer.**
*Why:* The multiplication logic that transformed church planting has barely touched philanthropy. This is where Exponential.org's argument gets applied one category over.

**Movement three — The unglamorous middle.**
*Why:* Launches attract money; year seven of a translation project does not. Patience is the patron's structural advantage over institutional grantmaking.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* The Barnhart giving group — dozens of employees and spouses who research recipients and look for the best people working on a problem rather than the strongest development departments.
- *Alternate:* Buford funding leaders instead of programs.
- *Image:* A well versus a well-driller. Fund one village or fund the man who can reach forty.
- *Scripture:* 2 Timothy 2:2 — the four-generation transmission verse, and the clearest multiplication instruction in the New Testament.
- *Objection:* "So relief work is second-class?" No — but most donors fund only relief because it's the only thing that gets presented to them.
- *Exercise:* Sort your giving list into annuals and perennials. Notice the ratio.

## 8. Caught, Not Taught *(the contagion multiplier)*

**Premise:** Generosity spreads by exposure to a generous person, not by exposure to an argument.

**Movement one — Nobody was argued into this.**
*Why:* Every story in the book traces back to a room, a trip, or a person — not to a sermon. Naming that pattern makes the reader ask who he's near.

**Movement two — Visibility versus vanity.**
*Why:* The chapter has an obvious tension with anonymity, and it has to be resolved rather than ignored. Resolution: tell the decision, not the number; make the reasoning public and the amount private.

**Movement three — The first follower.**
*Why:* One giver alone is an eccentric. The second one makes it a movement. This is why peer environments outperform campaigns.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* The Journey of Generosity — a room with no ask, and why the absence of solicitation is what produces the honesty.
- *Alternate:* Generous Giving privately funded since 2000 precisely so it never has to ask.
- *Image:* The first follower, not the virus. Post-2020, contagion metaphors carry baggage this chapter doesn't need.
- *Scripture:* 2 Corinthians 8:1–7 — Paul using one church's generosity to provoke another's.
- *Objection:* "Isn't this just peer pressure?" Distinguish provocation from manipulation, using Paul's own method.
- *Exercise:* Tell one person the reasoning behind a gift you made. Not the amount.

## 9. What Pooled Capital Can Do *(the collaboration multiplier)*

**Premise:** Some problems cannot be funded by any single family, and solo philanthropy quietly limits vision to what one checkbook can reach.

**Movement one — Loneliness, not stinginess, is the more common condition.**
*Why:* Wealthy Christians are handled constantly and rarely accompanied. The chapter's emotional entry point.

**Movement two — A table asks better questions than an individual.**
*Why:* Peer review improves giving the way it improves investing. Nobody funds their worst idea in front of five peers.

**Movement three — What collaboration costs.**
*Why:* Shared credit, slower decisions, compromise on approach. Honest accounting keeps the chapter from reading as a brochure for convening organizations.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* The Gathering's convening model and what it changed for families who joined it.
- *Alternate:* A collaborative fund that solved something no member could have funded alone.
- *Image:* Syndication. No serious allocator does every deal alone.
- *Scripture:* Proverbs 27:17; Ecclesiastes 4:9–12.
- *Objection:* "I don't want a committee running my giving." Fair. Distinguish a table from a board.
- *Exercise:* Identify six to twelve peers. Put a date on the calendar.

## 10. The Return You Won't See *(the eternal multiplier)*

**Premise:** The largest portion of the return is beyond the horizon, which is exactly what makes it hard to hold and impossible to fake.

**Movement one — Terminal value.**
*Why:* In any discounted cash flow, most of the value sits beyond the forecast period, and every allocator has quietly accepted a number he cannot verify. That is the most honest available analogy for eternal reward, and it will land harder with this reader than any sermon illustration.

**Movement two — The restraint the doctrine requires.**
*Why:* Eternal reward is real and it is not a transaction. The chapter must refuse to specify the exchange rate.

**Movement three — Treasure sent ahead.**
*Why:* Alcorn's contribution, engaged directly and credited, because every reader has encountered it and the book should build on it rather than around it.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* Stanley Tam making God the legal owner of the company in 1936 and becoming its employee — an allocation decision made entirely on unseen return.
- *Alternate:* LeTourneau pledging again from underneath a hundred-thousand-dollar loss.
- *Image:* Terminal value — the largest and least certain number in every model anyone here has ever built.
- *Scripture:* Matthew 6:19–21; 1 Timothy 6:17–19; 2 Corinthians 4:18.
- *Objection:* "Isn't giving for reward a lower motive?" Engage it honestly; Scripture offers the reward without embarrassment.
- *Exercise:* Write the sentence you would want read at your funeral about what you did with what you were given.

# PART III — BUILDING THE ENGINE

## 11. The Family Engine

**Premise:** A family is either a machine that produces givers or a machine that produces heirs, and the difference is designed, not hoped for.

**Movement one — Purpose statement before allocation policy.**
*Why:* Families argue about money when they have never agreed on what the money is for.

**Movement two — The meeting, the fund, the vote.**
*Why:* Three concrete mechanisms, described specifically enough to copy.

**Movement three — What to do about the child who isn't interested.**
*Why:* Every family has one, and its absence from most books is why they feel unreal.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* The Davis family's practices across four generations.
- *Image:* Governance. Every owner knows a business without governance eventually consumes itself.
- *Scripture:* Joshua 4:20–24 — the stones exist so children will ask.
- *Objection:* "This will create conflict." It will surface conflict that already exists. Better now than at the reading of the will.
- *Exercise:* Schedule the first family meeting and give it one real decision.

## 12. The Business Engine

**Premise:** A company can be a one-time gift or a perpetual funding instrument, and the choice is structural.

**Movement one — Give half, reinvest half.**
*Why:* The Barnhart model stated as a mechanism, not a testimony — this is the chapter's teachable core.

**Movement two — Ownership transfer as the terminal move.**
*Why:* Green, Barnhart, and Tam all arrived here independently. Three roads, one destination, worth showing as a pattern.

**Movement three — The exit window.**
*Why:* The most consequential eighteen months in an owner's financial life, and the one nobody prepares him for spiritually.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* Barnhart Crane — fifty thousand in year one becoming a million a month, then the company itself into a trust in 2007.
- *Alternate:* Green moving Hobby Lobby's voting stock beyond the family's reach.
- *Image:* Dry powder. Capacity held ready for a moment you can't schedule.
- *Scripture:* Luke 12:42–48.
- *Objection:* "My business isn't big enough for this to matter." The Barnharts started at ten employees.
- *Exercise:* The pre-exit checklist — eight questions before any letter of intent.

## 13. The Church Engine

**Premise:** Most churches produce donors. A few produce givers. The difference shows up a decade later.

**Movement one — Generosity as discipleship, not budget management.**
*Why:* Congregations trained to hear every mention of money as an ask defend themselves accordingly.

**Movement two — What a church owes its wealthy families.**
*Why:* Underserved and over-solicited at the same time. This is where the Journey of Generosity belongs in a local congregation.

**Movement three — Preaching money in a year with no campaign.**
*Why:* The single most credible act available to a pastor on this subject.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* A church that ran generosity teaching with nothing to fund, and what happened to giving afterward.
- *Image:* Compounding again, applied to a congregation's giving culture over a decade.
- *Scripture:* 2 Corinthians 9:6–15.
- *Objection:* Pastors will hear this as a critique. Write it as an invitation, and let a pastor read the draft.
- *Exercise:* For pastors — one generosity series, no ask attached.

## 14. The Fund Engine

**Premise:** A charitable fund is either a deployment instrument or a savings account with better tax treatment.

**Movement one — Velocity as the governing metric.**
*Why:* Returns to chapter 3 and turns critique into operating policy.

**Movement two — Friction is not neutral.**
*Why:* Slow structures silently convert generosity into accumulation. This is the strongest argument in the book for design mattering spiritually.

**Movement three — Speed as obedience.**
*Why:* A family that needs eighteen months to say yes will miss most of what it's asked to do.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* A same-week gift made possible by structure built years earlier.
- *Image:* Settlement time. Every allocator knows the difference between having capital and having it available.
- *Scripture:* Proverbs 3:27–28.
- *Objection:* "Aren't you selling your own product?" This is the chapter where the authors are most exposed. Keep all vehicle mechanics in the appendix and let the principle stand alone.
- *Exercise:* Time your own giving process end to end. Most families have never measured it.

# PART IV — THE HUNDREDFOLD

## 15. When It Compounds

**Premise:** Three families, thirty years, and what actually happened — told with numbers rather than adjectives.

**Movement one — The full arc of one family, start to present.**
*Why:* The book has been arguing in parts; this chapter shows the whole thing running at once.

**Movement two — What went wrong along the way.**
*Why:* A chapter of unbroken success would undo the credibility of the previous fourteen.

**Movement three — What none of them predicted.**
*Why:* Sets up the final chapter by showing that the outcome exceeded the plan every time.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* Barnhart, Green, and one ordinary-income family — the third one is the most important and the hardest to source.
- *Image:* The curve itself, shown once, as the book's only chart.
- *Scripture:* 1 Corinthians 3:6–8.
- *Objection:* "These are extraordinary people." Answer with the ordinary-income family, or the objection stands.
- *Exercise:* Draw your own thirty-year curve. Mark where you are on it.

## 16. The Finish

**Premise:** Aim at an end rather than a growth curve.

**Movement one — Perpetual need versus a finishable assignment.**
*Why:* Almost nobody offers high-capacity families a finish, and the scarcity of it is the whole opportunity.

**Movement two — Working backward from the end.**
*Why:* Converts vision into this year's number, which is the only way a final chapter earns its keep.

**Movement three — The last check.**
*Why:* The Signatry's vision of a finish — the final check for the final missionary to the final unreached people group — is the largest available frame, and it makes Matthew 24:14 a funding statement rather than a prophecy chart.

**Illustration bank**
- *Anchor:* The vision statement itself, and what it does to a family that adopts it.
- *Image:* Retire the metaphors here. The last chapter should be plain.
- *Scripture:* Matthew 24:14; Revelation 7:9; 2 Timothy 4:7.
- *Objection:* "Isn't a finish line naive?" Engage it. The honest answer involves theological humility about timing.
- *Exercise:* Write the family's end-state sentence. One sentence, no qualifiers.

# ILLUSTRATION STRATEGY

**Rotate the story types.** Four kinds are in play — historical patrons, mid-century industrialists, living American families, and global givers. No two consecutive chapters should lead with the same type, or the book develops a monotone. Current draft leans heavily on living American business owners; that is the gap to close.

**One financial metaphor per chapter, maximum.** They are potent with this reader and they cheapen fast. Assigned above: opex/capex, reinvestment rate, velocity, the leak, compounding, well-driller, first follower, syndication, terminal value, governance, dry powder, settlement time. Chapter 16 gets none on purpose.

**Every chapter answers an objection.** Listed above per chapter. This is what separates a book from a brochure, and it is the discipline most books in this category skip.

**Every chapter ends in an exercise, not an exhortation.** Sixteen exercises become the study guide and the workbook without additional writing.

**The gap that still needs closing.** Twelve of the current anchors are American business owners. Source at least four stories at ordinary income levels and three from outside the United States, and place the first one no later than chapter 2 — otherwise the book proves that exponential generosity is what happens to people who sell companies, which is a much smaller claim than the cover makes.
