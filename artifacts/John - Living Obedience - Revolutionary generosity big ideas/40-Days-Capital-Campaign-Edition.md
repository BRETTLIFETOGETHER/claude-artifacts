# 40 DAYS OF GENEROSITY
## The Capital Campaign Edition

**A two-phase architecture for churches raising money**
LifeTogether · Status: developing, August 2026

---

# THE PROBLEM THIS EDITION HAS TO SOLVE

Every other edition rests on one promise: nobody is asked for anything. That constraint is the product. It is why people talk honestly in groups, why they tell their friends, and why the material works on families who have spent their whole lives being solicited.

A capital campaign is an ask. A large, public, sustained one.

You cannot run both in the same six weeks and tell the truth. A church that preaches formation for five weeks and hands out pledge cards in week six has not integrated two things — it has revealed that the first five weeks were a setup, and the congregation will conclude exactly that. The damage isn't to the campaign. It's to every future word that church says about money.

So this edition does not blend them. **It sequences them.**

---

# THE ARCHITECTURE

## Phase One · Formation — six weeks, no ask

The standard 40 Days of Generosity. Weekend, groups, daily reader, children's track. The covenant is stated in week one and held through week six. No pledge card, no vision presentation, no capital language of any kind.

**What the church says out loud in week one:** *We are not asking you for anything during these six weeks. Later this year we will talk with you about a building. Today is not that, and I will not blur the two.*

Naming the future ask is what protects the present formation. A congregation that knows the campaign is coming and watches you refuse to touch it for six weeks will trust you completely when you finally do.

## The Gap · ninety days minimum

This is the part churches will want to cut and must not.

Ninety days does three things. It proves the covenant was real. It gives the practices time to take — a family that set a finish line in March is a different donor in September than they were in March. And it separates the two seasons cleanly enough in the congregation's memory that the formation isn't retroactively reinterpreted.

Under sixty days, don't bother sequencing. Just run an honest capital campaign and skip the formation edition entirely.

## Phase Two · Commitment — four weeks, an honest ask

Short, direct, and openly what it is. Four weekends, a vision presentation, an asset track, and a commitment weekend.

**The covenant changes, and you say so.** *For six weeks last spring we didn't ask you for anything. Today I'm going to ask. That's the difference between those weeks and these, and I want you to hear me name it.*

---

# WHY THIS OUTPERFORMS THE USUAL MODEL

A typical church capital campaign asks people to decide two things simultaneously — whether they believe in the project, and what they believe about their own money. Most people have never worked out the second one, so they default to a number that feels safe, which is usually a percentage of what they gave last year.

Sequencing separates the decisions. By the time the vision is presented, a meaningful share of the congregation has already settled ownership, named a lifestyle number, walked through their fear, and had one conversation with their spouse. They aren't deciding what they believe about money in the lobby with a pen. They decided in March.

The second reason is mechanical and larger.

---

# THE ASSET TRACK — where capital campaigns leave the money

Almost every church capital campaign asks for three-year cash pledges. Almost none asks for anything else.

That is a serious error. Roughly nine-tenths of household wealth in your congregation sits outside cash — closely held business interests, appreciated stock, real estate, equipment, farmland. A family that would strain to pledge sixty thousand in cash over three years may be able to give a rental property outright without touching their income at all.

**What the asset track adds to phase two.**

*A named advisor liaison.* One person — often a member who is a CPA, attorney, or financial advisor — who can take a call and knows the sequence.

*One evening seminar, three weeks before commitment weekend.* Not a sermon. A room where someone explains the difference between giving the asset and giving the proceeds, with real arithmetic on a screen.

*The sequence rule, taught explicitly.* An asset given before it is sold produces materially more for the church than the same asset sold and the proceeds donated. The window closes when the transaction is papered.

*The pre-exit question, asked once from the platform.* If you are within five years of selling a business, come talk to us before you sign anything — not because we want it, but because you will otherwise lose the option.

*A clear disclaimer.* The church is not giving tax or legal advice, and every family works through their own counsel.

This single addition typically matters more to a campaign's outcome than the entire promotional budget.

# PHASE TWO — THE FOUR WEEKS

**Week One · The Vision.** What the church intends to do and why it matters. Told through the people it will serve, not through square footage. No numbers this week.

**Week Two · The Cost.** The full number, the timeline, and what happens if the church falls short. Honesty here buys more than optimism does. This is also the week the asset seminar is announced.

**Week Three · The Testimony.** Someone in the congregation who has already decided, telling why — no amount named. Ideally a household of ordinary means, because a wealthy testimony this week tells everyone else their part is small.

**Week Four · Commitment Weekend.** Cards, prayer, and the ask made plainly and once. Do not ask twice. Do not read the total from the platform the following Sunday.

---

# THE EIGHTEEN-MONTH CALENDAR

| Month | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Leadership decides; covenant agreed in writing; dates set |
| 3 | Host recruitment and training |
| 4–5 | **Phase One** — six weeks of formation, no ask |
| 6–8 | **The gap.** Nothing capital-related said from the platform |
| 7 | Quiet phase: leadership and major-gift conversations begin privately |
| 9 | Asset seminar |
| 9–10 | **Phase Two** — four weeks, commitment weekend at the end |
| 11–18 | Fulfillment, reporting, and the follow-through the church promised |

# WHAT CARRIES OVER, AND WHAT DOESN'T

**Carries over.** The finish line practice. The asset inventory. The family conversation. The ownership statement. These are the reason the second phase performs — people are giving out of a settled framework rather than a momentary appeal.

**Does not carry over.** The no-ask covenant, and you must say so rather than let it quietly lapse. The campaign name should also change; running phase two under the formation campaign's brand is precisely the blur this whole architecture exists to prevent.

**Never carries over.** The group sessions. Do not turn a formation group into a campaign team. The people in those rooms told you things under a promise, and converting the room converts the promise into a technique.

---

# WHAT WILL GO WRONG

**Somebody will propose collapsing the gap.** Usually a consultant, usually citing momentum. The answer is that momentum built on a broken promise costs more than it raises.

**Somebody will want a soft ask in week five of phase one.** Just a card. Just an interest form. No.

**The formation campaign will be judged by the capital result.** Guard against it early by agreeing with your board, in writing, that phase one is not measured in dollars.

**The asset track will be under-resourced.** It looks like a niche add-on and it is where the largest gifts live. Staff it.

**A major donor will be approached during phase one.** Leadership must know the gap applies to private conversations too, or the covenant is a public fiction.

---

# THE HONEST ALTERNATIVE

If your church cannot wait ninety days, do not run this edition. Run a straightforward capital campaign, do it well, and use the 40 Days of Generosity edition next year in a season when you need nothing.

That is not a lesser option. A church that asks honestly and directly has done nothing wrong. The only unrecoverable move is to promise formation and deliver an appeal, and no amount of good design fixes that.
