# Day 6

## In-Laws or the Out-Laws

### The answer is absolutely yes

**"Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother, and be joined to his wife; and they shall become one flesh."** GENESIS 2:24

People ask me a version of the same question every time.

Do you really want the in-laws, or the out-laws, in the meeting?

And the answer is absolutely yes.

I say it with a smile because the phrase is a joke families make about themselves, and underneath the joke is a real question about who belongs in the room.

Here is the practical reason, and it is not sentimental.

If they are not there, your son or daughter is going home and telling them what happened. That retelling is going to happen whether you invite them or not. And I guarantee you that what gets told is not identical to what was said.

Not because anybody is dishonest. Because a person recounting a two-hour conversation from memory, filtered through their own reaction, at the end of a long day, produces a version. Then the spouse forms an opinion from that version and holds it for years.

So you can have everyone hear the same thing at the same time, or you can have a set of secondhand accounts circulating in households you are not in.

There is a second reason that matters more over time.

The person who married in came from a different house with a different set of values, and they brought all of it with them. That is not a problem to be managed. It is information your family needs, and in most families nobody has ever asked for it.

I have watched a daughter-in-law say the thing everybody in the room recognized immediately as true, in year nine, the first time anyone asked her what she thought.

Genesis says a man leaves his father and mother and is joined to his wife. It is describing the formation of something new rather than an addition to something existing.

Which is exactly the thing families get wrong here. The new household is not a branch of yours. It is its own, and treating it as a branch is what produces the polite distance so many families mistake for peace.

So invite them. And then actually ask them something.

# Day 7

## Not Every Conversation Is the Same

### Using the same type of meeting for everything

**"There is an appointed time for everything, and there is a time for every matter under heaven."** ECCLESIASTES 3:1

One of the most common problems is using the same kind of meeting for everything.

Not every conversation is the same, and there are generally four kinds.

**A learning meeting.** You are teaching people how something works. Maybe how a trust functions in your family. The goal is understanding, because at some point they are going to be responsible for it.

**An alignment meeting.** People are sharing perspectives. You want everybody's input, so there is real ownership in whatever comes next.

**A decision-making meeting.** We have a lake house. We are thinking about selling it. What do you think? And one person says do not sell it, I love waterskiing. And another says I would rather have a place in the mountains, I never go there anyway.

**A long-term planning meeting.** Here is where we are, here is what we are considering, and we want everybody to speak into it.

Those four require different preparation, different length, and different expectations, and families run all of them the same way.

Here is what happens when the format does not match the purpose.

A learning meeting run as a decision meeting produces premature votes on something nobody understands yet.

A decision meeting run as an alignment meeting never decides anything, and everyone leaves frustrated without being able to say why.

An alignment meeting run as a learning meeting is a lecture, and the people whose input you wanted have concluded their input was not actually wanted.

The frustration in each case is real, and it gets misattributed to the family rather than to the format.

Ecclesiastes says there is an appointed time for every matter. It is usually read as comfort about seasons of life, and it is also a plain statement that different things belong in different moments.

So before your next gathering, name which of the four it is. Out loud, at the start, to everyone.

That one sentence does more for a family meeting than any amount of preparation.

# Day 8

## It's the China and the Guns

### Most family fights are not over the money

**"An inheritance gained hurriedly at the beginning will not be blessed in the end."** PROVERBS 20:21

Here is something that surprises families, and it should not.

Most family fights are not over the money. They are over the china. They are over the jewelry. They are over the guns, especially with the men. The cars.

I had a client with a gun room. Thirty-five guns. And I told him he needed to decide which rifle goes to which grandchild before they fight about it after he is gone.

I have another client with three Rolls-Royces. Somebody has to make a decision about those, and if he does not, three people will make it for him under conditions nobody would choose.

The reason this happens is not greed. It is that these objects carry meaning that money does not.

Money is fungible. A dollar is identical to another dollar, and a family can divide it without anybody feeling that something specific was taken from them.

A grandfather's watch cannot be divided. There is one, it went to somebody, and the person it did not go to has received information about their place in the family whether or not any was intended.

Brett has his own version of this. He had an uncle who took him hunting as a boy, and that uncle had a Browning twelve-gauge, and Brett would have loved to have it. He never had the heart to ask. His uncle passed, the gun went to his aunt, and he still feels awkward about it.

It was never about the gun.

That is the thing to understand. These objects are proxies for something, and because they are proxies, the conflict about them is never proportionate to their value and always proportionate to what they represent.

Proverbs says an inheritance gained hurriedly will not be blessed. The word hurriedly describes a pace, and there is nothing more hurried than a family dividing personal effects in the week after a funeral.

The remedy is unglamorous and completely effective. Decide now. Write it down. Tell people.

You will be amazed how much conflict never happens.

# Day 9

## Two Votes Out of Ten

### So they couldn't control what was going on

**"In humility consider one another as more important than yourselves."** PHILIPPIANS 2:3

I ran a meeting with a family of ten. Mom and dad, four children, and all four spouses.

Dad said something at the start that I have thought about ever since. He said it is going to go much better if you facilitate this, because if I do it, they are going to think I am just telling them what I want.

So we voted on things. And mom and dad had two votes out of ten.

They could not control the outcome. And the children were vastly more engaged, because they were making a decision as a family rather than being asked to ratify one that had already been made.

Now, I want to be careful here, because this can be misread as a technique for diluting parental authority, and that is not what it is.

Nothing was taken from those parents. They still had every advantage in the room. Thirty years more experience, the moral weight of being the ones who built it, and the simple fact that everyone was there because they had asked.

What the arrangement removed was the assumption. Without it, every child in that room would have spent the meeting trying to work out what dad wanted, which is what children of every age do, and the conversation would have been a very elaborate way of arriving at his opinion.

Here is the outcome that matters. The children did not decide anything mom and dad disagreed with.

They had lived in this culture their whole lives. What came out was recognizably the family's own values, in the children's words, which is the only version that transfers.

There is a version of this available to any family without a facilitator. Somebody other than the person who usually decides holds the conversation. Or you take an actual vote. Or you simply go around the room and let the person with the most authority speak last.

That last one costs nothing and changes almost everything.

Paul says in humility consider others more important than yourselves. It is not a call to pretend you know less. It is a call to arrange things so that other people can actually be heard.

Two votes out of ten is what that looks like on a Tuesday.

# Day 10

## Is the Next Steward Prepared?

### How are they going to be prepared if they don't even know?

**"It is required of stewards that one be found trustworthy."** 1 CORINTHIANS 4:2

Ron Blue used to ask a question that settles most of the debate about how much to tell your family.

Is the next steward prepared?

And then the follow-up, which is the part that ends the argument. How are they going to be prepared if they do not even know what they are going to be responsible for?

I raise it here because the most common question I get about family meetings is whether to have them with numbers or without.

Both are legitimate. I have run them both ways. Sometimes mom and dad are not sure they want the children to know what exists, because it has grown to be a significant number and they do not feel the children are ready for it.

That is a real concern and it deserves respect rather than dismissal.

But hold it against Ron's question. If readiness is the goal, and readiness requires preparation, and preparation requires knowing what you are being prepared for, then indefinite withholding is not a neutral position. It is a decision to not prepare somebody.

Here is what I have seen when families do tell.

The reaction is almost never entitlement. That is what parents fear and it is not what happens. What happens is that people get serious. Something that was abstract becomes real, and a person who was casually uninterested starts paying attention in a way they never did before.

I did watch one family where the reveal produced fear rather than excitement in a son-in-law, and it was worth understanding why. He was not overwhelmed by the amount. He was thinking about his own children and what it would do to them, which was a more mature reaction than anyone expected.

So the question is not whether to tell. It is when, and in what order, and with what preparation attached.

Paul says it is required of stewards that one be found trustworthy. Required.

And trustworthiness is developed by being trusted with something, which means somebody has to extend the first trust before it has been demonstrated.

That first extension is yours. Nobody else can make it.

# Day 11

## What Did She Think About What He Just Said?

### It can get sparky in that moment

**"Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor."** ECCLESIASTES 4:9

Here is a move that changes a family conversation more than anything else I know, and it takes one sentence.

Somebody says something. And instead of responding to it, you turn to their spouse and ask what she thought about what he just said.

It can get sparky in that moment. I will not pretend otherwise.

But what comes out is almost always the thing the room needed.

Here is why it works. In most families, couples have an agreed public position, and one of them delivers it. That is not deception, it is ordinary partnership. But the agreed position is a compromise, and the actual views underneath it are usually more interesting and sometimes quite different.

When you ask the other spouse directly, in the room, you are not creating a disagreement. You are surfacing one that already existed and had been managed privately.

And managed privately is exactly where a family disagreement causes the most damage, because it cannot be worked on.

I see this most with older couples talking about the end of their lives. A man will describe what he wants, and it is thoughtful and generous and it is his vision. And his wife is sitting right there with something specific in mind that has never been asked for.

Frequently what she wants is modest. Frequently he would happily give it to her. Nobody has said it out loud in forty years because there was never an occasion where the question was asked directly.

I have watched a man's whole answer change in ten minutes because somebody asked his wife what she thought.

Ecclesiastes says two are better than one because they have a good return for their labor. It is usually read at weddings.

The mechanism it describes is not sentiment. Two people produce more than one, and they only do that when both are actually contributing rather than one contributing on behalf of both.

So in your next family conversation, when somebody speaks for a couple, ask the other one.

Kindly, without implying disagreement, and then wait.

# Day 12

## Somebody Else Should Lead It

### If I do it, they'll think I'm telling them what I want

**"Let another praise you, and not your own mouth."** PROVERBS 27:2

The father in that family of ten said something to me that most people in his position never say.

If I lead this, they are going to think I am just telling them what I want.

He was right, and the fact that he knew it is why the meeting worked.

Here is the honest problem at the center of this whole book. Everything about running a good family meeting assumes somebody in the room can hold it neutrally. And in a family, nobody is neutral. Everybody has a stake and a history and a position.

The coaching version of this material solves it by bringing in an advisor. That is a legitimate answer and it is not available to most families reading this.

So here are the four ways families actually solve it themselves.

**The question does the work.** If it is written down in front of everybody, nobody had to be the person who raised it. The book asked it. That is real cover and it is frequently enough.

**Rotate the chair.** I watched a family pass facilitation around. The matriarch took one part, then a son-in-law, then one of the daughters. Engagement went up markedly, because it stopped being one person's meeting.

**Split the room.** I worked with a large family where we gathered all the men, then all the women, then the seven who had married in. That last conversation surfaced things the parents needed to hear that would never have come out in the full room.

**Bring somebody in.** Always available, and it should not be the default, or the family never develops the capacity.

Notice what all four have in common. None of them requires the person with the most authority to become neutral, which is not possible.

They arrange the room so neutrality is not required.

Proverbs says let another praise you and not your own mouth. It is about self-promotion, and underneath it is a broader principle. Some things carry weight only when they come from somebody other than the person who benefits.

The same is true of a question. Asked by dad, it is a prompt. Asked by the book, or by a sibling, or by the son-in-law holding the chair, it is a question.

# Day 13

## We Are a Family First

### The tension for somebody who is a builder

**"So we, who are many, are one body in Christ, and individually parts of one another."** ROMANS 12:5

I was in a meeting where one of the adult children stood up.

He said, we are a family. We are a family first.

The context was a father who is a builder, a type A, somebody who had run this thing successfully for decades and who was running the family meeting the same way he ran a leadership meeting. Efficient. Structured. Moving.

And nothing he was doing was wrong on its own terms. It was simply the wrong terms.

If you are the CEO of a company, all your employees do exactly what you tell them. That is not true in your family, and applying the first posture to the second produces a room where everybody complies and nobody engages.

The son was not being disrespectful. He was naming the category error.

I have a version of this in my own house. I will say to my wife, Sue, can we go out and have coffee.

And her next question is, is this a meeting? Or are we going to go have coffee?

She is asking which mode I am in, because she can feel the difference and she would like to know before we sit down.

That question is one of the more useful things anybody has ever asked me.

Here is why this matters beyond tone. A family meeting run as a business meeting will produce business outcomes, which is to say decisions, action items, and clarity.

Those are good and they are not the point. The point is a family that is closer, more trusting, and more willing to say true things. Those outcomes do not come from efficiency. They come from an unhurried room where somebody can say something half-formed without it being processed.

Paul says we are individually parts of one another. It is a strange construction and it is deliberately not the language of an organization.

An organization has members. A body has parts that belong to each other.

The father in that room was not wrong to want it to go well. He was applying the only competence he had, which is what most of us do.

**1.** What mode do you run your family gatherings in?

**2.** Would somebody in your family recognize the phrase, is this a meeting?

**4.** What competence are you applying that does not belong here?

# Day 14

## I Didn't Ask Him Once

### Just want to know it's on the table

**"He who restrains his words has knowledge, and one who has a cool spirit is a person of understanding."** PROVERBS 17:27

There is a practice in leading a family conversation that is easy to describe and hard to do.

You have to pay attention to who is speaking and who is not.

And then, when needed, you invite people in. I would be interested in your perspective. Or, we have not heard from you yet. It does not have to be long. Whatever two cents they have.

The reason this matters is that a room does not distribute itself evenly. Some people find speaking easy and some do not, and the ones who do will fill every available space without any bad intent at all.

So somebody has to notice, and noticing is a job.

I worked with a family where one member had been away for years. He had drifted, and there was history, and he came for one hour as a favor.

He stayed two days.

At the end of it he said something I have never forgotten. He said, I did not know you really cared about my opinion.

Nobody had ever asked him. Not once. And the absence of asking had been read, correctly, as an absence of interest, and he had responded by removing himself, and the family had interpreted the removal as disinterest on his part.

Both were wrong. Neither would have found out without somebody asking a question.

Here is the practical form. Do not say we would love to hear from everyone, which is a pleasantry that lands on nobody. Ask a person, by name, a specific question, and then wait.

That feels awkward and the awkwardness is the whole job.

There is a second half that matters as much. Once they answer, do not evaluate it. Just take it. If the first thing a quiet person says gets assessed, that is the last thing they will say.

Proverbs says the one who restrains his words has knowledge and a cool spirit belongs to a person of understanding. It is usually read as counsel against talking too much.

Read it as a description of what you want in whoever is holding a family conversation. Restraint. Not because they have nothing to say. Because their saying it would cost somebody else the chance.

**2.** Has anyone ever asked them directly, by name?

**3.** Who has removed themselves, and how was that interpreted?

**4.** What happens in your family when a quiet person finally says something?

# Day 15

## Is This a Meeting?

### Or are we going to go have coffee

**"A joyful heart is good medicine, but a broken spirit dries up the bones."** PROVERBS 17:22

I want to end this session on something lighter, because there is a failure mode on the other side and families fall into it too.

You can over-formalize this.

My wife's question is the corrective. Sue, can we go out and have coffee. Is this a meeting? Or are we going to go have coffee?

She is not objecting to meetings. She is asking me not to disguise one as coffee.

Here is the failure I am describing. A family reads material like this, takes it seriously, and turns every interaction into an occasion. Every conversation has an agenda. Every dinner becomes an opportunity. The children start to dread visits because something is always being worked on.

That is not what any of this is for, and it produces exactly the closure it was meant to prevent.

The families who do this well have a much lighter touch than people expect. They have one or two structured gatherings a year. The rest of the time they are simply a family, and the structured gatherings work precisely because the rest of the time is not structured.

There is a diagnostic buried in Sue's question that is worth stealing.

Before you sit down with somebody in your family, know which one it is, and say so.

If it is a meeting, say it is a meeting. That is fair and it lets people arrive prepared. If it is coffee, then let it be coffee, and do not smuggle an agenda into it, because people can feel the smuggling and it costs you the coffee and the agenda both.

Proverbs says a joyful heart is good medicine. It sits in a book largely concerned with wisdom and discipline, and it is there as a corrective.

A family that only ever does serious things together is not a healthier family. It is a family that has confused intention with intensity.

That is the end of this session. Next is what to do when a conversation drifts, which assumes you have had enough of them for drift to be your problem.
