# 🎬 SESSION 1 — WHY HAVE A FAMILY MEETING

There Is Going to Be a Family Meeting

*Source: filmed Meetings S1; Meetings script S1; Family Book ch. 12*

**BRETT: CENTER**

Well, welcome to Family Legacy by Design. We are so glad you have joined us.

My name is Brett, and I am here with the well-known sage and guru of working with families, Tom Conway. It is great to have you, Tom.

We are kicking off a brand new series on family meetings, and this one is close to the heart of everything. Because most families know these conversations matter. Very few of them actually happen in any consistent way.

And it is not because people do not care. It is usually because nobody is quite sure what it would look like, or how to start, or what might happen once everybody is actually in the room.

So we want to make this simple and very practical.

One note before we start. If the phrase *family meeting* already feels heavy to you, you are not alone. For a lot of families it sounds like something serious and uncomfortable is about to happen, and that alone keeps people from starting. Nothing in this series requires you to open the hardest subject first. Start where you can.

**BRETT: TURN**

So Tom, when you think about a family meeting, what is it really all about?

**TOM: CENTER**

Well, I often tell people there is going to be a family meeting. It is just a matter of whether you are going to be there or not.

A family meeting is simply a time when a family comes together intentionally to talk about things that matter to them. It is not complicated. It is not overly formal.

It is about creating space. Space that does not happen on its own.

Most families talk on a regular basis, but those conversations stay at a surface level. What is missing is the ones further out. The ones that matter most. Where things are going, what we are building together, what the future is going to look like.

**BRETT: CENTER**

That is so helpful, Tom, because most families would say, we talk all the time, we do not need a meeting.

And what you are pointing to is different. It is not just talking. It is creating space for conversations that actually move something forward.

**BRETT: TURN**

So why do families delay?

**TOM: CENTER**

Most of the time it comes down to uncertainty.

Families think about having a meeting and everybody starts to get nervous, because they start anticipating what might happen. They know the personalities in the room. They know the dynamics. And they are not sure how it is going to go.

So instead of stepping into it, they delay it. They kick the can down the road. Not because it is unimportant. Because it feels unclear.

And over time, those conversations do not go away. They just get more complicated.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, that is an important distinction, because from the outside it can look like avoidance. What is really happening is people are trying to anticipate how it will go and they are not confident where they stand.

So where do you start?

**TOM: CENTER**

Purpose. Before you think about structure, you have to be clear on why you are getting together.

Are we trying to communicate something more clearly? Are we trying to solve a problem? Are we trying to teach the next generation something? Are we trying to build understanding?

If that is not clear, the meeting is going to drift.

And there is a concept Bob Beale talks about. He wrote a book called *Stop Setting Goals, Start Solving Problems.* He points out that about half of people respond well to goals, and the other half feel like they are being put in a box they do not want to be in. But give those same people a problem to solve, and they engage. They will come out with a list of things to do.

So instead of saying, my goal is to lose fifteen pounds, they say, I have got a problem, I am fifteen pounds overweight, what am I going to do about it. And then they move.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, once the purpose is clear, then you can think about structure.

**TOM: CENTER**

An agenda gives a meeting structure. It helps people know what you are trying to accomplish, how the conversation will move, how long it will take.

And that matters because people are wired differently. Some people are naturally structured and some are not at all. So the agenda is a tool. It is not rigid. It just keeps the meeting from drifting.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, give people the ground rules, because I think this is what makes a room feel safe.

**TOM: CENTER**

Just a few. Not a long list.

Listen to understand. Give people the opportunity to speak at any time. Keep it respectful.

When people are clear about the expectations, they are more comfortable participating. Without that, one or two people carry the whole thing and everybody else stays quiet.

And you want the family involved, and that includes spouses. People ask me, do you really want the in-laws, or the out-laws, in the meeting? And the answer is absolutely yes.

Because if they are not there, your son or daughter is going home and telling them what happened. And I guarantee you it is not the same as what was said in the room.

It is better for everyone to hear the same thing at the same time.

> **Scripture.** Proverbs 16:3. *Commit your works to the Lord and your plans will be established.* Notice the order. Commit first. The establishing is His part.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

**SCENARIO ONE.** A family that finally decides to do it. Everybody shows up. The intention is good. There is no agenda, no expectations set, and not everyone who should be there is in the room. The conversation moves around. Two people carry most of it. At the end, nobody is quite sure what was decided.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

What you are seeing there is a meeting with the right intent and not enough clarity about how it was going to work.

When there is no sense of what success looks like, people do not know how to engage. And when the right people are not in the room, the conversation does not stay aligned afterward.

So it feels like something happened. It just does not move anything forward.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Let me give you another version of this.

**SCENARIO TWO.** Same family, six months later. They bring the right people. They set expectations ahead of time. They have a simple agenda. And they keep the focus on one or two things instead of trying to cover everything.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

The difference is intentionality.

The meeting was not just something they decided to do. It was something they prepared for. People knew why they were there. They had space to participate. The conversation stayed focused.

It may not have solved everything. But it created movement. And that is what a successful meeting does.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, this is the part I most want people to hear before they start.

**TOM: CENTER**

You do not have to get this perfect.

If something is worth doing, it is worth doing poorly.

I heard a man speak once, and he contested the phrase that says if something is worth doing, it is worth doing well. He said, I do not believe that at all. If something is worth doing, it is worth doing poorly, because even if it is not perfect the first time, the second time will be a little better. The third time will be better still.

Because the way you get better at it is by doing it. The way you do not get better at it is by not doing it.

**BRETT: CENTER**

To the next generation watching, one thing.

You do not have to wait to be asked. If your family has never done this, somebody has to go first, and it does not have to be your father. Asking for an hour together to talk about where the family is headed is not overstepping. In most families it is a relief.

**BRETT: CENTER**

**THIS WEEK.** Pick a date.

That is the entire assignment. Not the agenda. Not the topics. A date on the calendar that people have said yes to.

**WHEN TO BRING SOMEONE IN.** If there is unresolved conflict in your family that has been sitting for years, or if one person's presence changes how everyone else behaves, consider having someone outside the family facilitate the first one. That is not a failure. It is wisdom.

**BRETT: TURN**

Tom, bring us home.

**TOM: CENTER**

Maybe the question is not, are we ready to do this perfectly.

Maybe it is just, are we willing to take the first step.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Next session, not every meeting should look the same. We will show you why.

And as Nike would say, just do it.

# 🎬 SESSION 2 — DIFFERENT WAYS TO GATHER

Matching the Meeting to What You Need

*Source: filmed Meetings S2; Meetings script S2; Training Manual S10*

**BRETT: CENTER**

Well, welcome back to our series, Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we are glad you are back.

Last session we talked about designing a family meeting. Starting with purpose and putting a simple structure around it. And for a lot of families, that alone is a big step.

But once that foundation is there, something else shows up pretty quickly.

Not every meeting should look the same.

And this is where a lot of families get stuck. Once they finally have a meeting, the tendency is to cover everything in one sitting. Finances, family dynamics, future plans, unresolved tensions, succession. All of it.

And when that happens, the meeting feels full. It does not feel productive.

**BRETT: TURN**

So Tom, what are you really trying to help a family understand here?

**TOM: CENTER**

You are matching the format of the meeting to the purpose of the meeting.

One of the most common problems is using the same kind of meeting for everything. And not every conversation is the same.

There are generally four kinds.

One is a **learning meeting.** Educational. You are teaching people how something works. Maybe even how a trust works in your family. The goal is understanding, because at some point they are going to take this over.

Second is an **alignment meeting.** People are sharing perspectives. You want everybody's input, so there is more ownership in whatever comes next.

Third is a **decision-making meeting.** We have a lake house. We are thinking of selling it. What do you think? And one person says, I love waterskiing, do not sell it. And another says, I would rather have a place in the mountains, I never go there anyway.

And fourth is a **long-term planning meeting.** This is where we are, this is what we are thinking about doing, we want everybody to speak into it.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, if the format does not match the purpose, the meeting can feel off even when the topic was right.

**TOM: CENTER**

That is exactly right. And that is where frustration builds. People walk away feeling like it did not accomplish what it was supposed to, even when the content mattered.

> **Scripture.** Ecclesiastes 3:1. *There is an appointed time for everything.* Different conversations need different approaches, and they need their own moment.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, and then there are the categories of what you actually talk about.

**TOM: CENTER**

Most family conversations fall into a few broad areas.

**Personal.** Relationships, life direction, individual situations.

**Business,** if the family owns or operates one together.

**Estate.** How things will transfer and how decisions get made.

**Philanthropic.** What impact do we want to have as a family with our giving, and who will lead those decisions over time.

Those categories help you organize. Instead of trying to cover everything, you start asking what are we actually trying to address.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, some of the topics that come up are not the ones people expect.

**TOM: CENTER**

`[TOM: the gun room. The thirty-five guns and telling him to decide which rifle goes to which grandchild before they fight about it when he is gone.]`

Because here is the thing. Most family fights are not over the money. They are over the china. They are over the jewelry. They are over the guns, especially with the men. The cars.

I have a client with three Rolls-Royces. Who gets those? That is a real decision somebody has to make.

**BRETT: CENTER**

I will tell on myself here, Tom, because you got me with this one.

I had an uncle who took me hunting when I was young. He had a Browning twelve-gauge, an automatic, and I would have loved to have that gun. And I never had the heart to ask him for it. He has passed away now, and it went to my aunt, and I still feel awkward about it.

And it is not property. That is exactly the point. It was never about the gun.

**BRETT: TURN**

Tom, one more before the scenarios. Meetings with and without numbers.

**TOM: CENTER**

Yeah. Sometimes mom and dad are not sure they want the kids to know what they have. Because it has grown to be a big number, and they do not feel the kids are ready for it.

So we have done family meetings both ways.

`[TOM: the family that did the reveal, and the in-law who got afraid rather than excited, because he was thinking about his own children. Keep the reaction. Lose the figure.]`

And Ron Blue used to ask the question that settles this for me. Is the next steward prepared? Well, how are they going to be prepared if they do not even know what they are going to be responsible for?

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

**SCENARIO ONE.** A family that schedules one meeting a year and tries to put everything in it. The business, the estate, the giving, and whatever tension is currently unresolved. Four hours. Everybody leaves exhausted and nothing is settled.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

You are trying to give people too many things to think about.

Pick one. Focus your attention on something that is really important, and let the rest wait for the next one. The more people understand what the purpose is, the more they can come in having actually thought about it beforehand.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Let me give you another version of this.

**SCENARIO TWO.** A family where something has changed suddenly. A diagnosis. A business offer. Something that moves the timeline.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

`[TOM: the client who called after a cancer diagnosis and said they needed to accelerate their thinking.]`

Sometimes circumstances force it, and you just have to get everybody together and go through those things.

And that is exactly why you do not wait for a reason. Because when the reason comes, you would much rather be adding to a conversation you have already been having than starting one from nothing.

**BRETT: CENTER**

To the next generation, here is what I would say about this session.

You probably have an opinion about the lake house. Or the business. Or the thing in the family that everyone tiptoes around. And you may have decided it is not your place to say it.

It is. Your parents almost certainly want to know what you think, and they have no way to find out unless you tell them.

**BRETT: CENTER**

**THIS WEEK.** Decide what kind of meeting your first one is going to be. Learning, alignment, decision, or planning.

Only one kind. That is the discipline.

**WHEN TO BRING SOMEONE IN.** If the topic is estate distribution or business succession, and this is the first time your family has ever discussed it out loud, consider having someone in the room who has done it before.

**BRETT: TURN**

Tom, bring us home.

**TOM: CENTER**

Every family is unique. What works for one is not going to work for another.

So we are trying to give you principles you can take and apply in your own context, with your own relationships, so you can have a good meeting and accomplish what you actually want to accomplish.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Next session is the one everybody asks about. Who leads it?

We will see you there.

# 🎬 SESSION 3 — WHO LEADS AND HOW

Creating a Room Where People Will Speak

*Source: filmed Meetings S2, S3, and S5*

**BRETT: CENTER**

Well, welcome back to our series on Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we are excited about this session, because it takes things to the next level.

Last session we talked about matching the kind of meeting to what you are trying to accomplish. And that changes a lot.

But once everyone is in the room, there is another layer that determines whether the meeting works. And that is how the conversation actually gets led.

Because you can have the right purpose, the right people, and a clear agenda, and still walk away feeling like it did not land.

**BRETT: TURN**

So Tom, let's start where most families do not want to start. What happens when the patriarch leads the meeting?

**TOM: CENTER**

A lot of times when the patriarch leads it, the kids are thinking, Dad is going to tell us what he wants us to hear. We need to be careful. We cannot say what we really think. We just need to be respectful, listen, and nod our heads.

`[TOM: the client whose four children were interviewed, and the exchange with him and his wife afterward. Keep it exactly as told.]`

Here is a man who was totally unaware. He thought he was helping his kids. And they all just assumed he was going to tell them what to do and they would get in line.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, this is the honest problem at the center of this whole series.

Everything you teach advisors about facilitating assumes there is a person in the room with no stake in the outcome. A family sitting down alone does not have that person.

So let's answer it directly. If you are a family doing this yourselves, what do you do?

**TOM: CENTER**

Well, the first thing is you have to understand what you are actually trying to create. You want mom and dad to be one of a group of peers. You do not want dad controlling the conversation or mom controlling the conversation. You want them to be participants.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, there are four ways we have seen families do that without anyone from the outside.

**The first is that the question does the work.** If it is written down in front of everybody, then nobody had to be the person who raised it. The page asked it, not your brother. That is real cover, and it is often enough.

**The second is to rotate the chair.**

**TOM: CENTER**

`[TOM: the family where facilitation was passed around. The matriarch took one part, then a son-in-law, then one of the daughters. Keep the shape.]`

And there was much greater engagement, because it was not one person's meeting.

**BRETT: CENTER**

**The third is the vote.**

**TOM: CENTER**

I did a meeting with a mom and dad who had four kids, and all of them were married. So there were ten of us in the room.

Dad said, it is going to go much better if you facilitate it, because if I do it, they are going to think I am just telling them what I want.

And when we voted on things, mom and dad only had two votes out of ten. So they could not control the outcome. And the kids felt much more engaged. They felt like they were making a decision as a family, rather than being asked to say yes to a decision that had already been made.

**BRETT: CENTER**

**And the fourth is to split the room.**

I had the privilege of working with a very large family, and we did not just get together as one group. We did a gathering with all the men, multigenerationally. Then another with all the women. And then one with the married-ins, about seven of them.

That last conversation was unique and dynamic and it surfaced things the matriarch and patriarch needed to hear that would never have come out in the full room.

**BRETT: TURN**

Tom, what about the person who does not speak?

**TOM: CENTER**

You have to pay attention to who is speaking and who is not. The quiet ones in the room, and even the people who are not in the room.

`[TOM: the family member who had been away for years, was invited to come for just an hour, stayed two days, and what he said to the group at the end.]`

And when needed, you invite people in. You might say, I would be interested in your perspective. Or, we have not heard from you yet. It does not have to be long. Just whatever two cents they have.

Because if people are not engaged, they are not going to feel connected to the outcome.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, there is a warning you gave that I think belongs to every parent watching this, not just advisors.

**TOM: CENTER**

There is a book called *Knowing God* by J.I. Packer, and at the end he talks about decision-making. And one of his points is: suspect yourself.

I thought that was a great comment. Because we all think we have got it under control.

So when you are interacting, especially with your kids, suspect yourself. Am I becoming overpowering here, to where the kid is just going to shut up because he knows I do not really care what he thinks?

`[TOM: the man who said "when my father wanted my opinion, he gave it to me," and what Tom told him.]`

Your kids are not your employees. You did not hire them. They were given to you.

> **Scripture.** 2 Timothy 1:7. *God has not given us a spirit of timidity, but of power and love and discipline.* That is a good picture of leading a family conversation. Not timid, willing to guide, and doing it with care and self-control.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

**SCENARIO ONE.** Someone leading a meeting who is very structured. Every box checked. Moving point to point, keeping it tight. And the family feels like there is no room to actually engage. At the end it was efficient and not relational at all.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

If you are the CEO of a company, all your employees do exactly what you tell them. That is not true in your family.

`[TOM: the meeting where one of the adult children stood up and said this is not a business, we are a family first.]`

And I will confess my own version of this. I will say to my wife, Sue, can we go out and have coffee. And her next question is, is this a meeting? Or are we going to go have coffee?

**BRETT: CENTER**

Let me give you another version of this.

**SCENARIO TWO.** The opposite. Nobody really leads. It is warm and pleasant and it wanders for two hours, and at the end everyone feels good and nothing was decided or even really said.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

Most people fall into one of those two extremes. Over-directing, or under-leading. The goal is the balance between them.

And somebody does need to take responsibility for guiding it. If nobody owns that, the conversation just goes wherever it goes.

That does not mean controlling it. It means creating direction without taking over.

**BRETT: CENTER**

To the next generation, this session is yours more than any other in the book.

If your family is going to rotate the chair, somebody has to be willing to take it. And the person who takes it does not need authority. They need to be able to ask a question and then be quiet.

That is a skill you can offer your family right now, at any age.

**BRETT: CENTER**

**THIS WEEK.** Decide who leads your first meeting. And let it be someone other than the person who usually decides things.

**WHEN TO BRING SOMEONE IN.** If one person's presence reliably shuts the room down, or if there is a history that makes any family member unsafe to lead, bring in an outside facilitator for the first few. Rotate the chair after trust has been built, not before.

**BRETT: TURN**

Tom, bring us home.

**TOM: CENTER**

The goal is not agreement. The goal is understanding.

Not just what did she say. Why did she say it. Because when people understand each other, you get family harmony, and that is what we are after.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Next session, what happens when the conversation drifts.

We will see you there.

# 🎬 SESSION 4 — WHAT TO TALK ABOUT

Keeping the Conversation on What Matters

*Source: filmed Meetings S4; Meetings script S4*

**BRETT: CENTER**

Well, welcome back to our series on Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett and this is Tom.

Last session we talked about who leads and how, and how to guide without taking over.

But even when all of that is working, something almost always happens.

The conversation starts to drift.

That is why we say legacy by design, not by drift and not by default. And the drift is not dramatic. It is subtle, and it is significant over time.

Somebody brings up something related but not quite on topic. You address it, because you have to. Then someone else comments. Another thought gets added. And before you know it, the conversation has moved away from what you set out to talk about.

**BRETT: TURN**

And Tom, what makes this hard is that nothing feels wrong in the moment.

**TOM: CENTER**

That is right. It all feels connected. It all feels worth discussing.

But by the end you realize you did not accomplish what you came to do.

So you are helping the conversation stay aligned with the purpose. Every conversation naturally expands. That is not bad. But when it goes from expanding to going down a different trail entirely, somebody needs to bring it back.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, and here is what I appreciate about how you do this. You are not ignoring what was said, and you are not letting it take over. You are doing both at once.

**TOM: CENTER**

If you ignore it, people feel dismissed. If you follow it too far, you lose focus.

So you acknowledge it, and then you bring it back. You might say, that is an important point, and I want us to come back to where we were going.

And sometimes you just write it down. Say, let's come back to that later. Then people know they were heard and the thing is not lost.

> **Scripture.** Proverbs 4:25. *Let your eyes look directly ahead.* The verse goes on to say give careful thought to the path you are on. That is exactly what somebody has to do in the room.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, how direct can you be?

**TOM: CENTER**

Sometimes you just need to be direct. We talk about being direct, being truthful, and being respectful. You are trying to hold all three at the same time.

If somebody keeps pushing it off track, you can say, I want to make sure we use our time well. Everyone gave time to be here. Let's hold that for later and get back to what we came to do.

Most people respond well when they understand why you are guiding it, because they want the meeting to accomplish something too.

And there is a principle in Colossians 4:6 that says let your conversation always be with grace. A lot of the magic here is doing it graciously while still getting people back where they need to be.

**BRETT: CENTER**

It is sort of being a nice pain in the neck.

**TOM: CENTER**

That is right. I am trying to get you back where you need to go, and doing it in a way where you do not hate me by the time it is over.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

**SCENARIO ONE.** A meeting with a clear purpose. Everybody starts on the same page. Halfway through, someone raises something related but not connected. Someone responds. Someone else adds to it. And twenty minutes later you are deep into a different conversation entirely.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

Well, if I was leading, we would not be twenty minutes into it. We would be five minutes into it, and then we would bring it back.

Because you have to remember what the goal is. Why are we doing this? We are doing it to serve the family. We are doing it to help the family communicate with each other.

All those other things may be important to the person who brought them up. They are not important to the purpose of what we are trying to accomplish right now.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Let me give you another version of this.

**SCENARIO TWO.** A family where one person consistently steers every conversation toward their own concern. Not maliciously. It is just where their attention is. And everyone else has quietly stopped bringing things up because they know how it will go.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

Then you need to be direct, and you may need to do it privately.

One thing I would do is take a break. Pull that person aside. Ask them to help you get the others engaged. Now they have a job, and it is not the job of dominating the room.

And that gets it off the hot seat, so you are not confronting them in front of everybody and making them feel bad.

What is said in public needs to be addressed in public. But how you handle a person is often better done in private.

**BRETT: CENTER**

To the next generation, one thing about this session.

If you are the person who has stopped bringing things up because of how it goes, the answer is not to stop coming. It is to write your one thing down before the meeting and ask for it to go on the agenda.

An item on a page is much harder to talk over than a person is.

**BRETT: CENTER**

**THIS WEEK.** Write the agenda for your first meeting. Two items maximum.

Then decide, before you walk in, what you are going to say when the conversation drifts. Having the words ready is most of the battle.

**WHEN TO BRING SOMEONE IN.** If drift is not really drift, and there is a subject the family is actively steering around, that is a different problem than focus. Get help with that one.

**BRETT: TURN**

Tom, bring us home.

**TOM: CENTER**

The purpose is the anchor. That is why we are here. The agenda lays out the direction.

And when you bring the conversation back to the purpose, everybody starts to feel like you are actually accomplishing something. Over time, they come to expect that, and they trust the meeting is going to stay centered on what matters to them.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Next session is the one that determines whether any of this lasts. What stays at the center.

# 🎬 SESSION 5 — YOUR VALUES, MISSION, AND VISION

Keeping the Family Grounded

*Source: filmed Meetings S5; Meetings script S5; filmed Clarity S3*

**BRETT: CENTER**

Welcome back to Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we are privileged to guide you through this.

We have covered a lot of ground. Designing the meeting. Choosing the right kind. Who leads it. Keeping it focused.

But underneath all of that is something that determines whether these meetings actually matter over time. And it is this question.

What stays at the very center?

Because here is what happens. Meetings slowly become more tactical. More transactional. More financial. You start talking about decisions and structures and responsibilities, and over time those things take over the air time.

And none of that is wrong. It is all important. But somewhere along the way, what matters most drifts into the background.

**BRETT: TURN**

So Tom, what are you really helping a family do here?

**TOM: CENTER**

You are helping them stay grounded in what matters most to them. It is not all about the money.

Over time, families naturally move toward urgent decisions. Responsibilities. Opportunities. Things they need to do. Those are important, but they become the focus and the deeper purpose gets lost.

So part of the work is consistently bringing the conversation back. What is our family all about? What are the values that are important to us? What is the vision, and how do we help it happen?

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, you say this needs its own meeting. Not a segment of one.

**TOM: CENTER**

It does. Values and vision usually do not get clarified in a short segment of a broader meeting. They need a different kind of focus.

So we help families create their vision, mission, and values in a separate gathering. It takes a few hours. Minimum of three, probably closer to four or five. A Saturday. A retreat. An overnight.

Everybody gets to speak into it. And they come up with what they agree on as the values they want to live out as a family.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And here is the beauty of it, Tom. Mom and dad are not in charge.

**TOM: CENTER**

That is right. In that family of ten I mentioned, mom and dad had two votes out of ten. So the kids really felt empowered to speak into what is important for this family.

And mom and dad were great with it. There was real ownership, and they were proud of their kids.

And honestly, the kids did not come up with anything mom and dad disagreed with. They have lived in this culture. And the married-ins came from different families, so they brought their part, and they all got a say.

> **Scripture.** Habakkuk 2:2. *Record the vision and inscribe it on tablets.* Tom's line on this is that a vision has to be visible if it is going to guide behavior.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, that is the hard part, isn't it. Families do the work and then it goes in a drawer.

**TOM: CENTER**

It does. People create these and never look at them again. It is like a budget. People make one, put it in a drawer, and look at it next year.

So when the family is together, pull it out. Say, this is what we said we wanted our family to be about. Is that still true? Is there anything we would change?

A lot of times there is nothing to change. But then the next question is the real one. How are we living this out?

And there is a principle in Deuteronomy. These things are to be on your hearts, and you are to talk about them regularly with each other.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, I love the creative ways families have done this.

`[TOM: the family where each person gave a short testimony about progress on one of the five family values, and the next year they handed the five values out to the five children.]`

`[TOM: the family that does a prayer meeting on video every other week, come or do not come.]`

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

**SCENARIO ONE.** A family that did all the work. They defined their values. They had real conversations. And for a while it shaped things. Then over time the meetings became about decisions and finances and opportunities. The values are still there. They are just not part of the conversation anymore.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

That is very common, because people change. What matters most naturally drifts unless it is brought back.

So you bring it back. This is what we said. Is this still what we believe?

And especially if something happened in the past year that ran contrary to it. Say someone had a health problem, and two of the siblings reached out and two did not. Well, wait. I thought family was important to us.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Let me give you another version of this.

**SCENARIO TWO.** A family facing a real decision where people genuinely disagree. Not because anyone is trying to create conflict. Because they are evaluating it differently, and they are not using the same framework anymore.

**BRETT: TURN**

**TOM: CENTER**

You have to realize that as your kids get older and get married, the person they married brought their own values into the family. Different from what your family was when you started.

They are trying to do exactly what you are doing. They are trying to create harmony in their family. But it is a different family than the one they grew up in.

So you have to be sensitive to that. You know your kids pretty well. You lived with them a long time. You do not know the values that came in from the people they married.

**BRETT: CENTER**

To the next generation, and especially to anyone who married into a family.

# 🎬 SESSION 6 — BUILDING A RHYTHM THAT LASTS

Staying Connected Over Time

*Source: filmed Meetings S6; Meetings script S6; Family Book ch. 12*

**BRETT: CENTER**

Well, welcome back to Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we are grateful you have stayed the course.

This is our final session, and it is about creating consistent rhythms. Helping your family stay connected over time. Because this does not happen one and done. It happens best over time.

We have talked about designing the meeting, choosing the right kind, who leads it, keeping it focused, and reinforcing the values.

All of that matters. But there is one piece that determines whether any of it lasts.

Because a family can have one great meeting and walk away encouraged and aligned and even excited. And then life picks back up. Schedules fill. People move. And without anyone realizing it, the connection starts to fade.

**BRETT: TURN**

So Tom, when you say rhythm, what is at stake?

**TOM: CENTER**

You are trying to build consistency. You are trying to build communication.

A meeting by itself is just an event. Rhythm is what turns an event into something that continues.

And a lot of it depends on the complexity of your situation. If there is a business in the middle of it, you might need to meet more often, because there are decisions to make. If not, it might be an annual get-together. Maybe semi-annual. Maybe you are together in the summer and again at Christmas, and one of those is a thirty-minute update rather than a three-hour meeting.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, geography is the obstacle for most families.

**TOM: CENTER**

It is. You have got kids scattered all over the country. You may video call from time to time, but you are not having normal connection time. It is not like everybody can come over Sunday afternoon for dinner.

So you find out what works, and who you really need to talk to. Some of your kids it is easy to have a conversation with. Others you have to go out of your way, because they have a lot going on.

In our family, my wife has a call every week with one of our daughters. The other daughter talks to me, and we have a call every three weeks. And then whatever I learn I bring home, and whatever Susan learns she shares.

And I share an office with my son, so I bring home a lot about their family just by being there.

**BRETT: CENTER**

Tom, and the rhythms are not all meetings.

**TOM: CENTER**

No. The families I work with, some of them do a vacation once a year and the parents cover it so it is no hassle on anybody.

Anything you can do to get them together is what you do.

We try to get most of our family together once a year. We go for a week and stay in a place together. Our situation does not change a lot, but sometimes there is something important we need to discuss and we will do it during that time.

And we do a trip down the river together. On rafts. The kids jump in the water and they pass food around. It is a blast. The point is you are just enjoying each other.

**BRETT: CENTER**

And Tom, family meetings are not meant to be a burden.

**TOM: CENTER**

They are not. But you need to have at least one. You need one where you are communicating where you are and what the situation is, so that everybody is aware.

`[TOM: the client who asked his children for their top three prayer requests for the year, and then asked for a report the next time they were together.]`

You want an ongoing relationship with your children, so it is not just how are you doing today, but how are you growing. How are you doing spiritually? What are you reading?

Because legacy is really simple when you get it down to it. If you can raise your kids to love Jesus, and they can raise their kids to love Jesus, you probably do not have to worry about a lot of other things. You do not have to worry about how the money is going to ruin them, because if they are following Jesus, they are probably going to be decent stewards.

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

Then you do not need a new meeting. You need thirty minutes inside the one you already have.

Toward the end of dinner. Short. Just communicating a few things so they know they are part of something bigger than themselves.

And Tom, what strikes me is that your daughters are competing over who gets to care for her.

The point is that they are connected. And that they love each other.
