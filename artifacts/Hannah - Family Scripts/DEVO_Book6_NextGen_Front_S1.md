# HOW TO USE THIS

Thirty readings. Five per session, six sessions, with two days off between. Ten minutes each.

**This is the book most likely to be read by two generations at once.** Everything in it applies to the parent or grandparent doing the preparing, and nearly all of it applies just as directly to the person being prepared. If you are in the next generation and somebody handed you this, it was not a hint. Read it as yours.

**This works completely on its own.** No videos required, no guide required, nobody else in your family required.

**It also runs alongside the guide.** If your family is working through *Raising the Next Generation*, these thirty days are the reading plan. The five readings in each group go with the matching session.

**Each day has six parts.** The reading. Reflect. Practice. One Question to Ask. The Bucket, which is private and which nobody reads. And Prayer, which is a direction rather than a written prayer.

**A note on the word children.** It appears throughout, and in most families reading this the children are grown. That is not carelessness. The relationship does not stop being what it is, and one of the things this book will keep asking is whether you are still treating an adult like one.

# Day 1

## An Heir Receives, a Steward Is Responsible

### It doesn't call us heirs, it calls us stewards

**"It is required of stewards that one be found trustworthy."** 1 CORINTHIANS 4:2

An heir receives. A steward is responsible.

Those are two different words for two different relationships to the same thing, and Scripture is not neutral between them. It does not call us heirs. It calls us stewards.

I want to start here because this single distinction reframes everything else in these thirty days.

An heir is defined by what arrives. The word describes a position in a sequence. You are an heir because of who your parents are, and nothing is required of you to hold the title. It is passive by definition.

A steward is defined by what is entrusted. The word describes a job. Somebody handed you something that belongs to someone else and expects an account of what you did with it.

Now think about what most families actually prepare their children to be.

We prepare them to receive. We think carefully about the amount, the timing, the structure, the tax treatment. Enormous effort goes into engineering the arrival. And then we hand it to a person who has been positioned their whole life as the endpoint of the transfer rather than the next one responsible for it.

That is not their failure. It is what they were trained for.

Here is what makes the distinction practical rather than philosophical. Responsibility can be given early and receiving cannot.

You cannot give someone their inheritance at twenty-two to see how it goes. But you can give them responsibility at twenty-two, in a size that fits, and watch. And then a slightly larger one. What you are doing is not testing them. You are building the only thing that makes the eventual arrival survivable, which is a person who has practiced.

The verse says it is required of stewards that one be found trustworthy. Notice the word required. Not hoped for, not ideal. Required. And notice what is being measured, which is trustworthiness rather than competence. A steward is not being graded on results. They are being graded on whether they can be trusted with something that is not theirs.

That is a thing you can develop in a person, and it takes years, and it cannot be started at the end.

So the question underneath this whole book is not what your children will receive. It is whether the people who receive it will have any framework for holding it.

And if you are in the next generation reading this, the question is simpler and more direct. What have you been made responsible for so far, and how did you handle it?

### Reflect

**2.** What is the evidence, in the last two years?

**4.** If something arrived tomorrow, what would each of them do with it in the first year?

# Day 2

## Growing Adults

### Are you treating them as adults, or as children?

**"When I became a man, I did away with childish things."** 1 CORINTHIANS 13:11

We use the term adult children all the time, and it is really an oxymoron.

Are they adults or are they children? Because the way you answer that determines almost everything about how you treat a thirty-five-year-old.

Brett has a better phrase for it, and I have started using it. **Growing adults.** Not adult children. Growing adults, with the emphasis on the first word, because they are still becoming something and so is everybody else.

The reason this matters is that families get stuck in a role assignment made twenty years ago and never revisited.

You were the parent of a child. That was correct and it required a particular posture. You decided things. You protected. You corrected. You knew more than they did about nearly everything, and acting on that was love.

Then they grew up, and in most families the posture did not change with them.

So you still explain things they already understand. You still offer the correction before it is asked for. You still make the decision and inform them, because that is what you did for twenty years and it worked. And they notice. They notice all of it, and what they conclude is that in your eyes they have not actually arrived.

Here is how it shows up. A grown man who is competent everywhere else in his life becomes quieter and smaller at his parents' table. Not because anyone did anything wrong that evening. Because the room has a shape, and he takes his old place in it.

The verse is Paul describing his own development, and the interesting part is who does the acting. *I did away with childish things.* He does it. Not his parents, not his teachers.

Which means the transition is theirs to make, and yours to allow. You cannot do it for them. But you can absolutely prevent it, and most families that get stuck here are stuck because the older generation is still holding a role nobody asked them to keep.

The practical question is not whether you respect your children. Almost every parent would say yes and mean it.

The question is whether they can tell.

### Reflect

**2.** What is one thing you still do for them that they could do themselves?

**3.** Who in your family gets smaller at the family table, and why?

**4.** If you asked them whether they feel treated as an adult, what would they say?

# Day 3

## He Never Let Them Take Over

### He still needed to make the last decision

**"The things which you have heard from me, entrust these to faithful men who will be able to teach others also."** 2 TIMOTHY 2:2

How many organizations have you watched simply peter out because the person who built them could not hand them over?

He stays longer than he should. He does not develop anybody underneath him, because developing somebody means eventually being replaced by them. And when he finally does bring someone in, he never lets them take over. He still has an office. He still needs to make the last decision.

I have seen this in businesses and I have seen it in ministries, and I have seen it in families where the thing being held is not an organization at all. It is just the position of being the one who decides.

What makes this hard to name is that it almost never looks like ego from the inside. It looks like responsibility.

The person holding on genuinely believes the thing would suffer without them. Sometimes they are right in the short term. They have thirty years of judgment that the next person does not have, and there will be mistakes, and those mistakes will be real.

But the arithmetic is not between a good decision now and a bad one. It is between a bad decision now, while you are here to help repair it, and the same bad decision made in five years when you are not.

Every handover has a cost. The only choice is whether you pay it while you can absorb it.

There is a second cost that is easier to miss. The person waiting does not wait indefinitely. They build a life around the assumption that the thing is not coming, and by the time it does come they have moved on internally, or they have grown resentful, or they have simply lost the years in which they would have learned it best.

Paul tells Timothy to entrust what he has heard to faithful people who will be able to teach others. Look at the chain. Paul to Timothy to faithful people to others. Four generations named in a single sentence, and every link requires somebody to let go.

If any one of them had held on, the sentence stops.

So the question is not whether the next person is ready. That question can be asked forever and will always have a defensible answer.

The question is what you are doing this year to make them ready, and whether there is a date.

**2.** What is your honest reason, and is it about them or about you?

**3.** Who has been waiting, and how long have they been waiting?

**4.** Is there a date, or only an intention?

# Day 4

## It's the Father Who Isn't Ready

### It's not that the boy's not ready

**"Take Joshua and lay your hand on him, and put some of your authority on him, so that all the congregation may obey him."** NUMBERS 27:18-20

I worked with a family where the son had been in the business for years. He was capable. He had proven himself by every measure anyone could name. And the father kept saying, not yet. He is not ready.

The son was close to walking away. Not in anger. He had simply concluded that it was never going to happen and that he should build something of his own before it was too late.

When it finally moved, and it did move, what became clear was this. It was not that the boy was not ready.

It was that the father was not ready.

I want to be careful with that sentence, because it can be read as an accusation and it is not one. The father was not selfish. He was frightened, and he had good reasons, and none of them had anything to do with his son's competence.

He was frightened of what he would be afterward. Of a Tuesday morning with nothing that needed him. Of the possibility that the thing would do fine without him, which is somehow worse than the possibility that it would fail.

Nobody says those things out loud, so instead the sentence that comes out is *he is not ready yet*, and that sentence is unfalsifiable. It can be said indefinitely and it always sounds responsible.

So here is a test worth applying to yourself, if you are the one holding.

If you cannot name what specifically would have to be true for the answer to become yes, then not yet is not an assessment. It is a feeling wearing the clothes of an assessment.

Name it. Write down the three things. And then either those things are achievable, in which case you have a plan and a date, or they are not, in which case you have learned that the real obstacle is somewhere else.

Look at what Moses is told to do with Joshua. Lay your hand on him. Put *some* of your authority on him. Not all of it, not yet, and not none of it either. Some. And do it publicly, so the congregation knows.

That is a transfer done in stages, in the open, by somebody who was still alive to see it happen. Moses did not die and leave a note.

He handed it over while he could still watch.

**3.** If you could not, what does that tell you?

**4.** What are you afraid you will be afterward?

# Day 5

## The Only Way You Build Trust Is by Communicating

### They just didn't know a lot of other things

**"Fathers, do not exasperate your children, so that they will not lose heart."** COLOSSIANS 3:21

There is a reason legacy does not transfer well from one generation to the next, and it is not the plan.

About sixty percent of the time it breaks down over communication and trust. About twenty-five percent of the time it is that the heirs were unprepared. Knock out those two and you have roughly an eighty-five percent chance of it going well.

Notice what is not on that list. It is not the tax strategy. It is not the structure. It is not the investment performance. Those account for the smallest slice by a wide margin, and they are where nearly all of the attention goes, because they are the part you can hire somebody to handle.

The other two you cannot outsource. They are yours.

And the two are connected. The only way you build trust is by communicating. There is no other mechanism. Trust is not a feeling that develops on its own between people who love each other. It is a conclusion people draw from repeated evidence, and the evidence is being told things.

I have sat with families where the children knew one piece and not the rest. They knew about the business because they worked in it. They did not know about the property, or the second entity, or what had been arranged for their mother. Nobody was hiding anything. It simply had never come up, and each unmentioned thing became a small piece of evidence that they were not quite trusted.

Then when everything finally got laid out, the reaction was not resentment about the amount. It was closer to relief, followed by a very reasonable question. *Why did we not know this?*

Here is the part that surprised me the first several times. Children who are told more do not become entitled. They become serious. Once they understand that something real will eventually be their responsibility, most of them start paying attention in a way they never did when it was abstract.

The verse tells fathers not to exasperate their children so they do not lose heart. Losing heart is a specific thing. It is not anger. It is a quiet giving up, the conclusion that trying to be included is not worth the effort.

That is what silence produces over twenty years. Not rebellion. Resignation.

So the first session ends here. Preparation is not primarily about money, and trust is not built by good intentions.

It is built by telling people things.
