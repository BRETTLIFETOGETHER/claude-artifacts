# HOW TO USE THIS

Thirty readings. Five per session, six sessions, with two days off between. Ten minutes each.

**A word about where this one sits.** Family meetings are events. This is everything else. The kitchen, the car, the phone call, the ten minutes after everyone else has gone to bed. Most of what actually gets communicated in a family never happens in a meeting, and this is the book about that.

**This works completely on its own.** No videos required, no guide required, nobody else in your family required.

**It also runs alongside the guide.** If your family is working through *Conversations That Matter*, these thirty days are the reading plan. The five readings in each group go with the matching session.

**Each day has six parts.** The reading. Reflect. Practice. One Question to Ask. The Bucket, which is private and which nobody reads. And Prayer, which is a direction rather than a written prayer.

**One thing before you begin.** More than any other book in this series, this one will produce a conversation you were not planning to have. That is the point, and it is also why it is worth reading a day ahead rather than in the moment before you speak to someone.

# Day 1

## They Don't Talk About What Matters

### And there are a whole bunch of reasons why

**"Therefore, laying aside falsehood, speak truth each one of you with his neighbor."** EPHESIANS 4:25

Families don't talk about what matters. And there are a whole bunch of things behind that, and it is worth asking why they don't.

I want to start here because this is the observation underneath everything else in these thirty days, and because people hear it as an accusation when it is really a description.

The families I am describing are not estranged. They are not cold. Many of them see each other constantly, text every day, show up for every birthday, and would tell you without hesitation that they are close. And they are close. That is what makes this worth examining rather than worth scolding.

What they do not do is talk about the things that actually determine what happens to them.

Not the money, though that is one. What each person expects. What somebody has been carrying alone for fifteen years. What one child assumed about a decision the parents made in 2009. Whether the plan everybody is nodding at is one anybody actually agreed to. Whether the thing that happened at the funeral is still sitting there.

Those conversations do not get had. And the reason is almost never that people do not care.

Here is what I have watched instead. Somebody thinks about raising it. They run the conversation forward in their head, and they can see how it might go, and they decide the moment is not right. Not never. Just not now. And then the season passes, and there is a wedding, and then a health scare, and then a good Christmas that nobody wants to spoil.

The delay is not avoidance in the way we usually mean that word. It is anticipation. People are not refusing the conversation. They are waiting for a version of it they can predict, and that version does not exist.

Meanwhile something else is happening, and this is the part I most want you to see. The conversation you are not having is not neutral ground. Silence is not a pause. Everyone in your family is drawing conclusions in the absence of information, and those conclusions harden. The longer nobody says anything, the more each person becomes certain about a version of the story nobody has confirmed.

So when the conversation finally does happen, and it will, it is no longer one conversation. It is one conversation plus eleven years of accumulated interpretation, and all of that has to be dealt with before anybody can get to the actual subject.

That is what makes waiting expensive. Not that the topic gets harder. That the topic collects things.

Paul tells the church at Ephesus to lay aside falsehood and speak truth with a neighbor. It is worth noticing that he does not say speak truth to strangers, or to enemies. He says neighbor. The person you see all the time. The one you are closest to and therefore most practiced at managing.

Those are the hardest people to be plain with, because you have so much more to lose and so many more chances to do it later.

This week is about why that happens. Not to fix it yet. To name it.

# Day 2

## Keeping the Peace

### They're keeping their mouth shut

**"Better is open rebuke than love that is concealed."** PROVERBS 27:5

They are trying to keep peace, so they are keeping their mouth shut.

That is what it looks like from the outside when a family is not talking. It does not look like conflict. It looks like a very pleasant dinner where a lot of people are being careful.

And I want to give the people doing it their due, because keeping quiet is usually an act of love rather than cowardice. Somebody decides that the relationship matters more than the point, and they swallow it. That is not a bad instinct. It is a good instinct applied in the wrong place, and it works beautifully in the short run.

The trouble is that it does not scale. What you can do once at a holiday you cannot do for twenty years, because the things you swallow do not dissolve. They accumulate, and they change the person doing the swallowing.

I have seen this most often in the same two positions in a family.

The first is the person who is not the loudest. In most families there is somebody who is easy to talk over, and everybody knows who it is, and nobody thinks of it as a problem because that person never objects. They never object because objecting has not gone well before.

The second is the in-law. Somebody married into this family nine years ago with a whole set of values from a different house, and has never once been asked what they think, and has correctly read the room as a place where offering it would be presumptuous.

Both of those people are keeping the peace. Both are being generous. And the family is poorer for it in a way nobody can quite name.

The proverb goes further than I would dare to. It says open rebuke is *better* than concealed love. Not equal. Better.

That is a hard sentence, and I do not think it is an endorsement of bluntness. I think it is a claim about what love is for. Love that stays concealed to protect a pleasant evening has bought something real at a price that is easy to miss, which is that the other person never gets the benefit of it.

So here is the question worth sitting with today. In your family, who is keeping the peace?

And a harder one behind it. Are you?

Because most of us can identify the quiet person at the table. Fewer of us notice the thing we ourselves have been carefully not saying for years, since from the inside it does not feel like silence. It feels like maturity.

# Day 3

## Everything Stays Assumed

### Without that, nobody actually knows

**"The first to plead his case seems right, until another comes and examines him."** PROVERBS 18:17

Without that conversation, everything stays just kind of assumed.

That is a small sentence and it describes a very large problem, so let me slow down on it.

An assumption in a family is not a guess. A guess knows it is a guess. An assumption has been promoted to a fact, quietly, over years, by nothing more than the absence of anybody contradicting it.

And the striking thing is how confident people are. If you sit with a family and ask each person separately what the others think, you will get answers delivered without hesitation. Oh, he would never want that. She has always felt that way about it. They are not interested, they have said so.

Then you ask the person themselves and the answer is different. Sometimes mildly. Sometimes not at all what anybody expected.

Nobody lied. Nobody was withholding. The family simply built a working model of each other twenty years ago and has been running it ever since without updating it, because updating requires asking, and asking requires an occasion, and the occasion never comes.

Here is where it does real damage. Assumptions do not stay in your head. They shape behavior.

If you assume your daughter is not interested in the business, you stop mentioning it. She notices that you never mention it and concludes she was not wanted. Neither of you has said a word and now there is a fact between you that neither of you created on purpose.

Multiply that by four children, two in-laws, and thirty years, and you have a family operating on a dense web of unchecked conclusions, all of them delivered with total confidence.

The proverb names something we all know and rarely apply at home. The first account sounds right until somebody examines it. In a courtroom that is obvious. In a family we skip the examination entirely, because examining sounds like distrust.

It is not distrust. It is the ordinary work of staying current with people who keep changing.

And the fix is not complicated, which is what makes this frustrating. It is one question, asked directly, of the person it is about. Not about them. Of them.

I would go so far as to say that most of what gets called a family conflict is actually two people defending positions neither of them has verified.

# Day 4

## A Better Time Later

### But later doesn't always come

**"You do not know what your life will be like tomorrow. You are just a vapor that appears for a little while and then vanishes away."** JAMES 4:14

People assume there will be a better time later. And later does not always come.

I have sat with families who were having the conversation for the first time in a hospital corridor, and I want to say plainly that it is a bad place to have it. Not because anything is impossible there. Because everything is compressed, and grief is present, and decisions that deserved two years are being made in an afternoon by people who are not at their best.

Nobody planned that. Every one of those families intended to talk earlier. The intention was real. The calendar was just always slightly wrong.

So let me name the thing that gets in the way, because it is not procrastination in the ordinary sense.

It is that there is genuinely no natural moment. Nothing in ordinary life produces an occasion for this. Birthdays are celebratory. Holidays are crowded. Weddings belong to someone else. The random Tuesday feels arbitrary and slightly alarming, because if Dad calls a meeting on a Tuesday, everybody assumes somebody is sick.

That is a real problem and it has a boring solution, which is that you have to manufacture the occasion. It will feel artificial. It is supposed to. Every important conversation your family has ever had that was not triggered by a crisis was manufactured by somebody.

There is a second thing worth saying, and it is gentler.

Waiting often is not about timing at all. It is about readiness, and specifically about not feeling ready to answer the questions that will follow. People delay the conversation because they have not decided yet, and they do not want to open something they cannot close.

If that is you, here is the release. You do not have to have decided. You are allowed to open a conversation with *I have been thinking about this and I do not know the answer yet, and I wanted you to be part of it before I did.*

That sentence is easier to say than the finished version, and in my experience families receive it better, because being brought in early is a different gift than being informed late.

James calls a life a vapor. He is not being morbid, he is being accurate about scale. The point is not that you might die tomorrow. The point is that you do not control the schedule, and you have been planning as though you do.

# Day 5

## It Does Not Get Simpler

### The stakes tend to get higher

**"There is a time to be silent and a time to speak."** ECCLESIASTES 3:7

Conversations that could have been simple earlier are rarely simple later. The stakes tend to get higher.

This is the closing thought of the first session, and it is the one I would most want a family to take away, because it corrects the arithmetic most people are doing.

The common assumption is that waiting is neutral. That the conversation sitting on the shelf is the same conversation whenever you take it down. It is not. It changes while it sits, and it changes in one direction.

Three things happen.

**The subject accumulates.** What began as a single question about one decision picks up adjacent material. Now it is that decision, plus how the decision was communicated, plus what somebody said about it in 2016, plus the fact that nobody has mentioned it since.

**The positions harden.** People do not hold an unexamined view loosely. They hold it more tightly over time, because they have been living as though it were true and have built things on top of it. Ten years in, you are not asking somebody to consider something. You are asking them to revise a decade.

**And the cast changes.** Somebody gets married. Somebody has children. Somebody gets sick. Every year the conversation waits, more people are affected by it and fewer of them were present for the history.

None of that is a reason for panic. It is a reason for arithmetic.

Now, Ecclesiastes says there is a time to be silent as well as a time to speak, and I do not want to flatten that. There genuinely are moments when the loving thing is to say nothing. Grief has its own calendar. Some things need to sit.

But I would ask you to be honest about which one you are actually in, because the same silence serves both, and only you know whether you are practicing restraint or postponing.

Here is a test I have found useful. Restraint has an end date. If you are waiting for something specific and could name it, you are exercising judgment. If you could not say what you are waiting for, or if the answer is some version of a better moment, you are postponing, and the arithmetic above is running.

So end this first session with the plainest question in the whole book.

What are you waiting for, and is it a reason or is it a habit?
