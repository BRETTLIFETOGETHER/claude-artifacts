# Day 16

## Direct, Truthful, Respectful

### All three at once, or it does damage

**"Let your statement be, 'Yes, yes' or 'No, no'; anything beyond these is of evil."** MATTHEW 5:37

There are three keys to communication that we come back to constantly.

Be direct. Be truthful. Be respectful.

Any two of them without the third does damage, and I want to walk through the three failures because most people can immediately identify which one is theirs.

**Direct and truthful without respect is cruelty.** It says the true thing, plainly, and leaves somebody diminished. People who do this often think of themselves as honest and are usually surprised to learn that their family experiences them as unsafe.

**Truthful and respectful without directness never actually gets said.** This is the most common failure in the families I work with. Everything is kind, everything is accurate, and the point is buried so deep that nobody can find it. Years pass. The person believes they have addressed something they have only ever gestured at.

**Direct and respectful without truth is pleasant and useless.** It is the conversation that goes well and changes nothing, because the actual thing was never on the table.

All three, at once, is the work.

Now, families tend to have a house style, and it is usually one of these three. Some families are blunt and wound each other. Some are gracious and never say anything. Some are warm and superficial.

Whichever yours is, it was probably set by one person a long time ago and everyone else adapted to it.

Jesus says let your yes be yes and your no be no, and that anything beyond that is of evil. It is a striking standard, and what strikes me is how much of ordinary family communication would fail it. Not through lying. Through hedging, implying, hinting, and hoping the other person infers.

That is the territory the verse is describing, and it names it more harshly than we would.

So today is diagnostic rather than instructional. Which of the three do you drop?

Because everyone drops one, and knowing which is most of the correction.

**1.** Which of the three do you personally drop most often?

**2.** What is your family's house style, and who set it?

**3.** Where has kindness kept you from being direct?

**4.** Where has directness cost somebody their dignity?

# Day 17

## Suspect Yourself

### We all think we've got it under control

**"Search me, God, and know my heart; put me to the test and know my anxious thoughts."** PSALM 139:23

There is a book called *Knowing God* by J.I. Packer, and near the end he talks about decision-making. One of his points has stayed with me for years.

Suspect yourself.

I thought that was a great comment, because we all think we have got it under control.

Here is where it applies most in a family.

When you are interacting with your children, especially adult children, suspect yourself. Am I becoming overpowering here? Is this a conversation, or am I steering it toward a conclusion I decided on before I sat down?

Because the honest answer is frequently the second one, and the person you are talking to knows it before you do.

I want to be precise about the mechanism, because this is not about being domineering. Most of the people I am describing would be horrified to be called that.

It is subtler. You have thirty years more experience. You have almost certainly thought about this longer. And you may well be right, which is the part that makes it dangerous, because being right is what licenses you to stop listening.

A conversation where one person already has the answer is not a conversation. It is a delivery with question marks in it, and your family can tell the difference immediately.

There is a second application that is harder. Suspect your reading of other people.

You have a picture of each person in your family and you are confident in it. That confidence was earned over decades of observation, and it is also probably several years out of date, and you have never checked it because checking feels unnecessary when you are sure.

Sureness is the condition under which people are most often wrong about their families.

David asks God to search him and know his anxious thoughts. It is a request to have something found that he could not find himself, which presumes that self-examination alone has limits.

That is exactly what suspecting yourself means. Not self-doubt. An awareness that your own view of yourself is the one view you cannot independently verify.

**4.** Would your family say you are open, or that you are polite about being closed?

# Day 18

## Vain Imaginations

### What somebody else is thinking

**"We are destroying arguments and every lofty thing raised up against the knowledge of God, and we are taking every thought captive."** 2 CORINTHIANS 10:5

A friend of mine used to call them vain imaginations, and I have not found a better phrase.

Here is what one looks like.

You and I are talking. Joe walks up. We stop talking.

Now Joe is wondering whether we were talking about him. Is that why we stopped?

And the reality is that we had simply finished the sentence. But Joe now has a vain imagination, and unless something contradicts it, it will sit there.

Multiply that by a family and thirty years.

Somebody was not invited to something and concluded it was deliberate. Somebody's call was not returned and concluded something about their standing. A decision was made without consultation and got read as a judgment about competence.

None of those conclusions was checked. All of them are now operating as facts, and behavior has been built on top of them.

There is a cousin to this that is worse, which is catastrophe thinking. It sounds like: this worries me, my parents just hate me, my sister always discounts me. It takes a single data point and builds an entire architecture on it.

What makes both so difficult in a family is that nobody says them out loud. A vain imagination is embarrassing to admit, because saying it exposes the insecurity underneath it. So it stays private and hardens.

And here is the part that should get your attention. You have some. Everyone does. You are carrying at least one conclusion about somebody in your family that you have never verified and are quite confident about.

Paul talks about taking every thought captive. It is usually applied to temptation, and it applies just as directly here.

A thought that has not been taken captive runs on its own. It generates supporting evidence, it resists contradiction, and eventually it becomes something you know rather than something you assumed.

Taking it captive means holding it long enough to ask whether it is true.

Which in a family is usually one question, asked directly, of the person it is about.

### Reflect

**1.** What have you concluded about somebody that you have never checked?

**2.** How long have you carried it?

**3.** What behavior have you built on top of it?

**4.** What might somebody be assuming about you?

# Day 19

## Catastrophe Thinking

### My parents just hate me

**"Do not fear, for I am with you; do not anxiously look about you, for I am your God."** ISAIAH 41:10

Catastrophe thinking is the older sibling of a vain imagination, and it does more damage.

A vain imagination is a wrong conclusion. Catastrophe thinking is a wrong conclusion built into a whole structure, usually about somebody's standing in the family.

It sounds like this. My parents just hate me. My sister always discounts me. Nobody in this family has ever taken me seriously.

Notice the words. Just. Always. Never. Nobody.

Those absolutes are the tell, and they are almost never accurate. But they are extremely stable, because once you hold one, every subsequent event gets sorted into it. A missed call becomes evidence. A short reply becomes evidence. A thing that was genuinely nothing becomes another entry in a file.

I have sat with families where two people held mirror-image catastrophes about each other, each accumulating evidence for years, neither having said a word.

Here is why this belongs in a communication book rather than a psychology one.

Catastrophe thinking is what fills the space when communication stops. It is not a personality defect. It is what a reasonable mind does with insufficient information about people who matter enormously to it.

Which means the remedy is not to think better. It is to have more information, and information in a family comes from being told things.

I want to name who is most vulnerable, because it is not who people expect. It is not the most anxious person. It is the person furthest from the center. The one who moved away. The one who married in. The one who is not in the business. Distance produces information scarcity, and information scarcity is what catastrophe thinking runs on.

Isaiah says do not anxiously look about you. It is a specific instruction about a specific posture, which is scanning for evidence of threat.

Scanning is what catastrophe thinking is. And the reason given for stopping is not that the scanning is inaccurate. It is that God is present, which addresses the fear underneath rather than the conclusions on top of it.

For a family, the practical version is simpler. Tell people things. The catastrophe cannot survive information.

# Day 20

## Would You Forgive Me

### That last ten percent

**"Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, so that you may be healed."** JAMES 5:16

When you mess up, you have got to admit that you messed up. And you need to ask people to forgive you for the way that you acted.

That last part is where most people stop short, and Brett calls it the last ten percent.

Here is what the first ninety percent looks like, and it is genuinely good. I am sorry that happened. I did not handle that well. I should not have said it that way.

All of that is real and all of it is easier than the ten percent, because none of it requires the other person to do anything.

The last ten percent is a question. Would you forgive me?

And a question is different because it hands something over. The other person now has a decision, and you have to wait for it, and they might say something other than yes, or say yes in a way that is not finished.

That vulnerability is the entire point. It is also why we avoid it.

I want to say something about what this does in a family, because it is disproportionate to the effort.

When a parent asks an adult child for forgiveness, something reorders. Not because the offense was necessarily large. Because the asking establishes that this is a relationship between two adults where accounts can be settled, rather than a hierarchy where the older person's actions are simply weather.

I have watched families change on that sentence. Not on a conversation about values. On a father asking a thirty-eight-year-old to forgive him for something from years earlier.

There is a caution. Asking forgiveness is not a technique and it will not work as one. If it is used to close a subject rather than to open a relationship, people can tell, and it costs more than it gains.

James says confess to one another and pray for one another so that you may be healed. The healing is attached to the mutuality. Not confess and be forgiven. Confess to one another.

Which means somebody has to go first, and in most families the person with the most standing to go first is the one who finds it hardest.

## You're Not Just Talking About Money

### Value, responsibility, fairness, and sometimes identity

**"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger."** PROVERBS 15:1

When a family conversation turns to money, something changes in the room, and it is worth understanding why.

You are not just talking about money.

You are talking about value, and responsibility, and fairness, and sometimes about identity. Money is the surface of the conversation and almost never the substance of it.

Here is how that shows up. Two siblings disagree about a decision, and it appears to be about an amount. It is not. One of them is asking whether the years they spent nearby counted for something. The other is asking whether being the one who left made them less part of this family.

Neither of them will say that. Both of them will argue about the amount, because the amount is the only part that is sayable.

Which is why these conversations escalate in a way that seems disproportionate to whatever is being discussed. It is not disproportionate. It is proportionate to the actual subject, which nobody has named.

So the first move in a hard conversation is not to negotiate. It is to find out what it is actually about.

That takes one question and some patience. What would this mean to you? Not what do you want. What would it mean.

The answers are usually not financial.

There is a second thing that happens with money specifically. People become different, and not better. Positions harden. Old material resurfaces with new evidence attached. Fair-mindedness gets harder to sustain.

I do not think the money creates the material. I think it gives buried things a reason to be raised.

The proverb about a gentle answer turning away wrath is the most practical verse in this session. It is not about being nice. It is about what a response does to the temperature of a room, and the claim is causal. The answer determines the next thing that happens.

In a conversation about money, where the real subject is identity, a harsh answer does not just fail. It confirms whatever the other person feared.

So the instruction is narrower than it sounds. Slow down, find out what it is about, and answer gently even when you are right.

# Day 22

## Security for One, Opportunity for Another

### Even within the same family

**"Now there are various kinds of gifts, but the same Spirit."** 1 CORINTHIANS 12:4

The same amount of money means completely different things to different people in one family.

For one person it is security. For another it is opportunity. For a third it is responsibility they did not ask for. For a fourth it is a scorecard.

None of them is wrong, and none of them knows the others hold a different meaning.

I want to draw that out, because it explains a great deal of family conflict that looks like disagreement and is actually mistranslation.

The person for whom money is security hears any proposal to deploy it as a threat. Their objection sounds like caution, and it is fear.

The person for whom it is opportunity hears any proposal to preserve it as waste. Their objection sounds like ambition, and it is often impatience.

The person for whom it is responsibility hears every conversation as an assignment. They are the one who goes quiet, and their silence gets read as agreement.

And the person for whom it is a scorecard is not tracking money at all. They are tracking whether they matter as much, and no amount settles that question because it was never a financial one.

Put those four in a room and give them one decision. They will not be able to agree, and they will each conclude that the others are being unreasonable, and every one of them will be behaving rationally given what money means to them.

The fix is not compromise. Compromise between mistranslated positions produces a decision nobody wanted.

The fix is that each person says what it means to them, out loud, before anybody proposes anything. That single step changes what the conversation is about.

Paul writes that there are varieties of gifts but the same Spirit. He is addressing a church that was ranking each other, and the argument is not that the differences are unimportant. It is that they are not a hierarchy.

The same applies here. The person who wants security is not more prudent than the person who wants opportunity. They are differently made, and a family that knows that can work with it.

A family that does not will keep having the same argument.

# Day 23

## Is There Anyone?

### That's why we ask a lot of questions

**"If your brother sins, go and show him his fault in private."** MATTHEW 18:15

There is one question I ask early with a family, and it does more than anything else on this list.

Is there anyone in your family that you have an issue with?

I ask it because I want to know before I end up sitting across from that person, and because in almost every family the answer is yes and nobody has ever been asked.

Brett tried it with his own family and one person looked at him and said, yeah. My mom.

And then you are in it, which is exactly the point.

Here is why this question is worth the discomfort.

Unresolved things in a family do not stay the size they started. They accumulate interpretation. The original event gets smaller in memory and the meaning around it grows, until eventually the thing being carried has very little to do with what happened.

And it will surface. It surfaces at a wedding, or at a hospital, or during a conversation about money, and it arrives at full size in a moment that cannot hold it.

Asking the question does not resolve anything. It makes the thing sayable, which is the necessary first step and is frequently mistaken for the whole job.

A warning about doing this well. If you ask and then defend, you have made it worse than not asking. The person has taken a risk and learned that the risk does not pay. They will not take it twice.

So if you ask, your only job in the next five minutes is to understand. Not to explain what actually happened. Not to provide context. Understand.

Jesus says if your brother sins against you, go to him privately. The word privately is doing a lot of work. Not to the family. Not to a sibling first. To the person.

Most families do the opposite, which is the subject of a later day.

For now the instruction is smaller. Find out whether anything is there. In most families it is, and in most families it has never been named, and naming it is the whole of today.

## Adult Children Is an Oxymoron

### Are you treating them as adults, or as children?

**"When I was a child, I used to speak like a child, think like a child, reason like a child; when I became a man, I did away with childish things."** 1 CORINTHIANS 13:11

We use the phrase adult children constantly, and it is really an oxymoron.

Are they adults, or are they children? Because how you answer determines how you talk to a forty-year-old.

I put this in the hard conversations session on purpose, because it is the single largest factor in whether a hard conversation with a grown child goes well.

Here is the pattern. A parent raises something difficult. The tone, without anyone intending it, is the tone used with a fifteen-year-old. Explanatory. Slightly patient. Pre-decided.

And the adult child responds the way they responded at fifteen, which is either compliance or resistance, and neither is the response of an adult, and the parent concludes that this child still cannot handle a serious conversation.

The conversation confirmed exactly what created it.

What makes this hard to see is that the parent is not wrong about knowing more. They usually do know more, about this subject, with more experience behind it. The knowledge asymmetry is real.

But the knowledge asymmetry is not the relationship. Two adults can have very different amounts of knowledge and still be two adults. It happens in every professional context in the world.

Here is a practical test. Before a hard conversation with an adult child, ask yourself how you would raise this with a colleague you respected who knew less than you about the subject.

You would explain your reasoning rather than your conclusion. You would ask what they thought before telling them what you thought. You would leave open the possibility of being persuaded.

Do that, and the conversation changes before anybody speaks.

Paul describes a transition from childish speech and reasoning to something else, and notice who performs the action. He does. I did away with childish things.

The transition is theirs to make. But you can prevent it indefinitely by continuing to address a person as the thing they are trying not to be.

# Day 25

## Slow It Down

### If things move too quickly, people react instead of thinking

**"Everyone must be quick to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger."** JAMES 1:19

This is the last day of the hard conversations session, and the instruction is a single word.

Slower.

When a conversation moves too quickly, people react rather than think. And a reaction, in a family, tends to be the thing everybody remembers afterward.

Here is what speed does in a hard conversation.

Somebody says something difficult. The instinct is to respond immediately, because silence feels like agreement or defeat. So a response arrives before any thinking has happened, and it is a defense of a position rather than an engagement with what was said.

That defense triggers a defense, and now the conversation is two people protecting territory, and neither of them will be able to reconstruct afterward how it got there.

The whole thing took about ninety seconds.

So slowing down is not politeness. It is the mechanism by which a hard conversation stays a conversation.

There are three practical ways to do it and none of them are complicated.

**Wait before responding.** Four or five seconds after somebody finishes. It feels endless and it changes what you say.

**Say what you heard before you say what you think.** Not as a technique. Genuinely check. *So what you are saying is this.* Half the time you will be wrong, which is useful information you would not otherwise have had.

**End before it is finished.** This is the one families resist. If a conversation is getting hot, stopping and setting a time to continue is not failure. It is the thing that makes continuing possible.

I have seen a family stop a conversation twenty minutes in, agree to come back to it in a week, and have a completely different conversation the second time. Nothing changed except the temperature.

James says quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger. Two of the three are about slowing down and only one is about speed.

That ratio is the instruction.

That is the end of this session. The last one is about what makes any of it last.

# Day 26

## Not a One and Done

### Trust is something you build on over time

**"Let us not become weary in doing good, for in due time we will reap if we do not become weary."** GALATIANS 6:9

Trust is not a one-time thing. It is something you build on over time.

I say that about wills and I say it about conversations, and the mechanism is identical.

Families treat a good conversation the way they treat a completed document. It happened. It went well. That subject is now handled.

But a conversation is not a deliverable. It is a deposit, and deposits only compound if there are more of them.

Here is what actually happens after one good conversation, and I have watched it many times.

The conversation goes well. Everyone leaves encouraged. There is a genuine sense that something shifted, and something did.

Then nothing happens for fourteen months.

And what decays in those fourteen months is not the content of the conversation. Everyone remembers it. What decays is the permission. Right afterward, everybody knew that this family could talk about things. A year later, nobody is sure anymore, because there is no evidence and the evidence is what permission runs on.

So the second conversation matters more than the first, and it matters most when there is nothing in particular to discuss.

That is the counterintuitive part. A family that only talks when there is a reason has taught itself that talking means something is wrong. A family that talks when there is no reason has made the hard conversation possible, because it does not require an occasion.

Paul says do not become weary in doing good, and the reason given is a harvest that comes in due time. The word due is doing something. It is not immediate and it is not on your schedule.

Which is the actual difficulty with consistency. You are asked to keep doing something without visible return for an unspecified period.

That is why intensity is more attractive than consistency. Intensity produces a feeling of progress. Consistency produces the actual result and produces almost no feeling at all along the way.

# Day 27

## Repeated Conversations Create Culture

### One conversation can create clarity

**"These words which I am commanding you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your sons and speak of them when you sit in your house."** DEUTERONOMY 6:6-7

One conversation can create clarity. Repeated conversations create culture.

I want to be precise about the difference, because families aim at the first and need the second.

Clarity is a state. After a good conversation, everybody knows something they did not know before. That is genuinely valuable and it is also perishable, because knowing something is not the same as living differently.

Culture is what a family does without deciding to. It is the set of things that happen by default, without anyone proposing them.

And culture is built by repetition rather than by insight. Nobody has ever changed a family's culture with one excellent conversation, however good it was.

Here is what repetition does that a single conversation cannot.

It removes the significance. The first time a family talks about something difficult, the conversation itself is an event, and events carry weight that makes people careful. By the fourth or fifth time, it is just a thing this family does, and people say more because less is riding on it.

It also distributes the initiation. In most families the first conversation is called by one person. By the fifth, somebody else has raised something, and that is the moment a culture actually exists rather than a person with a habit.

The Deuteronomy passage is the clearest description of culture in Scripture, and what strikes me is how unstructured it is. When you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, when you rise up.

That is not a curriculum. It is a description of ordinary life with something running through it constantly.

Families read that passage and hear an instruction to have family devotions, which is fine. What it actually describes is something more ambitious and less scheduled, which is a subject that comes up in the car.

That is what culture is. Not the meeting. What gets talked about when nobody planned to.

# Day 28

## Consistency, Not Intensity

### The long-term outcome is shaped by consistency

**"He who is faithful in a very little thing is also faithful in much."** LUKE 16:10

If I had to reduce this whole book to one sentence, it might be this.

The long-term outcome is shaped by consistency, not intensity.

Families badly want it to be the other way, and I understand why.

Intensity is available. You can schedule a weekend. You can bring somebody in. You can have the big conversation, and it will be memorable and probably good.

Consistency is not available in the same way. You cannot do a lot of it at once. It only happens at the rate of one small thing at a time, which means the result is always further away than you would like.

So families choose intensity, and then are puzzled when the effect fades.

Here is the honest comparison. I have watched two families with nearly identical starting points. Similar resources, similar structures, similar goodwill. Both had a strong first conversation.

The difference was entirely what happened afterward.

One family treated it as an event that had been completed. The other treated it as the first instance of something. Nothing dramatic followed in either case, and five years later they were in completely different places.

It was not knowledge. It was not resources. It was not even intention, because both families intended the same thing.

It was follow-through, which is the least interesting virtue and the one that actually determines outcomes.

Now, I do not want to be discouraging about intensity. A good weekend is worth having. It just cannot be the strategy, and it is frequently used as a way of buying permission not to do the small things.

Jesus says whoever is faithful in a very little thing is faithful also in much. It is said about money and it describes something more general, which is that character shows up at small scale and does not appear from nowhere at large scale.

Your family's ability to have a hard conversation in five years is being built right now, in whether the ordinary ones happen.

# Day 29

## No Visible Conflict

### Assuming things are fine because nothing is wrong

**"They have healed the brokenness of My people superficially, saying, 'Peace, peace,' but there is no peace."** JEREMIAH 6:14

Here is the most common reason a family stops having these conversations.

Nothing is wrong.

There is no visible conflict. Everyone is getting along. The holidays were pleasant. And in the absence of a problem, the conversations feel unnecessary, so they quietly stop.

I want to name what is happening there, because it is not laziness.

Families use conflict as their signal. If something is wrong, we address it. If nothing is wrong, we enjoy it. That is a reasonable-sounding policy and it has one enormous flaw, which is that it only ever produces conversations under bad conditions.

By the time there is visible conflict, the conversation is much harder than it would have been. Positions have formed. Something has already been said. Somebody is defended.

The best conditions for a hard conversation are precisely the conditions under which families conclude they do not need one.

There is a second version of this that is more subtle. Some families have no visible conflict because the conflict has gone somewhere else. It is not absent, it is submerged, and submerged conflict produces a very convincing peace.

The tell is not tension. It is a slight carefulness. Certain subjects that do not come up. A person who is a little quieter than they used to be. Nothing you could point to.

Jeremiah says something severe about people who heal a wound superficially and declare peace where there is none. It is directed at leaders who preferred a calm surface to an honest diagnosis.

That is a strong charge and I do not want to over-apply it. Most families with a quiet surface are genuinely fine.

But some are not, and the ones that are not look identical from outside, which is why the question has to be asked rather than assumed.

So the diagnostic for today is simple and slightly uncomfortable.

Is your family peaceful, or is it quiet?

## Set the Next One

### Just continue the process

**"Let us consider how to encourage one another in love and good deeds, not forsaking our own assembling together."** HEBREWS 10:24-25

This is the last day, and the instruction is the least profound thing in the entire book.

Set the next one.

That is it. Before you close this, put something on a calendar.

I end here deliberately, because everything else in these thirty days is available to a family that will not do this one thing, and none of it will matter.

You could understand every barrier. You could master the pause. You could name your assumptions and ask the hard question and say the true thing well. And if there is no next conversation, all of it is a good month you had once.

Here is the shape of what actually works, and it is unimpressive.

A time that repeats. Short enough that nobody dreads it. Frequent enough that nobody has to remember how. And it happens whether or not there is anything to discuss, because that is precisely what makes it possible to discuss something when there is.

For some families that is a monthly call. For some it is a walk. For some it is thirty minutes at the end of a meal that was already happening.

The form does not matter. The repetition does.

And one more thing, which is the hardest for the person reading this.

You will probably have to be the one who sets it, more than once, without being thanked, and possibly without anyone else ever taking a turn.

That is not a failure. Somebody in every family goes first and frequently keeps going first, and the fact that it is uneven does not make it less worth doing.

Hebrews says let us consider how to encourage one another, and not forsake assembling together. The word consider is interesting. It means give thought to it, plan it, work out how.

Encouragement is being described as something requiring forethought rather than something that happens when people feel warmly toward each other.

That is the whole of it. Give thought to how, and then do not stop meeting.

So close the book and set a date.
