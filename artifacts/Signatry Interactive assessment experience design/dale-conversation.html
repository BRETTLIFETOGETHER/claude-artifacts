Preparation · The conversation with Dale Armstrong

Ten questions, and the three that decide everything.

The twenty-five thousand is approved. The next ask is two hundred fifty thousand for the build, inside a ten-year vision of roughly two and a half million. Against a $2.5 billion giving goal, the first is one hundredth of one percent and the whole thing is one tenth of one percent. Say that early. It moves the room from can we afford this to what would actually move the two and a half billion, which is the only question his leadership team cares about.

Questions one, five and eight are the ones to get answered. They determine the metric, the case and the decision path — and the page Dale carries upstairs cannot be written until they are answered in his words rather than ours. The other seven are useful. These three are load-bearing.

The ten

1
What has to be true in five years for The Signatry to reach $2.5 billion — and which of those things do you not yet have a system for?Load-bearing. Whatever he names is the thing being funded, and it will be his language when he carries it upstairs.
2
Where do the donated dollars come from today — advisors, families direct, business exits?You cannot design distribution without the current mix. If most of it arrives through advisors, the advisor play is the strategy and everything else is decoration.
3
What happens after a family funds the account? Who owns that relationship, and what do you send them?The softest spot at almost every donor-advised sponsor, and exactly where forty days of formation belongs.
4
How many advisory firms send you flow, and what would it take to double that?Tells you whether the constraint is advisor acquisition or advisor activation. Different problems, different products.
5
Of the families you serve, how many would you call formed — the next generation engaged, the money carrying a purpose beyond the tax event?Load-bearing. The number will be small. That number is the case for the entire build.
6
What already exists internally that we should build on rather than duplicate?Ask for their plans, content and tools. You avoid rebuilding what they have, and you learn how serious the internal roadmap already is.
7
When the team says you cannot crack how to reach these families — what has been tried, and what did it cost?Keeps you from proposing what they burned money on last year. What they spent failing is roughly what they will spend succeeding.
8
If we proved this with a hundred families and a hundred advisors, what would that unlock — and who has to see the proof?Load-bearing. The pilot question and the political question at once. Tells you whether Dale can approve this or whether it goes to a board.
9
Where would funding come from — operating budget, a donor who cares about this specifically, or a family that would fund the build?He may lack the budget and still have three families who would write the check. That turns a cost conversation into a fundraising one.
10
If this works, how do we structure it so The Signatry is not funding it forever?Where licensing and revenue share enter — asked as stewardship of their dollars. Ask it and you are a partner. Wait to be asked and you are a vendor.

Hold one sentence, not twenty initiatives

Dale cannot carry twenty things to a leadership team. He can carry one: The Signatry becomes where a Christian family goes to find out what to do next — not just where the money sits. Everything else is an instrument of that or a later phase.

Bring back three things

His answer to one, his number for five, and the names from eight. Those three write the leadership page overleaf. Without them we would be authoring his vision for him, which is the one thing that loses the room.

The page his leadership team actually needs.

Everything below is finished except three slots. Each slot is filled with what Dale says, not what we would say for him — which is what makes it survive a leadership meeting he attends and we do not.

Slot one — the gap, in his words  ·  from question 1

The one thing that has to be true for $2.5 billion and has no system behind it today.

Slot two — the number  ·  from question 5

How many families they would call formed, against how many they serve. The gap between those two figures is the case.

Slot three — who decides, and what proof they need  ·  from question 8

Names, and the evidence each one requires before a yes.

What $250,000 buys. The assessment and family profile built to production standard, the advisor edition and co-branded licensing layer, the first tranche of the toolbox, and the pilot run with the hundred and the hundred — instrumented, so the number in slot two can be measured again at the end rather than estimated.

The proportion, stated plainly

$250,000 is one hundredth of one percent of a $2.5 billion goal. The full ten-year build is one tenth of one percent. No leadership team declines on arithmetic; they decline on whether the thing works. So the page argues the pilot, not the price.

What is deliberately not on this page

Whispers, the Kingdom Impact Network, the church model, the book series, the wider advisor field. All real, all later. Introducing partners before there is proof spends credibility on a concept.

The ask on this page is one decision: fund the pilot, name the hundred advisors the way Steve is naming the forty families, and measure it in the spring. Everything larger follows from a number that either moved or did not.
