01
Executive Summary

Almost every instruction that mattered in Scripture would have failed a reasonable review on the day it was given.

Fishermen drop their nets in the middle of a workday. A widow cooks the last of her flour for a stranger. A commander sends most of his army home before the battle. Jesus walks toward the cross rather than away from it. None of it adds up, and that is not a flaw in the obedience — it is the evidence of it. The people who obeyed were not being unreasonable. They were working from a larger set of facts than the people watching them.

Irrational is the word the watching world reaches for. Scripture reaches for three better ones: surrender, honor, faith.

Irrational Obedience is a forty-day formation experience, in print and digital, built on one idea: God often calls His people to obey before they understand. Forty biblical narratives. Forty contemporary stories. Six teaching sessions with Steve. A daily digital journey, an obedience assessment, and a family legacy profile.

It is not primarily a book. A book is finished when it is read. This is a repeatable discipleship engine — a system a family runs together, an advisor runs with clients, and a congregation runs as a season — and it is built so that the second thing made from it costs a fraction of the first.

The whole design turns on forty days. Short enough that a family will actually finish it. Long enough that something moves. Six stages, six weeks, one conversation at the table each week, and a household that arrives at the far end using words it did not have before.

Irrational Obedience is the working title. Living Obedience is the experience that follows — the yes worked out over a life. They are first and second rather than one or the other, and the final naming belongs to the author.

Vision and the Problem

To help families and leaders develop a culture of courageous, immediate, generational obedience.

Transformation happens when faith becomes practical rather than theoretical, when obedience becomes immediate rather than delayed, when generosity becomes normal rather than exceptional, and when legacy becomes intentional rather than accidental. Every one of those is a shift from something a family agrees with to something a family does.

Three gaps stand in between.

  1. Inspiration without implementation

    People are moved by a sermon or a book and then handed nothing to do on Tuesday. The distance between conviction and action is where most formation quietly dies.

  2. Individual faith without family integration

    Nearly all spiritual formation is built for one person reading alone. Very little of it is built for a household, and almost none of it is built for three generations in the same room.

  3. Wealth and calling disconnected from obedience

    Advisors and the families they serve rarely share a spiritual framework for decisions that are, at bottom, spiritual — what a business is for, what is enough, and what the children are being prepared to carry.

None of the three is a content problem. Good content already exists in abundance. They are structure problems — nobody has built the thing that carries a conviction from a page into a household and keeps it there past February.

What the Project Delivers

Three bodies of work, built together and priced as one.

Not just physical but digital. Not just material but marketing assets. Not just a book but something built to reach more families. Not just for families but for the advisors who serve them.

A · The Forty Days

Forty biblical narratives paired with forty contemporary obedience stories. The voice is aligned to Steve throughout — it should sound like a man talking, not like a devotional. Pastoral clarity, accessible to a reader with no theological training, and a tone that works equally at a kitchen table and across an advisor's desk. Scripture in NIV, integrated rather than appended, with theological humility wherever mature Christians legitimately disagree.

The forty contemporary stories are sourced across the people this house already knows: legacy families, founders, Christian financial advisors, donor families, pastors, professional advisors, parents and grandparents. Each answers one question — what did God ask you to do that didn't make sense at the time, and what happened when you obeyed? We interview, transcribe, shape each to roughly four hundred words, and clear permissions in writing before anything is printed.

A specimen day follows. It is shown for position: the final text, trim and design are developed with the author.

The manuscript is delivered in five blocks of eight days rather than as one finished draft, so the voice can be corrected at day eight instead of at day forty.

Stage Three · SurrenderedDay 21

What Are You Still Holding?

“Go, sell everything you have and give to the poor.” — Mark 10:21, NIV

He was not a bad man. He was the kind of man any congregation would be glad to have — moral, successful, genuinely searching. He asked the right question, and he asked it of the right person. And Mark records something easy to miss: Jesus looked at him and loved him. What followed was not a rebuke. It was a diagnosis.

The one thing he could not release turned out to be the one thing that had a grip on him. That is the uncomfortable arithmetic of surrender. We do not discover what owns us by examining what we believe. We discover it by noticing what we will not let go of.

Most of us have an answer to that question and have never said it out loud. Saying it out loud is most of the work.

Think About It

If God asked you tomorrow for the one asset you would least like to name out loud, what would it be — and what does that tell you?

Talk About It

Ask the people at your table what they think you would find hardest to release. Their answer is usually faster than yours.

Live It

Name it. Write it on one line today, and tell one person you trust that you wrote it.

Pray

Father, I have been careful with the one thing I never offered You. I am offering it now, before I understand what You will do with it.

C · The Digital Experience

Daily delivery, not a download.

A book on a nightstand reaches one reader. The same forty days delivered to a phone each morning reaches a household, a small group, an advisor's client list and a congregation — and it tells you who is still reading on day thirty-one.

Every printed page carries the code that opens the same day, so the two formats are one experience rather than two products. A family that reads on paper and a son who reads on a phone arrive at the same table on Thursday having covered the same ground.

  • Daily delivery — the reading, the Scripture, the action and the companion clip, each morning, in the order the journey intends.
  • Video integration — by code from the page or in the app, so nothing has to be searched for.
  • Progress tracking — who is still with it on day thirty-one, by household rather than by individual reader.
  • Reflection prompts a reader keeps — what a person writes on Day 21 is still there on Day 40, which is where most of the change becomes visible to them.
  • Group and cohort views — so a host, an advisor or a pastor can run the experience without administering it by hand.

The digital layer is not a companion app. It is the distribution. Print reaches the people who buy a book. Delivery reaches everyone an advisor, a congregation or a family can invite — which is a materially larger number, and a knowable one.

The Assessment and the Family Profile

Every member answers for themselves. The family gets one picture.

One instrument, six dimensions — the same six stages the journey travels. Twenty-four short statements, about ten minutes, and the result is not a grade. It is a route: which stage to read first, which teaching to watch, and the one question to bring to your own table this week.

Each person in the family takes it individually — the founder, the spouse, the adult children, the next generation. Their results then roll up into a single family legacy profile: where the household is settled, where it is open, and — the part families almost never see stated plainly — where the generations quietly disagree. Two people can love each other and hold opposite convictions about what is enough. Most families discover that during a crisis. This surfaces it over dinner.

The Assessment · continued

What the profile makes possible.

The profile is the engine underneath everything that follows. Once a family has one on file, the system can recommend what they should do next rather than asking them to choose from a catalog — a particular stage to revisit, a tool for the decision in front of them, another journey when this one is finished.

A customized family edition

It also makes something possible that no publisher can offer: a named family, their own profile, their own transitions, their own Scriptures and questions. The Whitfield Family's Irrational Obedience Journey — assembled for them rather than selected by them.

That is not part of this project and does not need to be decided now. It is named here because it is the reason the profile is built properly the first time rather than bolted on later.

Where an advisor fits

A Christian advisor has very little to hand a family in the years before a transaction, and almost nothing genuinely about the family rather than the assets. This is something to give without asking for anything — and when a family chooses to share what came back, the conversation that follows is a different conversation entirely.

Assessment and profile results stay with the family. An advisor sees what the family chooses to share and nothing else. Results are never routed to a development office or used to rank a household, and that commitment is printed inside the instrument itself. It is what makes families willing to answer honestly, which is the only reason the instrument is worth building.

The Year-End Window

Thanksgiving through December 31 is the highest concentration of charitable decision-making in the year.

It is also the only stretch when three generations of a family are reliably under one roof. Delivering in November puts this in hand for both.

Thanksgiving Week
Soft launch · awareness activation

Early access to selected advisor partners and families. A short Gratitude and Obedience pre-launch reading for the holiday week. Advisors introduced to the framework as a year-end client engagement tool, and the first storytelling begins around legacy, gratitude and obedience.

Black Friday – Giving Tuesday
Engagement surge

Public awareness push begins. Advisors introduce the experience to their top clients. Families are invited in as a year-end spiritual reset, and assessment and profile onboarding opens.

December
Core experience window

Cohorts run the forty days. Advisors use it as a client deepening tool rather than a campaign. Families hold the weekly conversation while everyone is already gathered.

December 15–31
Giving and legacy decision window

Integration with The Signatry and the advisor networks across end-of-year giving, non-liquid asset gift planning, donor-advised fund engagement and legacy intention setting. Families complete assessments and profiles, and bring what they choose to share into conversations about giving, estate and business transition, and generational alignment.

January
The new-year cohorts

The same forty days open to groups, congregations and families who did not begin in November. No family has to wait for January, and none has to have started by then.

Nothing inside the experience itself asks anyone for anything. No appeal, no card, no giving link, no result routed anywhere the family did not send it. That is not a limitation — it is the reason an advisor can hand it to a client in December without it reading as a pitch. Generosity is a theme inside the forty days. It is never the transaction.

Strategic Impact

Five ecosystem-level outcomes, and the timing is what produces them.

  1. New client acquisition · advisors

    A differentiated spiritual-legacy engagement tool, and an entry point into new high-capacity family relationships. Something an advisor can give without asking for anything, in the season when trust and relational depth are actually built.

  2. Generational retention

    Families engage across multiple generations during holiday gatherings and come away with shared language for obedience, generosity and calling. Relationships that include the children outlast the ones that only include the parents — which is what makes both assets and relationships more likely to hold across a transfer.

  3. Platform expansion · The Signatry and the advisor ecosystem

    A repeatable year-end engagement system rather than a one-time release: integration into donor-advised fund conversations, and a real expansion of the advisor toolkit. The same architecture carries the next experience at a fraction of the original build cost.

  4. New revenue streams

    Advisor and firm licensing, cohort access, family experience subscriptions, the assessment and profile as a licensed instrument, and the formation product suite that follows. All of it sits on the licensing platform described in section twelve, which carries no development fee.

  5. Business owner transition and non-liquid giving

    Owners engage the exit conversation at year end, while almost everything that could be done still can be — including gifts of business equity, real estate and appreciated assets, which only work before a transaction closes. Most owners learn about that window after it has shut. This puts the question in front of them a year early, as formation rather than as a proposal.

The Editions

One experience. Every generation, and every room.

The forty days ask a household to have a conversation at the table — but as commissioned, only the adults will have read anything. A father finishes a book, then asks his fifteen-year-old a question the fifteen-year-old has no context for. That is the quiet flaw in almost every family discipleship product ever published, and the editions close it. Same forty days, same six stages, same week; a nine-year-old, a nineteen-year-old and a grandfather all arrive at Thursday's conversation having read something written for them.

The theology never changes between editions. What changes is the stakes and the instruments. A child surrenders a toy; a founder surrenders a company. It is the same stage.

The Toolbox

Most families will never do the same forty days. Almost none of them should.

A family in the year before a sale, a family that has just buried its patriarch, and a family whose children are eleven and fourteen have almost nothing in common except that all three would be handed the same book. That is the limitation of publishing, and why so much good material sits unopened on the shelves of the people who could most use it.

Over 100 customizable tools

Every instrument built across this work becomes a tool that stands on its own, brands to the firm or the family, and works in a single meeting without committing anyone to a forty-day anything. An advisor does not always need a season; sometimes he needs one page for one conversation on Thursday. Ten of more than a hundred:

  • Before the Letter of Intent — eight questions for an owner while every option is still open
  • How Much Is Enough — the conversation a couple has never quite finished
  • The Family Giving Statement — five decisions, one signed page
  • The Grant Table Agenda — ninety minutes, and the children decide one of them
  • Twenty Questions, One Evening — the whole family discovery in a single sitting
  • The Holiday Table Guide — for the years when everyone is home and nobody says anything
  • The Grandparent Interview — before the stories are gone
  • The Inheritance Conversation — at eighteen, at twenty-five, at thirty

Roughly a hundred more sit behind these, across discovery, family conversation, story capture, giving, business transition, next generation, children and youth, advisor practice and church. The library grows with every experience added to it.

Specimen · One Tool of More Than a Hundred
Tool 47 · Business and TransitionThe Signatry Toolbox

Before the Letter of Intent

Eight questions for a business owner while every option is still open. Roughly forty minutes. Nothing is signed and nothing is proposed.

  1. If this sold next year, what would you want true about your family the day after?
  2. What number is enough — and when did you last say it out loud to your spouse?
  3. Who besides you knows what you would want done if it happened without warning?
  4. What percentage of this company do you think of as yours?
  5. Which of your children could run it, and which of them wants to?
  6. What would you regret not giving while you could still see where it went?
  7. What would you want your grandchildren to know about how this was built?
  8. What decision have you been putting off because it is easier to decide later?

Facilitator note. Question four is the one that changes the conversation. Ask it, then say nothing for fifteen seconds.

In Closing

This is not a publishing project. It is a discipleship infrastructure project.

The Signatry means the one who signs. The vision is to write the last check to the last worker reaching the last unreached people group — and that check is only possible because, somewhere upstream, a family signed first and decided that what they held was not finally theirs.

A book will not change that arithmetic on its own. What it can do is put the decision in front of a family in the year they are actually making it — before the sale closes, before the documents are drafted, before the children have already decided what they think the money means.

The result is not readers. It is families who have made a decision together, advisors who have a reason to ask a better question, and a message that outlasts the room it is currently spoken in.
