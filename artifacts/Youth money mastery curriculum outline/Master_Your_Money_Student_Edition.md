# MASTER YOUR MONEY — STUDENT EDITION
### A 6-Part Curriculum & Devotional for Junior High–High School (Built for the Sophomore in the Middle)

---

## How This Version Is Different (and Why)

This is a faithful youth adaptation of the adult *Master Your Money* curriculum. The
theology is unchanged — **God owns it all, we're stewards in a growth process,
faithfulness matters more than the amount, and faith requires action.** What changes is
*who's in the room.*

A 15-year-old doesn't have a mortgage, a 401(k), or an estate to plan. But they do have a
phone, a part-time paycheck or an allowance, a group chat full of comparison, a first
debit card, and a brain that is actively deciding *who they are.* That last part is the key:
adolescence is the identity-forming season of life, so this version leads with **identity
before instructions** — "whose am I" before "what do I do." Every habit we want an adult
to have is cheaper, easier, and stickier to build at 16 than at 46. That's the whole pitch.

**The seven design moves that make this a best-practice youth curriculum:**

1. **Identity-first, not mechanics-first.** Teens are wired to ask "who am I?" We answer the
   ownership question *as an identity question*, then let money flow from it. Heart before behavior.
2. **One big idea per session.** Adolescent attention is real and finite. Each session lands a
   single sticky truth instead of ten sub-points, so students leave able to say it back.
3. **Present-tense, relatable scenarios.** No retirement or estate planning. We use the money life
   a sophomore actually lives — jobs, phones, comparison, first cards, buy-now-pay-later, gaming
   purchases, saving for a car.
4. **Timeless principle + timely example, kept separate.** Every session names the eternal truth
   *and* a disposable modern example, so leaders can swap the example in ten years without the
   curriculum aging out.
5. **Experiential, not lecture-only.** Each session ends with a *do-it* challenge scaled to a
   teenager's actual money — because teens learn habits by repetition, not information.
6. **Discussion- and peer-driven.** At this age, peers are the loudest voice. We use that on
   purpose: story-sharing, group challenges, and honest conversation instead of a talking head.
7. **Grace over guilt, and money-shame-aware.** We keep the adult curriculum's "invitation, not
   obligation" tone — and add an explicit rule for leaders: never assume a student's family wealth.
   Some kids have a lot, some have almost nothing, and money is a sensitive topic in a room of
   teenagers. We frame everything around *the heart*, which levels the room.

Each session below carries a **🎯 Best Practice in Action** callout so you can see the design
choice behind the design.

# SESSION 1 — The Ownership Question
### Whose Is It Anyway?

**🎯 Best Practice in Action — Identity before instructions.** We open the whole series on the
single question a teenager's brain is already obsessed with: *who am I, and whose am I?* Settle
ownership and every other session has a foundation. Skip it and we're just teaching budgeting to
people who don't care yet.

**Big Idea:** None of it is actually mine. I'm the manager, not the owner.

**Timeless principle:** God owns everything; I'm a steward. *(Psalm 24:1 — "The earth is the
Lord's, and everything in it.")*

**Why it hits at this age:** Sophomores are fighting to *own* their identity, their choices,
their stuff. This session flips the script in a way that's actually freeing: if you're the
owner, the pressure to control everything is on you. If you're the manager, the weight belongs
to Someone bigger. That's a relief, not a demotion.

**Hook (pick one):**
- "What's the most expensive thing you own — and could you actually afford to replace it right now?"
- Quick game: name three things in your pocket/bag. Who *actually* paid for them? Whose name is on the plan, the phone, the car you ride in?

**Teaching beats:**
- The phone in your hand, the bed you sleep in, the lungs you're breathing with — trace it back far enough and you didn't make any of it. You're borrowing all of it.
- That's not meant to make you feel small. It makes you feel **steady.** Owners have to guarantee the future and carry the weight alone. Managers just have to be faithful with what they've been handed.
- Once you see God as the owner, every money choice becomes a "what would a good manager do?" question — not a "what can I get away with?" question.
- This is where **contentment** comes from: not having everything, but trusting that the Owner has you covered.

**Talk about it:**
- Where do you feel the most pressure to *have* something? Where does that pressure come from?
- If you really believed nothing was ultimately yours, what's one thing you'd worry about less?
- What's the difference between "this is mine" and "this is mine to take care of"?

**Try it this week (the challenge):** Pick **one thing** — money, a possession, a worry about the
future — and say out loud, "God, this is yours. Show me how to manage it well." Then, before your
next purchase this week, pause once and ask: *am I acting like an owner or a manager right now?*

**Memory verse:** Psalm 24:1

**Leader note:** Some students in your room have very little; some have a lot. Keep every example
about *the heart*, never about the size of the pile. The ownership truth lands the same whether a
student has $7 or $7,000 — and that's the point.

# SESSION 2 — The Wisdom Question
### Does God Even Care About My $20?

**🎯 Best Practice in Action — Make the principle work at their scale.** A teenager assumes Bible
money advice is "adult stuff" for people with real money. This session proves the opposite:
faithfulness with $20 is the *same skill* as faithfulness with $20,000. We meet them at their
actual dollar amount instead of waiting for them to grow into the lesson.

**Big Idea:** God's money wisdom is practical, and it works even when you only have a little.

**Timeless principle:** The four truths under everything —
1. **God owns it all** *(so every money choice is a spiritual choice).*
2. **You're in a growth process** *(God uses money to grow you, not just fund you — Phil. 4:11–12).*
3. **The amount doesn't matter; faithfulness does** *(Luke 16:10 — "whoever is faithful with little…").*
4. **Faith requires action** *(believing it changes nothing until you actually do something).*

**Why it hits at this age:** Teens are allergic to vague. "Be wise with money" means nothing.
Four clear, repeatable truths give them something they can actually hold — and the "amount
doesn't matter" principle dismantles the #1 teen excuse: *"I'll start when I have real money."*

**Hook (pick one):**
- "Would you rather be handed $1,000 today, or $1 that doubles every day for a month?" (It doubles to over a billion — wisdom and time beat a lump sum. Save this image; it returns in Session 5.)
- "What's the dumbest thing you've ever spent money on? No judgment." (Set the honest, grace-filled tone early.)

**Teaching beats:**
- God's wisdom about money isn't a list of rules to feel guilty about. It's a gift — the same way a coach's advice is a gift, not a punishment.
- Run the four principles plainly. Land hard on #3: God is not waiting for you to be rich. He's watching whether you're faithful *now.* The habits you build at $20 are the exact habits you'll need at $20,000.
- #4 is the turn: this whole series is useless as information. It only works as *action.* Don't try to nail all four this week — pick **one.**

**Talk about it:**
- Which of the four principles is easiest for you to believe? Which is hardest to actually live?
- Be honest: have you ever told yourself "I'll be responsible with money later, when I have more"? What's the flaw in that?
- What would change if you treated a $20 decision as a spiritual decision?

**Try it this week (the challenge):** Pick the **one** principle you break the most and take one
small step. If it's spending — track every dollar for seven days. If it's giving — set a "give"
amount aside *first,* before you spend anything. If it's planning — pick one thing to start
saving for.

**Memory verse:** Philippians 4:11–12

**Leader note:** Resist the urge to cover all four principles deeply. Pick one to emphasize and
let students own it. Depth on one beats a tour of all four.

# SESSION 3 — The Freedom Question
### How Do I Not Get Trapped?

**🎯 Best Practice in Action — Name the actual trap before they fall in.** The adult version asks
about "financial independence." A sophomore is years from that — but they are *right now* one tap
away from their first comparison spiral, first credit card, first "buy now, pay later" checkout.
We translate "freedom" into the two traps that are live for them *today,* so the lesson is
preventative, not theoretical.

**Big Idea:** Stuff and debt both *promise* freedom and deliver the opposite.

**Timeless principle:** Debt takes your options; comparison takes your peace. Real freedom is
contentment, not accumulation. *(Proverbs 22:7 — "the borrower is slave to the lender";
1 Timothy 6:6 — "godliness with contentment is great gain.")*

**Why it hits at this age:** Two cages get built in the teen years. **The comparison cage** —
everyone online looks richer, and the feed is engineered to make you feel behind. **The debt
cage** — the first card, the financing offer, the "split it into 4 easy payments" button that
makes broke feel like rich. Most adults drowning in debt started the *habit* young. Catching it
at 16 is the whole game.

**Hook (pick one):**
- "Show of hands — ever bought something *only* because someone else had it? How'd you feel about it a month later?"
- Quick teardown: pull up how "buy now, pay later" actually works. It's debt with a friendly font.

**Teaching beats:**
- **The comparison trap.** Use the Contentment Spectrum: **Complacent** (give up, "I'll never have nice things") ↔ **Content** (grateful, honor God with what I have) ↔ **Discontent** (driven, jealous, never enough). The feed pushes you to the edges. Contentment is the only spot that's actually free.
- **The debt trap.** Debt isn't just "owing money." It's *future-you* working to pay for present-you's wants. Proverbs literally calls the borrower a *slave* to the lender — not because debt is a sin, but because it quietly steals your options.
- Freedom isn't having enough money to buy whatever you want. Freedom is wanting what you have enough that money stops bossing you around.

**Talk about it:**
- Where on the spectrum do you live most days — complacent, content, or discontent? What pushes you there?
- What's one thing social media has made you feel like you *need*? Do you actually?
- Why do you think "split it into 4 payments" feels so easy? What's it really doing?

**Try it this week (the challenge):** Two-parter. **(1) Comparison:** one day this week, every
time you feel the "I need that" pull, write down one thing you already have and are grateful for.
**(2) Debt:** make a personal rule *now* — "I don't buy what I can't pay for today" — and say it
to one friend so it's real.

**Memory verse:** 1 Timothy 6:6

**Leader note:** Don't shame anyone who's already in over their head (a financed phone, money owed
to a friend or parent). The tone is "here's the exit," never "you blew it." Freedom language only.

# SESSION 4 — The Planning Question
### Where Do I Even Start?

**🎯 Best Practice in Action — Replace dread words with a do-able tool.** Teens hear "budget" and
think "punishment" — same as adults, but worse. We swap it for a four-word framework they can
actually run on a $40 paycheck, and reframe planning as a *map,* not a cage. Concrete and
hands-on beats abstract and inspiring every time at this age.

**Big Idea:** A plan isn't a cage — it's a map. And starting this young is a superpower.

**Timeless principle:** Planning is clarity, not control. A spending plan just shows you what you
actually value. *(Proverbs 16:3 — "Commit to the Lord whatever you do, and your plans will
succeed"; Matthew 6:21 — "where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.")*

**Why it hits at this age:** Most teens have *never* made a plan for money — it just shows up and
disappears. The "where did my money go?" feeling is universal. Give them one simple, repeatable
tool and they get the rare experience of *clarity replacing chaos* — which, even at 16, drops the
low-grade anxiety more than they expect.

**Hook (pick one):**
- "Quick — how much money do you have right now, and where did your last $20 go?" (Most can't answer the second part. That's the point.)
- "If you got $500 tomorrow, what would happen to it? Be honest."

**Teaching beats:**
- A budget isn't God telling you what you *can't* do. It's *you* deciding what matters most *before* the money shows up. It's a values document — your spending always reveals what you actually care about (Matt. 6:21).
- The framework: **Live / Give / Owe / Grow.**
  - **Live** — your everyday stuff: food, fun, phone.
  - **Give** — generosity, set aside *first* (church, others in need).
  - **Owe** — anything you owe (money to a parent, a friend you borrowed from). Pay it. And aim to keep this box empty for life.
  - **Grow** — saving for something bigger: a car, college, a goal.
- Then the superpower: you get a head start most adults never had. Adults spend their whole lives climbing from **Struggling → Surviving → Stable → Secure → Surplus.** You get to start building stable habits before you even earn real money. That's an unfair advantage, and it's yours for free.

**Talk about it:**
- Which box would your money naturally over-fill if you didn't plan? Which would get ignored?
- Does the idea of a plan feel more like freedom or restriction to you? Why?
- What's one thing worth "growing" toward in the next year?

**Try it this week (the challenge):** Make a one-page **Live / Give / Owe / Grow** plan for next
month's money — whatever the source (job, allowance, gifts). Put a real number or percentage in
each box. *Give first.*

**Memory verse:** Proverbs 16:3

**Leader note:** Some students get $0 of regular money. Adapt: have them plan a *gift* of $100, or
their next paycheck, or birthday money. The skill is the goal, not the size of the plan.

# SESSION 5 — The Purpose Question
### What's the Money Actually For?

**🎯 Best Practice in Action — Future-cast to a milestone that's actually near.** The adult version
zooms out to retirement and legacy. For a sophomore that's invisible. We aim the "long game" at
milestones a teen can *feel* coming — a car, college, the first apartment, a dream — and show how
compounding rewards them for caring early. Near-future vision motivates; far-future doesn't.

**Big Idea:** Money is a tool, not a trophy. Aim it at something that lasts.

**Timeless principle:** Goals give your money direction; without a target you'll spend on whatever's
loudest. Discipline now produces freedom later. *(Proverbs 21:5 — "the plans of the diligent lead
to profit"; Hebrews 12:11 — discipline "produces a harvest of righteousness and peace.")*

**Why it hits at this age:** Teens are dreamers — that's a strength to harness, not lecture out of
them. This session lets them dream *with God* and gives the dream a number. Plus, the math of
**compounding** is genuinely thrilling to a 16-year-old: starting at 16 instead of 26 isn't a
little better, it's *enormously* better, and they have the one ingredient adults are desperate
for — time.

**Hook (pick one):**
- Bring back the doubling penny from Session 2. Then: "What if you started saving at 16 vs. 26? The 16-year-old wins by a fortune, often without ever saving more — just earlier. Time is the cheat code, and you're the only one in your family who still has all of it."
- "What's a dream that costs money? Now — what's step one toward it?"

**Teaching beats:**
- A trophy you set on a shelf to look at. A tool you pick up to *build* something. Money is the second one. The question isn't "how much can I get?" — it's "what am I building?"
- **Compounding / margin:** small amounts, saved consistently and given time, grow into something that doesn't feel possible at the start. "Margin" just means a little breathing room between what you have and what you spend — and breathing room is where freedom and generosity live.
- **Faith goals:** the best goals aren't just "what do I want?" but "God, what would you have me prepare for?" Goals turn faith into a direction. A goal you *write down* is a goal you actually move toward — "if you aim at nothing, you'll hit it every time."

**Talk about it:**
- What's something worth saving for that future-you would thank you for?
- What's the difference between using money as a tool and chasing it as a trophy? Where do you lean?
- If you asked God what to prepare for, what do you think He'd point you toward?

**Try it this week (the challenge):** Write down **one God-sized goal** — something a year or more
out — and the first small step toward it. Pray over it. Tell one person so it's real.

**Memory verse:** Proverbs 21:5

**Leader note:** Celebrate the dreaming. The instinct is to make teens "realistic," but this
session works *because* it gives big dreams a faithful, doable first step instead of shutting them down.

# SESSION 6 — The Generosity Question
### Why Does Giving Change Everything?

**🎯 Best Practice in Action — End on the heart, and prove it's possible *now.*** Generosity is the
emotional peak of the series, and the easiest place to accidentally guilt teenagers (who mostly
feel broke). We do the opposite: no pressure, no quota — and we prove a 16-year-old with $12 can
be radically generous *today.* Ending on action they can actually take this week is what makes
the whole series stick.

**Big Idea:** Giving breaks money's grip on my heart — and I can do it even when I'm broke.

**Timeless principle:** Generosity is a response, not a requirement. We give because *we* need the
freedom, not because God needs the cash. *(2 Corinthians 9:7 — "God loves a cheerful giver.")*

**Why it hits at this age:** Money competes with God for the controls of a heart that's still being
built. Giving is how you keep it from winning — and teens are *uniquely* generous when it's framed
as freedom and impact rather than obligation. The lie they need broken is "I'll give when I have
more." The truth: if you can't give $5 now, more money won't make you generous later — it'll just
make you better at keeping it.

**Hook (pick one):**
- "Tell us about a time someone was generous to *you*. How'd it feel?"
- "Be honest — does giving feel like losing something or gaining something? Why?"

**Teaching beats:**
- Generosity isn't God needing your money — He owns it all already (back to Session 1). It's you proving to yourself that money doesn't own *you.* Giving is the lever that loosens its grip on your heart.
- It's a *response,* never pressure or a quota (2 Cor. 9:7). The amount isn't the point — the freedom is. A genuinely given $5 does more in your heart than a guilted $500.
- You don't have to wait to be rich to be generous. Generosity is a muscle. Build it small now and it'll be strong when the dollars are big later. **Give time, talent, or treasure** — generosity isn't only money.

**Talk about it:**
- What makes giving feel hard? Be specific.
- What's something *besides money* you could be generous with right now — time, skill, attention?
- If money has any grip on your heart, where do you feel it most?

**Try it this week (the challenge):** Give something away **on purpose** this week — money, time,
or skill — to a person or cause, with no expectation back. Then notice how it changed *you.*

**Memory verse:** 2 Corinthians 9:7

**Closing for the whole series:** This isn't the finish line — it's the start of a new posture.
When money is handled with **ownership, wisdom, freedom, planning, purpose, and generosity,**
contentment grows. You're not waiting until you're an adult to master your money. You're starting
now, with a head start most people never get.

## The 6-Day Take-Home Devotional Rhythm (Per Session)

Built short on purpose — a teen will do 5 honest minutes a day and skip a 30-minute one.

- **Day 1 — Read it.** The memory verse + one short paragraph restating the Big Idea.
- **Day 2 — Spot it.** Find one place in your real life today where this showed up.
- **Day 3 — Pray it.** A one-line prayer template tied to the session.
- **Day 4 — Do it.** The session challenge, with a checkbox.
- **Day 5 — Share it.** Text one friend or family member one thing you're learning.
- **Day 6 — Catch up / reflect.** Miss a day? No guilt — just pick it back up. *(We're not chasing perfection.)*

## Design Notes — Why Each Choice Is a Best Practice

| Adult version | Student version | The youth best practice behind the change |
|---|---|---|
| Opens on stewardship as a money concept | Opens on ownership as an **identity** question | Adolescence is the identity-formation stage — lead with "whose am I" |
| Retirement, estate, net-worth statements | Cars, college, first apartment, dreams | Future-casting only motivates teens when the milestone feels *near* |
| "Financial independence" | "How do I not get trapped?" (comparison + debt) | Preventative — names the traps that are live for a 16-year-old *today* |
| Dense multi-point teaching | **One big idea per session** | Honors real adolescent attention spans; students can repeat it back |
| Read-and-discuss | **Do-it challenge scaled to teen money** | Teens build habits by repetition, not information |
| Lecture-forward | Discussion- and peer-forward | At this age peers are the loudest influence — use it on purpose |
| Grace-over-guilt tone | Same tone **+ explicit money-shame guardrails** | A room of teens spans wide wealth gaps; keep it about the heart |
| 40-day devotional | **6-day, 5-minute-a-day rhythm** | Short and forgiving = actually completed |
| Timeless truths | Timeless truth **labeled separately** from timely example | Lets leaders swap examples in 10 years without the curriculum aging out |
