Summary

The short version

Three things are true, and the third is the one most likely to be argued with.

The bottleneck

It is not the app. It is a retrievable library and a captured version of Brett's assembly method. Fix those first. Ship a searchable internal assistant and a GitHub migration inside the first month to earn latitude, then treat the questionnaire-to-content product as a phased build on Supabase and pgvector that starts life as a Claude-grounded prototype.

The sequencing

Brett's 90-day plan gets the content right and the order wrong. It is too linear. Inventory, taxonomy, and capturing Brett's method have to run in parallel from week one, because both the app and the campaign prototypes depend on artifacts that only exist if month one delivers them.

The correction

Your four points are largely right. The one to challenge is the absolutism in point one. You do not need the whole terabyte organized before anything works. You need a few hundred assets tagged and the method written down. Organization is the critical path for the product, not for your first visible wins.

Section 1

Key findings

Five facts that shape the build. Each one has a design consequence, not just an interesting number.

Airtable is right, with an ownership asterisk

It is still the fastest way for a non-engineer to build a real relational catalog with intake forms. But Bending Spoons announced a definitive agreement on August 4, 2026 to acquire Airtable in an all-cash deal at roughly $1.285 billion enterprise value, expected to close before year-end pending regulatory approval. The acquirer's history with Evernote and WeTransfer points to price discipline before product investment.

Design consequence: keep the schema exportable to CSV and Postgres. No deep lock-in, no Airtable-only logic that would be expensive to rebuild.

Supabase plus pgvector is the retrieval foundation

pgvector ships free on all plans, and Pro at $25 per month per organization gives an 8 GB database and removes the inactivity pause. Vectors, application data, and auth all live in one Postgres database, which is exactly the shape an AI-assisted developer can build against without stitching four services together.

Design consequence: one database. Josh can point Claude Code at a single schema rather than orchestrating a stack.

Assembly is cheap enough to prototype freely

Sonnet 5 runs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output. Prompt caching drops cached input to roughly a tenth of base, and the Batch API adds a flat 50 percent on both directions; the two discounts stack. Embeddings are close to free — voyage-4-lite at $0.02 per million tokens with a large free allowance.

Design consequence: nobody gets to justify architectural complexity on token cost. The expense here is human curation and approval time.

The Netlify-to-GitHub move is low risk

Netlify's GitHub App gives continuous deployment, deploy previews, and branch deploys. A repo can be connected without touching the existing live site, because the old deploy stays exactly as it is until the domain is cut over.

Design consequence: migrate per site, in parallel, and cut over only after a preview URL renders identically. There is always a rollback.

Claude's Drive connector cannot reorganize Drive

It reads, summarizes, cross-references, and can save new files. It cannot move, rename, trash, or update metadata on files that already exist — Anthropic's own issue tracker describes the connector as effectively read-only for modifications.

Design consequence: there is no one-click Drive cleanup. Month one includes real human hours. Do not let anyone promise otherwise.

The finding nobody put on the list: rights

The archive carries mixed contributors, vintages, and publishability. The original Doing Life Together series with Zondervan in 2002 was credited to Brett Eastman, Dee Eastman, Todd Wendorff and Denise Wendorff, with Doug Fields on the student editions, and much of twenty years of material will carry similar shared authorship and publisher rights.

Design consequence: rights and provenance are a required schema field and a hard retrieval filter, captured during inventory rather than retrofitted after an app has already assembled a packet for a client.

Section 2

Diagnosis

The critical path runs through three artifacts, in this dependency order. Everything else — the app, the campaigns, most of the automation lab — sits downstream of them.

Artifact A

A controlled vocabulary and metadata schema

Locked lists, not free text. Free-text tags rot within weeks and take retrieval quality down with them.

Artifact B

A few hundred priority assets actually tagged

Tagged against that schema, with rights status populated from the first row forward.

Artifact C

A written, testable spec of Brett's method

How he assembles a recommendation by hand today. Whatever he does manually is the specification.

Where the current 90-day plan breaks

It reads as three sequential months: organize, then build the engine, then build campaign prototypes. That back-loads the highest-risk item. The campaign prototypes in month three and the flagship project in month two both require the taxonomy and the documented method, which are month-one deliverables. If month one slips — and inventory projects always slip — months two and three collapse with it.

Run inventory, taxonomy, and method capture concurrently from week one, and pull one flagship campaign prototype forward so there is something to show at the end of month one rather than the end of month three. The plan is right about the governance decisions: one ranked queue, three active projects, Brett feeds the queue rather than the people. Lock those before touching a single tool.

Your four points, judged plainly

Right in direction, wrong in absolutism

1. Organization is the bottleneck

The app does depend on the data layer. But saying that until it exists every AI feature is just Brett's memory with extra steps overstates it. A genuinely useful grounded internal assistant can ship over a few hundred well-tagged assets while the long tail stays messy. Do not hold visible wins hostage to a complete inventory you will not finish for months. Organize the priority set, ship against it, expand.

Correct and unambiguous

2. Move off zip-file Netlify onto GitHub

Table stakes. Teachable in days, de-risks everything the two younger staff touch, costs almost nothing, and is clean and visible. Do it early.

Correct, and the most important thing you understand

3. The app is retrieval and assembly, not a novel AI problem

Section 3

Versioning

Semantic-lite: vMAJOR.MINOR. Major increments on a substantive content change Brett approves; minor on edits and formatting. Store the version in the metadata row and in the filename. Drive's invisible version history is not a source of truth — it does not survive downloads, copies, or moves.

File naming

Apply going forward; backfill only the priority set.

[Series]_[Audience]_[Type]_[Session##]_[Title-kebab]_v[MAJOR.MINOR]

Generosity_Family_SessionGuide_03_teaching-your-kids-to-give_v1.2

The hard truth about Drive automation

The realistic path is: Claude, or Claude Code with Drive synced locally, reads files and proposes metadata rows and new names; a human approves; then Claude writes renamed copies into the new structure or a person does the moves. Budget human time for it. This is the unglamorous core of month one and there is no way around it.

Section 4

The app, in three phases

Each phase ships and each one unlocks the next. Phase gates are enforced: no Supabase build until the priority assets are tagged, no Phase 2 assessment work until Phase 1 ships.

Weeks, not months

Formalize the manual workflow before building anything custom

A single Claude Project loaded with the approved priority assets plus a written assembly procedure — his intake questions, his selection logic, his output format. This is not the product. It is the specification and the first demo, and it proves the assembly logic works. Claude Projects hold considerably more than the raw context window suggests, because paid plans shift into a retrieval mode that expands effective capacity substantially as project knowledge grows, so a few hundred trimmed assets is workable.

Includes
  • A fixed intake questionnaire run inside the project
  • Retrieval over the loaded assets
  • A curated output packet
Excludes
  • Any real user login
  • Any assessment scoring
  • Anything customer-facing

Data required: the priority assets, tagged, plus the written procedure.

The real MVP

Intake in, draft curated packet out, with a human gate

  • Data layer: Supabase Postgres holding the asset metadata mirror plus a pgvector column for embeddings.
  • Ingestion: a Claude Code script that pulls approved assets, chunks them, embeds each chunk with Voyage, and stores vectors plus metadata. Only Approved or Published, rights-clear assets get embedded.
  • Retrieval: hybrid search — vector similarity plus keyword — filtered by structured metadata for audience, topic, and rights.
  • Assembly: a Claude API call taking intake answers plus retrieved chunks, producing a curated recommendation with citations back to source asset IDs.
  • Intake: a simple web form built with Lovable or v0, deployed on Netlify, writing answers to Supabase.
  • Human gate: nothing reaches a client without Brett's approval. The app produces a draft packet, never a finished deliverable.
Excludes
  • The personality and values assessment
  • Self-serve customer accounts
  • Payment
  • One-click polished output
Data required first
  • Priority assets embedded
  • Rights status populated, so the app can never surface "Do not use" material
  • Audience tags reliable
Only after Phase 1 is shipping

The questionnaire-to-campaign product Brett is describing

  • A personality or values assessment layer, scored and mapped to content attributes
  • User accounts by client type — pastor, family, advisor, individual
  • Saved and shareable campaign outputs
  • Attractive previews built for sales conversations
  • Packaged multi-week campaigns as first-class objects

This is where a front-end tool like Softr, sitting over the Airtable and Supabase data, earns its place for client-facing portals. Airtable's own Interfaces are weak for external users, while Softr provides logins, permissions, and white-label apps without per-seat costs.

Data required first: a validated assessment instrument — a content and possibly licensing decision, not an engineering one — plus a critical mass of the library embedded and campaign templates defined by Brett.

The build-vs-buy fork

If the assessment requires a validated, licensed third-party instrument, that becomes a procurement and legal decision that could gate Phase 2 for months. If Brett is comfortable with a proprietary, LifeTogether-authored assessment, the timeline stays under your control. Push hard for the proprietary route unless a client contractually requires a named instrument.

On cost

A curated packet that reads tens of thousands of tokens of retrieved context and writes a few thousand tokens of output costs cents at Sonnet 5 pricing, and caching plus batch discounts cut that further. One wrinkle worth watching: the $2 and $10 rate launched as introductory pricing, and current Anthropic documentation indicates the previously scheduled September 1 increase to $3 and $15 was cancelled, though some third-party trackers still cite the old expiry. Verify before building a budget on it. Either way, the expense in this project is human curation and approval time.

Section 5

Website and dev workflow migration

Move the two staff from zip-upload deploys to GitHub-backed continuous deployment without breaking a single live site. Per site, in parallel to the existing deploy, cut over only once verified. Time budget: a week of part-time work.

  1. Inventory the live sites. For each, capture the custom domain, DNS, and build settings. Many are likely plain static sites with no build step, which makes this easier.
  2. Create a GitHub organization. Add the two staff and Josh. Free tier; private repos are free.
  3. Start with one low-stakes site. Create a repo and commit the current files. Teach this through GitHub Desktop, not the command line — that alone removes the biggest source of fear.
  4. Connect the repo to Netlify using the GitHub App, which handles continuous deployment via OAuth. For static sites the publish directory is often the root or a build folder.
  5. Verify on a preview URL that the GitHub-driven deploy is identical. Only then point the custom domain at it. The old zip project stays untouched, so there is always a rollback.
  6. Turn on deploy previews. This is the single feature that most changes how they work — they can show Brett a preview link instead of gambling on a live deploy.
  7. Repeat for each remaining site, most stakeful last.
Training the two staff

They use ChatGPT Pro and Claude Max daily and are starting Claude Code, so they are not beginners. Teach the mental model, not the commands: a repo is the single source of truth, a commit is a labeled save point, main is what is live, a branch is a safe place to try things, a pull request is review before it goes live. Skip Git internals entirely.

Tools and guardrails
  • GitHub Desktop for commit, push, branch, and pull request
  • VS Code or their editor for edits; Claude Code for the building
  • No terminal Git required anywhere in this workflow
  • Protect the main branch so nothing deploys without a pull request
  • Connect Claude Code to GitHub via web setup so they can drive repo changes from the tool they already use
Done looks like: every live site deploys from a GitHub repo on push, main is protected, deploy previews are on, and both staff can independently make a change on a branch, open a pull request, preview it, and merge it.
Section 6

Automations, ranked by value over effort

Three to build, in this order. Everything else waits, and the reasons matter as much as the ranking.

Build first

1. A searchable internal assistant grounded in approved material

Highest value, and the effort is mostly inventory work already underway. This is the feature that directly attacks "nobody can answer what we have on X," it makes Brett's memory scalable, and it is the natural first slice of the app itself.

Build second

2. A weekly report of what Brett created this week

Very low effort, high visibility, and it serves the queue-and-approval model directly by making Brett's output legible without him doing anything. A meeting-notes tool plus a scheduled Claude summary over new Drive files gets most of the way there. This is the cheapest trust-builder available.

Build third

3. Meeting transcripts into projects and next actions

Transcript in, structured candidate tasks out, Hana triages. This is the highest-leverage way to stop the idea firehose from overwhelming her, and it feeds the ranked queue rather than competing with it.

  • Deduplicating new content against the library — valuable, but it depends on embeddings existing. A Phase 1 byproduct, not a standalone early build.
  • Routing approved content to the right folder and database — fragile until there is a defined intake path, because the Drive connector cannot move existing files. Build it for new content only, later.
  • Personalized campaign recommendations from intake — this is the app, not a side automation.
  • File naming and metadata automation — partially a byproduct of the inventory workflow. Full automation is low return until volume justifies it.
  • Intake forms for pastors, families, and advisors — these are the front door to Phase 1, not standalone automations.
  • Approval flagging and product briefs from recordings — useful second wave, after the top three prove the pattern.
Connective automation

Make over Zapier. Credit-based pricing from around $9 per month for 10,000 credits on annual billing is materially cheaper than Zapier's $19.99 for 750 tasks on the multi-step, AI-heavy workflows this project will generate. The caveat is that polling-heavy scenarios burn credits.

Meeting capture

A bot-free option such as Granola, from around $18 per month, avoids a recorder announcing itself in client and family conversations — which matters a great deal given who these conversations are with. Fireflies is the stronger choice only if CRM sync becomes a requirement later.

Section 8

What is most likely to kill this

In order. The first one is not a tooling problem, which is exactly why it is first.

Risk 1 · Highest

Brett bypasses the queue and hands ideas directly to people

This destroys the operating model that makes everything else survivable, and it is the failure mode with the strongest gravitational pull.

Mitigation, first two weeks: get the agreement in writing, and — the part that actually determines the outcome — make the queue lower friction for Brett than grabbing someone in a hallway. If dumping an idea into the queue is more work than an in-person ask, he will not use it, and no amount of agreement will change that.
Risk 2

The inventory stalls and month one slips

Inventory is boring and open-ended, and it is where this kind of project usually dies.

Mitigation: cap the first pass at exactly 100 assets, timebox it, and explicitly permit the long tail to stay messy. Ship the assistant against the 100 so there is a visible payoff pulling the work forward.
Risk 3

Rights and provenance get skipped in the rush to tag

Given shared authorship and publisher relationships across the archive, an assembled packet containing non-publishable material is a genuine liability — more so with wealthy family and advisor clients.

Mitigation: rights status is a required field from the very first asset, and "Do not use" and "unclear" are hard retrieval filters from day one, not a later cleanup.
Risk 4

Josh gets pulled into errands and the role collapses into assistant work

Exactly what Brett said he did not want, and it happens by drift rather than decision.

Mitigation: the operating agreements below, secured in the first two weeks before momentum sets the pattern.
Risk 5

Over-engineering the app before the data exists

Mitigation: enforce the phase gates. No Supabase build until the priority assets are tagged; no Phase 2 assessment work until Phase 1 ships.
Secondary but real

Vendor and key-person exposure

Airtable price increases driven by the Bending Spoons acquisition and its history — keep the schema exportable. And key-person dependency on Josh himself, which the plan already answers by documenting the repeatable method as the work happens rather than afterward.

Section 9

Role and boundaries

The role owns systems and repeatable methods, not tasks. The title should say so — Innovation Lab Director rather than Coordinator, because "coordinator" invites people to route coordination, which is to say errands, to you. Take the Director framing and live up to it by producing systems rather than by being available.

Six agreements to secure in writing in week one

0 of 6 secured

How to frame the role to each person

"I turn your ideas and archives into systems and finished products. The fastest way to lose that is for me to become the person you hand individual tasks to. Feed the queue, and I will give you completed prototypes and a library you can actually search. Every customer-facing thing still comes to you for content and theological approval. I am not deciding content — I am building the machine that assembles and ships it."

"You own priorities; I own building. You approve workflows before I roll them out. We keep one ranked queue and no more than three active projects. I will not start a fourth thing without something coming off the list, and I will not accept work that skips the queue, including from Brett."

"You own business model and pricing. I will build the campaign-production method and the app so that whatever you decide to sell, we can produce and preview it repeatably. Tell me the packaging you want to test and I will make it demoable."

The through-line

Your leverage comes from building things that keep working when Brett changes direction. Every agreement above exists to protect that. The moment the work collapses into ad-hoc requests, the value Brett is actually hiring you for disappears.
