## A WELCOME FROM TOM AND BRETT
### From Tom
When I started out in financial services, I thought I understood my job. Help clients get clear on what they wanted to do with what they had been entrusted with. Make sure the documents matched those intentions. Communicate the plan to the people who would inherit it. If the strategy was sound and the paperwork was tight, I believed I had done good work.

Over the years I started noticing cracks in that picture.

Families with what looked like airtight plans were coming apart anyway. I watched siblings stop speaking to each other. I watched a family business sold within months of a transition. I watched heirs receive more than they had any framework for holding, and instead of freedom they found conflict, or isolation, or a kind of low-grade resentment that never quite surfaced but never went away either.

For a while I assumed the problem was with the families.

Eventually I had to admit it was also with us. Our profession trains us almost entirely on the what of money. Investments, structures, tax strategy, documents. We get very good at the portfolio and we leave the people mostly alone. We manage numbers carefully and prepare the next generation hardly at all.

That realization set me on a different path, and what came out of it is what we now call Legacy by Design. It brings technical work and relational work into the same room instead of treating them as separate disciplines. It rests on three words that will come up again and again in this series. Clarity about what a family actually values. Alignment between their plans and those values. And Communication that keeps trust intact across generations.

It also widens the definition of legacy past money into five areas: Personal, Family, Financial, Business, and Charitable. When those five are working together, families do not simply hold onto wealth. They do something with it.

### From Brett
I came to this work from a different door.

For more than twenty-five years I have built resources that help people go through something together rather than alone. Small groups in churches, campaigns that a whole congregation walks through in the same six weeks, curriculum designed so that an ordinary person can lead it in a living room. Somewhere in the first decade of that I stopped believing that information changes people. What changes people is being in a room where somebody asks a real question and then waits for the answer.

When I met Tom, I recognized what he had built immediately, because it is the same thing in a different setting.

He had spent thirty years discovering that the families who make it are not the ones with better documents. They are the ones who learned how to talk to each other, usually because somebody sat with them and asked the questions nobody else was asking. That is a small group. It happens to be a family, and it happens to involve a great deal of money, and the mechanism underneath it is exactly the one I had been building for two decades.

I will say the pastoral thing plainly, because it is why I am here. I have watched families come apart with a great deal of money in the room, and I have watched families hold together with very little, and the difference was almost never financial. It was whether the generation that built something ever told the generation receiving it what any of it was for.

There is no document that carries that. A person has to say it out loud to another person, and somebody usually has to help them start.

### From both of us
This is not a book about being better at your job, though we think it will make you better at it.

It is about a specific and unusual position you occupy. You are frequently the only person in a family's life with the standing to ask about what matters most and the continuity to still be there in five years when the first conversation finally pays off. Very few people get that. Almost nobody uses it.

We wrote this to help you use it.

We are glad you are here.

**Tom Conway**
**Brett Eastman**

## THE SERIES, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
For the copyright page, the back jacket, the website, and anywhere the series needs to be described in a single block.

**Family Legacy Coaching by Design Series**

The Family Legacy Coaching by Design Series equips advisors to do the work almost nobody around a wealthy family is assigned to do. Not the transfer, which is handled well by people who are already there, but the preparation of the family who will receive it. Across six six-session modules, Tom Conway and Brett Eastman take the process Tom built over three decades of sitting with families and put it in a form a practicing advisor can use on Monday. It rests on three words. Clarity about what a family actually values, Alignment between their plans and those values, and Communication that keeps trust intact across generations. It widens the definition of legacy past money into five areas: Personal, Family, Financial, Business, and Charitable. And it is built for one advisor working alone as readily as for a team, because the shift it asks for is not a reorganization. It is a change in what you ask, and when.

**Short version, roughly fifty words, for spine copy and listings:**

Six modules that equip advisors to guide families through the transfer of wisdom as carefully as the transfer of wealth. Built on Tom Conway's thirty years of practice, organized around clarity, alignment, and communication, and designed to work for one advisor alone or an entire team.

## DESIGN NOTES
For Hannah and Joel.

**Signatures.** Both names set as cursive signatures at the close of the welcome, stacked rather than side by side, Tom above Brett. Recommend scanned actual signatures rather than a script typeface. If a typeface is used, one weight only and no flourish, because two ornate signatures in sequence reads as a certificate rather than a letter.

**Author photo, front.** One photo of Tom and Brett together, taken at the shoot. Wrapping it across the spine onto the back is a stronger treatment than two separate headshots, and it does the positioning work the welcome is now doing in copy. Both authors, one image, no hierarchy.

**Author photo, back.** If the front image wraps, the back does not need a second photo of the two of them. What the back needs is either the five module thumbnails or the photo, not both competing. Recommend thumbnails on the back and the wrap image on the front and spine.

**If a second image is wanted on the back**, use a small paired headshot strip beneath the author line rather than a second full image, and keep the five thumbnails as the dominant element.

**Source for the images.** Joel's shoot. A frame grab will hold at the sizes here provided it is from the highest resolution capture, and the wrap will need enough headroom on the left of frame to survive the spine.

**Five thumbnails.** Module covers at consistent size, evenly spaced, captions in two lines. This is the Lifetogether campaign back treatment and advisors will recognize the convention. Do not add a sixth for the foundation title itself, since the reader is holding it.

**Back jacket hierarchy.** Headline, sub-headline, body, thumbnails, author line, endorsement, imprint block. Body copy stays under one hundred and twenty words or the thumbnails lose the bottom third of the panel.

**Open item.** The series is described here as the **Family Legacy Coaching by Design Series**, which is also the title of the Coaching Book. That is a third instance of the naming collision already flagged on the guide and on the family foundation title. Recommend the series line reads **Family Legacy Coaching Series** and the Coaching Book keeps *Family Legacy Coaching by Design*, or the book retitles. Needs one decision that resolves all three.
