# DAY 21
# Because They're Personal
## You're Talking About Identity, Perspective, and Sometimes Emotion
**"The tongue has the power of life and death."**
PROVERBS 18:21

Why are these conversations so difficult? Because they're personal.

When you're talking about values, expectations, roles, or the future, you're not just talking about ideas. You're talking about identity, perspective, and sometimes emotion.

I want to be exact about that word personal, because advisors use it loosely and the looseness costs them.

A conversation is not personal because it concerns a family rather than a portfolio. It is personal when the subject cannot be discussed without a person being implicated in it. That is a much narrower category and it explains why some family conversations proceed easily and others do not.

Take a decision about which child will run the business. On the surface that is an operational question with a defensible answer. Underneath it, one person is being told something about his capability, another is being told something about her father's regard, and a third is discovering where he ranks. Nobody in the room says any of that. Everyone in the room knows it.

Or take a conversation about how much is enough. Ask a man what he needs for the rest of his life and you have asked him to state, in front of his wife, what he is afraid of. He will answer with a number and the number will be about the fear.

That is what identity means here. It is not that the topic is sensitive. It is that a person's standing in the family is being adjudicated by a conversation that appears to be about something else entirely.

And when that happens, people can become cautious. Or defensive. Or they may avoid the conversation altogether.

Those three are not personality types and I would encourage you to stop reading them that way. They are three responses to the same perceived risk, chosen mostly by temperament, and any one person will use all three depending on the subject. The quiet daughter is not a quiet person. She is quiet about this.

There is a principle in Proverbs 18:21. The tongue has the power of life and death.

Words matter. And people sense that, even if they can't always articulate it.

That last part is the practical piece. Nobody in the room is thinking, my identity is at stake in this conversation about the buy-sell. What they experience is a vague reluctance, a feeling that this is not a good time, an impulse to change the subject that arrives before any reasoning does. The sensing runs ahead of the articulating, which is why asking someone why they are hesitant almost never produces a useful answer.

So do not ask why. Assume the reason and design for it.

What I mean practically is this. Go into the conversation having already worked out what each person has at stake beyond the stated subject. Not to raise it, and certainly not to name it for them. To know it, so that you can recognize what you are seeing when someone goes quiet, and so you are not surprised into filling the silence with reassurance.

I will add one thing that changed how I work. The person with the most at stake in a family conversation is frequently not the person with the most money at stake. It is often the one whose place in the family is least settled, and that has almost no correlation with the balance sheet. If you are watching the wrong person, you will misread the whole room.

The stakes feel high because they are. When words carry that much, communication either becomes very careful, or it breaks down.

# DAY 22
# Not Because People Don't Care
## But Often Because It Matters So Much
**"Do not let any unwholesome talk come out of your mouths, but only what is helpful for building others up according to their needs."**
EPHESIANS 4:29

Communication breaks down not because people don't care, but often because it matters so much.

I have said that sentence in a great many rooms and it lands harder than anything else I say about families, because it contradicts what everyone present has privately concluded.

The family's own theory is usually that somebody does not care. The father thinks the children are not interested. The children think the father does not want them involved. Each of them has evidence, which is the other's silence, and silence supports any interpretation you bring to it.

The truth is generally the opposite. The conversation has not happened because everyone involved cares a great deal, and caring a great deal is what makes a subject too heavy to pick up casually.

Consider how this works in ordinary life. You can raise a difficult subject with a colleague over coffee without much preparation, because if it goes badly you have lost a conversation. You cannot raise the same subject with your brother the same way, because if it goes badly you have lost something you cannot replace. The higher the value of the relationship, the higher the cost of a bad attempt, and therefore the greater the caution.

That means avoidance in a family is frequently a signal of importance rather than indifference. I have learned to read a long-avoided subject as a marker of where the family's real concern is, not where its apathy is.

This changes what you say to a client, and I would encourage you to say it directly. When a man tells you his children have never raised the subject of the business with him, the reflexive read is that they do not care about it. The better hypothesis is that they care so much they cannot find a low-stakes way in. Offer him that hypothesis. Most fathers have never considered it and it reorganizes the whole picture for them.

There is a second consequence and it is about you.

If the difficulty is proportional to the stakes, then the conversations that are hardest to start are the ones with the most value in them. That is an uncomfortable rule because it means your instinct to route around the hard subject is routing around exactly the thing you were hired to reach. The comfortable conversations are comfortable because less is riding on them.

There is a principle in Ephesians 4:29. Do not let any unwholesome talk come out of your mouths, but only what is helpful for building others up according to their needs.

I want the last clause, because it is the one that gets dropped. According to their needs.

That is not a general instruction to be encouraging. It is a requirement to know something specific about the person in front of you before you decide what is helpful to say, which means the standard for good speech in that verse includes an obligation to have paid attention first.

Applied to a family conversation, it rules out two things at once. It rules out the blunt version, where somebody says the true thing without regard for what it will do. And it rules out the evasive version, where somebody says the pleasant thing because it is easier, which is also not according to anyone's needs.

What remains is the harder middle, which is saying the true thing in the form this particular person can receive. That takes knowing them.

Nobody in these families is indifferent. They are careful, and the care is the reason for the silence.

**3.** If difficulty is proportional to stakes, which conversation have you routed around because it was hard, and what was in it?

**4.** According to their needs requires knowing them first. Whose needs do you not actually know well enough to speak into?

# DAY 23
# Everyone Knew It, But No One Was Initiating It
## It Wasn't Guided Well
**"Therefore each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to your neighbor, for we are all members of one body."**
EPHESIANS 4:25

I worked with a family where this was very clear. They needed to have a conversation about expectations for the future. Everyone knew it, but no one was initiating it.

Eventually, the conversation happened. But it wasn't guided well.

People reacted quickly. Assumptions came out. And the conversation became tense.

Afterward, everyone felt like it hadn't gone well, and they were less willing to have another conversation.

There are two failures in that account and they are usually treated as one. Separating them is the whole value of the story.

The first failure is that nobody initiated. That one is widely understood and families are used to being gently criticized for it. What is less understood is why nobody did, and it is not cowardice. In most families there is no obvious person whose role it is to start. The father is a participant with an interest. The children are participants with interests. Anyone who initiates is understood to have an agenda, because in a family, raising a subject is itself a move.

That is a structural absence rather than a character failure, and it explains something advisors miss. These conversations do not fail to start because nobody is brave enough. They fail to start because there is no neutral party in the room, and every available initiator has a stake.

You are the neutral party. That is a substantial part of what you are actually for, and most advisors have never thought of it as part of the job description.

The second failure is that when it finally happened, it wasn't guided. Nobody was holding the room. There was no order of speaking, no rule about responding, no stated purpose, and no one whose job was the process rather than the outcome.

I want to be careful here, because the two failures compound in a specific way and the compounding is the real damage.

An unguided conversation on a subject that has been avoided for years will go badly, almost without exception. Years of unspoken material arrives at once, without sequencing, into a room that is already braced. That is not a family with a communication problem. That is a predictable outcome of the conditions.

And what the family learns from it is the wrong lesson. They conclude that this subject cannot be discussed, or that this family cannot discuss things, when what actually happened is that a difficult conversation was attempted without any structure. The conclusion is false and it is now operating.

That is the compounding. One bad attempt does not just fail. It closes the door on the next attempt, and the next one would have been the one that worked.

There is a principle in Ephesians 4:25. Each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to your neighbor, for we are all members of one body.

The reason given at the end is what makes it usable. Not because truthfulness is a virtue in the abstract. Because we are members of one body, which is to say the parts are connected and what one withholds from another is withheld from the whole thing.

A family is that kind of arrangement whether or not anyone has said so. Information held privately by one member is not neutral. It is a piece missing from a system that needs it to function.

Somebody has to start it, and there is a reason nobody in that family could.

**3.** You are the neutral party. Does that appear anywhere in how you describe your role to clients?

**4.** One bad attempt closes the door on the next one. Where have you inherited a family that had already tried and failed, and how did you handle it?

# DAY 24
# They Were Less Willing to Have Another Conversation
## The Same Topic, But a Completely Different Experience
**"Gracious words are a honeycomb, sweet to the soul and healing to the bones."**
PROVERBS 16:24

So we stepped back and approached it differently. Instead of starting with conclusions or positions, we started with understanding. We asked each person to share how they were thinking, without interruption. And we slowed the conversation down.

That changed everything. The same topic, but a completely different experience.

I have thought about that family for a long time, because the outcome makes an uncomfortable claim and I want to state it plainly rather than soften it.

Nothing about their situation improved between the two conversations. The facts were identical. The differences between them were the same differences. Nobody had a change of heart in the intervening weeks and nobody conceded anything. The only variable that moved was how the conversation was run.

Which means the first conversation did not fail because of the subject or the family. It failed because of the structure, and structure is something an outsider can supply.

That is an encouraging conclusion and it is also a demanding one. If the structure is the variable, then the outcome is largely your responsibility, and an advisor cannot walk out of a bad family meeting and attribute it to the family.

Let me be specific about what changed, because it was three things and none of them are sophisticated.

We started with understanding rather than with positions. In the first conversation people arrived having decided what they wanted and led with it, which meant the first ten minutes established four incompatible destinations and everything after was a defense of one of them. Starting with how each person is thinking produces a different opening: four accounts rather than four demands, and accounts can be listened to.

Each person shared without interruption. That one rule does more work than everything else combined. Interruption is not just rude, it is informational. It tells the room that finishing a thought is contingent on somebody else's patience, and people who learn that mid-meeting begin editing while they speak.

We slowed it down. Reactivity is a function of speed. A room moving fast produces defenses because defending is quick and thinking is not.

There is a principle in Proverbs 16:24. Gracious words are a honeycomb, sweet to the soul and healing to the bones.

Healing is the word I want, and it is the reason this verse belongs to this day rather than to a day about pleasantness.

Bones get healed because something was broken. The verse is not describing a nice atmosphere in a room where nothing has gone wrong. It is describing repair, which means it applies exactly to a family that has already had the bad conversation and concluded that these conversations do not work.

I have watched that repair happen and it does not take a year. What it takes is one conversation that goes better than the family expected, on the same subject that went badly before. That single experience is what revises their conclusion, and it revises it quickly, because families are not stubborn about this. They are working from evidence, and you have just given them new evidence.

So if you inherit a family that has tried and failed, do not avoid the subject that failed. Go back to the same one. A new subject that goes well proves nothing about the old one.

Same topic, run differently, is the whole treatment.

**3.** Interruption teaches people to edit while they speak. Where does interruption go unchecked in the meetings you run?

**4.** A new subject that goes well proves nothing about the old one. What failed subject have you been avoiding on a family's behalf?

# DAY 25
# Listening Without Immediately Responding
## So People Can Think Instead of React
**"Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry."**
JAMES 1:19

You focus on a few key things. First, creating space for each person to speak. Second, listening without immediately responding. And third, slowing the pace of the conversation so people can think instead of react.

Those three things alone can change the dynamic significantly.

The middle one is the hardest and it is the one I want to spend the reading on, because it is the only one that requires something of you continuously rather than once at the start.

Space is a decision you make before the meeting. Pace is largely set by your own tempo. But listening without immediately responding has to be sustained across an hour, against an instinct that fires every time somebody finishes a sentence.

Let me say what it actually means, because it is not a general encouragement to listen well.

It means that when a person stops talking, nobody answers them. The next person speaks about their own view rather than about the previous one. Responses are held until every account is on the table, and only then does anyone address what anyone else said.

That feels wrong for about four minutes. Then it stops feeling wrong, and what emerges is a room where finishing a thought is not contingent on anybody's patience.

The mechanism is worth understanding rather than just copying. When responses are immediate, every speaker after the first is speaking into an argument that has already started. They adjust. They anticipate the objection and pre-answer it, or they harden because they have just watched somebody get corrected. By the fourth person, nobody is describing their own view anymore. They are positioning against three others.

Hold the responses and each person describes their actual position, because there is nothing yet to position against.

I will admit the version of this I got wrong for years. I thought listening meant not talking. It does not. A person can be entirely silent and still be visibly preparing, and families read that instantly. The leaning forward, the small nod that means yes I have already understood you and I am waiting, the pen that starts moving. All of it communicates that the listening has stopped and the responding has begun.

I also want to name something practical about the room. I prefer to do this in person. I do not particularly like doing it over video. You pick up body language and other cues face to face that simply do not come through on a screen, and in a conversation where most of the information is in how something is said, that matters more than it does in a technical meeting. Sometimes video is the practical option and I use it. For the conversation that matters most, I will get on a plane.

There is a principle in James 1:19. Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry.

That verse appeared earlier in this material in its shorter form. Here it is worth taking whole, because the third clause is the one that explains the other two.

Anger is placed last, after listening and speaking, and the order is not decorative. Slow listening produces fast speech, and fast speech produces the anger. It is a sequence rather than a list of three separate virtues, and it means the intervention point is at the front. By the time a family conversation has become heated, the failure happened two steps earlier.

You cannot manage the anger in the room. You can manage the speed at the front of it.
## REFLECT

**2.** A person can be silent and visibly preparing. What do you do with your body while someone is talking, and what does it tell them?

**3.** By the fourth person, nobody is describing their own view. Where have you watched a meeting turn into positioning without noticing when?

**4.** The intervention point is at the front. What would that change about when you step into a conversation that is heating up?
