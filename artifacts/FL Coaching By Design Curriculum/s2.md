> **PROVERBS 20:5**

Your training rewarded you for knowing things. Somewhere early on you learned that the moment of value in a client meeting is the moment you say something the client could not have said himself, and you have been optimizing for that moment ever since. It is not a flaw. It is most of what a good advisor is for.

It also stops working at a specific point, and the point is predictable. It stops working when the question in front of the family is not technical. Nobody in the room lacks information about whether the son should run the business. What they lack is a way to say what they actually think about it in front of each other. You can bring a recommendation into that room and watch it die on the table, and the reason it died had nothing to do with whether it was correct.

Tom's framing of this is that the role changes. You are not being asked to stop being an expert. You are being asked to stop leading with the expertise, which is a different thing and considerably harder, because the habit of leading with it is what got you here.

You are drawing things out, not just putting things in.
## OPENING STORY
### They Had Answers
The family was not underserved. That was the first thing Tom noticed when he came into it.

There were several advisors around them, all competent, all doing careful work. The technical recommendations were sound. The documents were current. Nobody was asleep. If you had graded the file on the usual criteria it would have graded well.

And nothing was moving.

Every decision that mattered had stalled somewhere short of a conclusion. There was hesitation in the room that nobody named. Some tension that surfaced sideways, in scheduling problems and in meetings that ended without decisions. When Tom asked what was holding things up, he got explanations that did not explain anything.

What he eventually concluded was not that the advice had been wrong. It was that the advice had never been the missing piece. The family had not had the conversations they needed to have with each other, and no amount of good recommendations was going to substitute for that.

Tom put it plainly: they had answers, but they did not have alignment.

So he stopped bringing recommendations into the room. He started with questions instead. What matters most to you here. How are you thinking about the future. What concerns you about this.

Then he did the part that most advisors find hardest, which is that he listened without moving toward a solution.

As those conversations went on, people in that family started to understand each other in ways they had not before. And when the decisions came back around, they were not difficult anymore. Nothing about the technical picture had changed. The recommendations that had been sitting there for months were the same recommendations.

The breakthrough had not come from a better answer.

## THE FRAMEWORK
### The Two Sides of Legacy Planning
Tom holds these as two sides of one job rather than as two specialties. An advisor who works only one side of it will produce plans that are correct and families that come apart anyway.

- **The Technical Side.** The financial, legal, and tax dimensions of a wealth transfer. Structures, documents, valuations, titling, tax exposure. Almost every professional around the family is working this side, and most of them are working it well.

- **The Relational Side.** The family itself. Values, faith, communication between generations, the readiness of the people who will receive what is being moved. Almost nobody around the family is assigned to this side, which is why it is usually where the failure originates.

### Legacy Planning Distinctives
The difference between traditional planning and legacy planning shows up in three places, and the third one is the one that matters:

- Traditional planning moves **financial wealth**. Legacy planning defines wealth transfer broadly, to include emotional, social, intellectual, financial, and spiritual dimensions alongside the legal documents.

- Traditional planning defines outcomes **materially**. Legacy planning defines outcomes **relationally**.

- Traditional planning is **transactional** in nature. Legacy planning is **transformational** in nature.

That last distinction is not a slogan and it has a practical test attached. A transaction ends. A transformation has to be maintained, which means the relationship continues past the closing, and that is precisely why this work holds families through a transition when a transaction-based relationship does not.

**Take one active engagement. Which side are you actually working, and who around that family is working the other one?**

## THE TOOL
### The Four Essential Skills
Tom names four skills a legacy planner has to be able to demonstrate, and he puts them in this order deliberately. The order is the tool.

- **Relational skills.** The capacity to be in a room with a family under strain and remain someone they are glad is there.

- **Listening skills.** Not waiting. Listening for what is underneath the sentence, and for who in the room has stopped talking.

- **Question asking skills, to gain understanding.** The phrase Tom uses is worth keeping intact. The purpose of the question is understanding, not extraction. You are not building a file.

- **Technical skills.** Estate planning, and the governance and succession side: family governance, business governance, the family council, and the distinction between ownership and management.

**How to run it on yourself.** Take your last three significant client meetings. Score each of the four from 1 to 5 for what you actually did in the room rather than what you are capable of. Most advisors find the fourth is a 5 and the second is a 2, and that the second is the one clients would name if asked what they valued.

Then do the harder version. Ask a colleague who has sat in with you to score you on the same four. The gap between your score and theirs is the working number.

Tom's own summary of the job is that technical knowledge helps, a tax or estate background helps, and the relational skills matter more. He adds one condition to that, which is having enough humility to find genuine experts in the areas that are not your strengths.

### Day One: The Heart of a Legacy Coach
*Family Legacy Coaching by Design*, Chapter 3, opening sections

Tom's case that the relational capacity of the advisor is not a personality trait but something developed on purpose.

**Coaching insight.** Read this looking for the word he keeps returning to, which is heart, and notice that he never uses it sentimentally. He means the settled decision about whose interests you are actually serving, made in advance, before the meeting where it costs you something. Identify the last decision you made where those interests diverged.

### Day Two: Building Relationships Across Generations
*Family Legacy Coaching by Design*, Chapter 3, later sections

Why building relationships with every generation of a family, rather than only the generation who signed the agreement, is a structural requirement of this work.

**Coaching insight.** Count the generations you have actually met in your top ten relationships. Not been introduced to at a wedding. Sat with. If the number is low, you are not behind in a personal sense, you are exposed in a business sense, and the exposure resolves in exactly one direction when the first generation is gone.

### Day Three: The Right Heart and a Multigenerational Mindset
*Family Legacy Coaching by Design*, Chapter 4, characteristics one and two

The first two of the five key characteristics of a successful legacy planner.

**Coaching insight.** The multigenerational mindset is the one advisors underestimate, because it sounds like an attitude and it is actually a set of scheduling and note-taking decisions. It means the twenty-nine-year-old daughter appears in your calendar, in your CRM, and in your succession plan for the relationship. Attitude that never touches the calendar is not a mindset.

### Day Four: A Collaborative Mindset and a Caring Mindset
*Family Legacy Coaching by Design*, Chapter 4, characteristics three and four

Working with the advisors already around a family, and the difference between understanding a client's assets and understanding a client.

**Coaching insight.** The collaborative mindset has a cost attached, which is that you will sometimes recommend bringing in someone who does part of your job better than you do. Decide in advance how you will handle that, because deciding in the moment tends to go the other way. Notice also which existing advisor around your largest client you have never spoken to.

### Day Five: A Heart Free from the Love of Money
*Family Legacy Coaching by Design*, Chapter 4, characteristic five

The fifth characteristic, and the hardest one to assess in yourself.

**Coaching insight.** This is not a reading about generosity. It is about whether your recommendation would change if your compensation changed, and the only honest way to test it is retrospectively. Take one recommendation you made in the last year and ask what you would have advised if the fee were identical either way. Do that quietly and do not report the answer to anyone.

The expertise stays. What changes is when you deploy it, and the discipline is almost entirely about sequence. Question, then silence, then understanding, then the recommendation that now has somewhere to land.

Next session takes up the first thing you draw out, which is what a family actually values, and it turns out there are five places to look.
