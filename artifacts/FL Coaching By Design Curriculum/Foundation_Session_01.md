## INTRODUCTION

> **KEY VERSE**
>
> "A good person leaves an inheritance for their children's children."
>
> **PROVERBS 13:22**

You already know how to do the technical work. You can model a distribution, read a trust, spot a basis problem, and tell a client something he does not want to hear about his buy-sell agreement. None of that is what this series is about, and none of it is going to be explained back to you.

Here is the part worth naming once, plainly, and then leaving alone. A generational transfer is moving through the families whose names are in your book right now. *[Wealth transfer figure and citation pending. See build notes.]* Some of those relationships will survive the transfer and most will not, and the ones that survive tend to have two things in common: somebody asked the family the questions nobody else was asking, and the next generation was already in the room before it mattered. Holding a few large relationships through a transition preserves a great deal of assets under management, and that is an honest reason to be reading this. It is rarely the reason advisors stay with the work.

This session is not about your clients. It is about what you believe legacy actually is, because that belief quietly determines which questions occur to you in a meeting and which ones never come to mind. Most advisors have never been asked to say it out loud.

Whether it is intentional or not, something is always being passed on.

## OPENING STORY

### Everything Looked Strong

The parents had done very well. Tom had the statements in front of him and there was nothing in them to worry about. Resources built patiently over decades, structures in place, documents current. From the outside, and frankly from the inside of most advisory relationships, this was a family that had it handled.

Then he spent time with the children.

There was no shared sense of what the family stood for. No real connection to any purpose behind what had been built. When he asked them how they thought about what their parents had accumulated, what came back was mostly uncertainty. Not resentment, and not entitlement either. They simply did not know what it was for.

Tom put it this way: the resources were there, but the deeper part of the legacy had never actually been passed on.

What made it worse was that the questions were already forming. *What is this for. What are we supposed to do with it. How are we even supposed to think about it.* Nobody had asked those out loud, and nobody had answered them, and in the absence of answers the children were quietly writing their own.

So he changed where the conversation started. Instead of opening with assets, he opened with purpose. What matters most to you. What do you want this family to be known for. What has shaped the way you think about money and about work and about each other.

It took a while. But the children began to see something they had not seen before, which was not what existed, but why it existed.

The money had been transferred long before any of it moved.

## BUILDING A HEALTHY CULTURE

This section is about your practice, not your client's family.

Tom spent years as head of development for an international missions agency, sitting across from wealthy donors, and he got in the habit of asking each of them three questions. What kind of professional advisors do you have. Do you have anyone who knows your goals, knows everything you own rather than one account or one policy, and has built a strategy that connects the two. Do your advisors share your convictions.

The answer to the second question was almost always no.

That is worth sitting with, because the people he was asking were not underserved. They had a CPA, an attorney, a planner, a money manager, someone for insurance. What they did not have was anyone holding the whole picture.

Your team either produces that kind of advisor or it does not, and it is mostly a matter of what gets rewarded internally. If the only things that get talked about in your Monday meeting are flows, production, and deadlines, then the relational side of this work will stay a personal hobby for whoever happens to care about it, and it will not survive that person's vacation.

A practice that can do legacy coaching has a few observable traits. Someone other than the lead advisor knows the family. Meeting notes capture what the client said about his children, not only what he decided about his portfolio. There is room in the calendar for a conversation that does not produce a transaction. And when a junior person asks a client something unusually good, that gets noticed out loud.

None of that requires a reorganization. It requires deciding what your team talks about.

**Where does the relational side of this work currently live in your practice, and what happens to it when you are out for two weeks?**

## THE FRAMEWORK

### The Three Kinds of Legacies

Most people, including most advisors, hold only one of these three at a time. Tom works all three, and the reason is practical rather than philosophical. A client who has never examined what he received will unconsciously repeat it or unconsciously invert it, and either way he is not choosing.

- **The legacy you have received.** What came down to you from the people who went before, financially and otherwise. Most of it arrived without commentary. Some of it you are still carrying without having agreed to it.

- **The legacy you will pass on.** What is scheduled to move, and what is intended to move. These are frequently not the same list, and the gap between them is where most of the trouble sits.

- **The legacy you are living out today.** The one currently in motion. It is being observed and recorded by the people who live with you, and it is the only one of the three you can still change.

### The Twin W's of Legacy

Tom keeps two words side by side through the entire process:

- **Wealth**
- **Wisdom**

Wealth transfers on its own. It has documents, custodians, deadlines, and a great many professionals attending to it. Wisdom transfers only if somebody decides to transfer it, and there is no institution assigned to the job.

That imbalance is the whole reason this work exists. Legacy, in the definition Tom uses, is something received from someone who has died and something transmitted from an ancestor or predecessor. Read from the receiving end it sounds passive. Read from the giving end it is entirely a set of choices, and most of them get made by default.

The most significant part of what people leave behind has very little to do with money. It has to do with values, character, and memories.

**Take one client family. Which of the three legacies has that family never examined, and what would you have to ask to open it?**

## THE TOOL

### The Reality Check

This is the first thing Tom puts in front of a family, and he does not soften it. He runs it as a list, read out loud, one line at a time.

- I will **die**.
- I will take **nothing** with me.
- I will probably die at a time I did not **anticipate**.
- Someone else will get my **stuff**.
- I can only decide, before I die, who gets my **stuff** after I die.

Nothing on that list is news to anyone in the room. That is exactly why it works. Tom is not delivering information, he is removing the option of treating this as hypothetical, and the last line is the one that does the work. The only decision available is the one made in advance.

He follows it with a line he has used for years: there will be a family meeting. The only question is whether you are going to be in it. People gather when someone dies and they talk about the stuff either way.

**How he uses it in the room.** Tom sat with a client worth roughly forty-five million, most of it in real estate, with four married children. The documents left five hundred thousand to each child and the rest to charity. Tom asked whether the children knew. They did not. The client had told them only that they should not expect anything.

So they held a meeting. The client's wife, it turned out, had never agreed with the number, and he moved it to a million each. Ten people were in the room, and the client had one vote out of ten rather than running the meeting. He told them what he intended, and that the gifts would come in the next few years rather than at his death, and that the bulk of the estate was still going to charity.

Tom's point about that meeting was not the tax outcome. It was what did not happen afterward. Had the client died with none of it said, the children would have received a number and built a story around it. *Did he not trust us. What did he think we would do with it.* Tom calls those vain imaginations, the explanations people construct when they have a fact and no context. Families do it constantly, and once one takes hold it is very hard to dislodge.

The Reality Check is what earns you the right to ask the next question. Run it first.
