# Not Defined by What Is Accumulated
## It's Defined by What Is Carried Forward
**"So the next generation would know them, and they in turn would tell their children."**
PSALM 78:6-7

You're helping them focus on what continues beyond them.

A lasting legacy is not defined by what is accumulated. It's defined by what is carried forward.

And that brings us to the next generation. Because no matter how well something is built, if the next generation isn't prepared, it's very difficult for that legacy to continue in a meaningful way.

I want to hold the two verbs against each other, because our entire profession is organized around the first one.

Accumulation is measurable, it is reported quarterly, and it is what a client asks about when he calls. Nobody is confused about how it works. It also has a property worth noticing: accumulation is complete at the moment of the client's death. Whatever the number is, it is final, and everything our industry does culminates in producing that number and moving it.

Carrying forward has none of those properties. It is not measurable, nobody reports on it, and it begins at the moment the other thing ends. It also cannot be done by the person who built it. Every other part of a client's financial life can be handled by professionals he hires. This one part can only be done by people he did not hire and cannot supervise, which is why it is the only part he has to prepare for in advance.

That asymmetry is the whole argument of this session and I have never met an advisor who disputed it once it was put plainly. What I have met is a great many who had never put it plainly.

Here is the version I use with clients, and it tends to land.

You have spent forty years building something that will be handed to four people, and you have spent almost nothing preparing the four people. If a stranger were doing this with a business you owned, you would call it a governance failure.

That framing works because it is not sentimental and it is not a criticism of anyone's parenting. It is an observation about resource allocation, and business owners understand resource allocation.

There is a principle in Psalm 78:6 and 7. So the next generation would know them, and they in turn would tell their children.

Notice the reach. It is not one handoff. It is a handoff followed by another handoff performed by the person who received the first one, which means the standard of success in that passage is the third generation rather than the second.

That is a considerably higher bar than most families are working toward, and it changes what preparation has to include. Preparing a child to receive is one job. Preparing a child to hand it on again is a different and larger job, because now they have to understand it well enough to explain it to somebody who was not there.

Understanding well enough to explain is the actual test, and most heirs cannot pass it. They know what they were given. They frequently cannot say why.

That's the picture of legacy. It continues from one generation to the next.

If it stops with the people you told, you did not build a legacy. You made a very large gift.

**3.** The standard in Psalm 78 is the third generation. Which of your families is working toward the second and stopping there?

**4.** Understanding well enough to explain it to someone who was not there. Which heir you know could pass that test?

# Preparation Doesn't Happen Automatically
## It's Not Just Exposure, It's Engagement
**"Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord."**
EPHESIANS 6:4

It starts with intentionality. Preparation doesn't happen automatically.

It's not just exposure. It's engagement.

That distinction is the most useful thing in this session and it is also the easiest to nod at without absorbing, so let me make it concrete.

Exposure is what almost every family already provides. The children know the business exists. They have been to the office. They understand roughly what the family owns and they have heard the founder's opinions about money at a hundred dinners. Nobody has hidden anything from them and no reasonable person would say they have been kept in the dark.

Engagement is different in one specific way. Something is asked of them.

That is the whole difference. Exposure is a broadcast and engagement requires a response. A child who has been exposed knows about the family enterprise. A child who has been engaged has done something in it, been accountable for something, been asked a question that had to be answered, and been present when a decision went a way he did not prefer.

The reason exposure fails is that it produces familiarity without competence, and familiarity is worse than ignorance because it feels like preparation. A thirty-year-old who has heard about the business his whole life will believe he understands it. He will not find out otherwise until he is responsible for it, and finding out then is expensive.

I want to be careful not to make this sound like a program. Families hear engagement and imagine an internship or a formal training track, and most families do not need that and will not do it. Engagement can be very small. Ask a daughter what she thinks about a decision before it is made and then tell her what you decided and why. That is engagement. It took ten minutes and it asked something of her.

There is a principle in Ephesians 6:4. Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord.

Two halves and both are load-bearing.

Bring them up is active and continuous. It is not an event and it does not describe something that happens as a byproduct of living in the same house. Somebody does it, deliberately, over years.

Do not exasperate is the warning, and I find it the more useful half for the families I work with. Exasperation is what happens when a person is held to a standard nobody explained, or given a responsibility without the information required to carry it, or told they will inherit something and never taught anything about it.

That describes a great many second generations I have met. They are not ungrateful. They are exasperated, in the precise sense of the word, and the exasperation is the predictable result of being exposed to something for thirty years and engaged in it never.

If you want a single test for a family, it is this. Has anyone ever asked this child a question about the family enterprise that his answer might have changed?

For most families the answer is no, and the fix costs one meeting.

**4.** Has anyone ever asked one of these children a question whose answer might have changed something?

# Assumed the Next Generation Would Figure Things Out
## The Transition Was More Difficult Than Expected
**"Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not turn from it."**
PROVERBS 22:6

I worked with two families who approached this very differently.

One family had built significant resources and assumed that over time, the next generation would figure things out. There was some exposure, but not much intentional involvement.

When the time came for greater responsibility, there was uncertainty. The next generation didn't feel fully prepared, and the transition was more difficult than expected.

The phrase I want is more difficult than expected, because it contains an assumption that had never been examined.

Somebody had an expectation. Nobody had ever stated what it was based on. The expectation was that these adults, being capable people who had grown up around this, would rise to it when the moment came, and that expectation had never been tested against anything because there had been no occasion to test it.

That is what assuming they will figure it out actually means. It is not negligence and it is not optimism. It is a forecast made without data, held confidently, about people who were never consulted about whether the forecast was accurate.

And I want to be fair to that family, because advisors read this story and conclude the parents were careless. They were not. Nobody had ever told them that preparation was a category of work. Every professional around them was working on the transfer, and the transfer was being handled superbly, and not one person in their advisory relationships had ever raised the question of whether anybody was ready to receive it.

That silence is on us as much as the assumption is on them.

Here is what makes this failure mode particularly hard to catch. The children in that family did not object during the years of assumption. Nobody said, I am not ready for this. They did not know they were not ready, because readiness is not a thing a person can assess from the outside of a responsibility. You find out you were unprepared by being handed something, and by then the discovery is the crisis rather than a warning about it.

So the family had no signal. The parents assumed. The children did not know enough to contradict the assumption. Everyone proceeded in good faith toward a transition that was going to be harder than any of them expected, and the first evidence of the problem arrived on the day it became the problem.

That is why this cannot be left to surface on its own. It will not surface. It only presents.

There is a principle in Proverbs 22:6. Start children off on the way they should go.

Start is the word. Not tell them about, not provide for, not expose them to. Start them, which implies motion that begins somewhere identifiable and continues.

The verse is often read as a guarantee about outcomes and I do not use it that way, because families who have watched a child go a different direction find that reading cruel and they are right to. What I take from it is the instruction at the front. Somebody starts it. It does not start itself, and there is no age at which it becomes too late to begin, though there are ages at which it becomes considerably more expensive.

Nobody in that family expected the transition to be hard. That was the entire problem.
## REFLECT

**3.** Readiness cannot be assessed from outside the responsibility. What does that mean for how much weight you give a client's reassurance about his children?

**4.** It does not surface, it presents. What is presenting somewhere in your book that you could have caught earlier?

# Not All at Once, But Over Time
## A Sense of Readiness and Confidence
**"Know therefore that the LORD your God is God; he is the faithful God, keeping his covenant of love to a thousand generations."**
DEUTERONOMY 7:9

The other family took a different approach. They involved the next generation gradually. They created opportunities for conversation, for understanding, and for participation.

Not all at once, but over time.

And when the time came for greater responsibility, there was a sense of readiness and confidence.

I have watched advisors hear that and reach for the wrong conclusion, which is that this family did more. They did not necessarily do more. They did it earlier and in smaller pieces, and the smallness is the operative fact.

Gradual is not a softer version of thorough. It is a different mechanism, and it works for a reason that has nothing to do with volume.

A person cannot absorb responsibility in a single transfer. What actually builds capability is a sequence of small responsibilities, each one slightly beyond the last, each carried long enough to become ordinary before the next arrives. That is how every competence any of us has was built, and there is no version of it that can be compressed into a year of intensive preparation before a transition.

Which means the timeline is not negotiable and it is not a matter of effort. A family that starts eighteen months out cannot produce what a family that started at ten years out has, regardless of how much they spend or how good their advisor is. Time is the input.

I want to be honest that this is discouraging for some of the families you serve, and I do not soften it with them. If a client is seventy-eight and nothing has been started, we are not going to build in two years what should have been built in twenty. What we can do is considerably better than nothing, and we should start today, and I say that plainly rather than pretending the arithmetic is different.

The other thing worth naming is what readiness and confidence actually are.

They are not knowledge. A next generation can be told everything, in detail, and arrive at a transition with complete information and no confidence at all, because confidence does not come from being informed. It comes from having done something and found out you could. That is why participation is on that list alongside conversation and understanding. Two of the three build knowledge. Only the third builds the thing that makes a person able to act.

There is a principle in Deuteronomy 7:9. He is the faithful God, keeping his covenant of love to a thousand generations.

A thousand generations is not a number anyone is meant to calculate with. It is a statement about the direction of the horizon, and I find it useful precisely because it is absurd against a human planning span.

Every family I work with is planning to the second generation and occasionally to the third. That verse describes a commitment that operates on a timescale where no individual's contribution is visible, which is exactly the timescale a legacy actually runs on. The founder of a family enterprise does not get to see whether it worked. Neither does his son.

Working on something whose outcome you will not observe requires a different motivation than the ones our profession usually supplies. That is the honest cost of this material and it is worth naming for a client who is being asked to start something slow.

The gradual family did not work harder. They started sooner, and then they kept going.

**4.** Working on something whose outcome you will not observe. What is your own motivation for that, honestly?

# DAY 30
# They're Engaged, Not Just Aware
## You Don't Have to Do Everything at Once
**"Entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others."**
2 TIMOTHY 2:2

You begin to see continuity. The next generation understands what has been built and why. They're engaged, not just aware. And there's a shared sense of responsibility.

The legacy is not just being transferred. It's being carried forward.

One of the tools I use with families tracks progress over time, so a family can see what has been accomplished and what still lies ahead. I recently reviewed one of these with a family I have worked with since 2016. We celebrated what had happened. Revised estate documents. A funded donor advised fund. Paid off mortgages. We also noted what still required attention, including a zero estate tax plan and a family mission trip that had not yet happened.

I tell that story at the end because of what it is not.

It is not a completion. That family has been at this for years and there are still items in the second column, and there will be items in the second column the next time we meet. Nobody graduates from this.

What they have is continuity, which is a different achievement and a better one. A family that has been reviewed annually for years has a rhythm rather than a project, and the next generation has grown up inside that rhythm, watching decisions get made and revisited, seeing the reasons stated out loud, participating in a conversation that predates their own involvement in it. That is what produces a shared sense of responsibility. Not a document. Repetition.

There is a principle in 2 Timothy 2:2. Entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others.

That's generational thinking. You're not just passing something on. You're preparing someone to carry it forward.

Notice that the qualification is teaching rather than holding. The standard is not whether a person can receive what you hand them. It is whether they can hand it on again, which requires them to understand it well enough to explain it to someone who was not there.

That has been the argument of these thirty days in one line, and it applies to your clients and to you in exactly the same way.

Start by involving the next generation in one meaningful way. Invite them into a conversation. Help them understand the bigger picture. Ask them how they're thinking about it.

You don't have to do everything at once. Just begin the process.

I would end where the work actually ends, which is not with a plan.

Somewhere in these thirty days you wrote something in a Bucket that nobody will read. A person you have exposed and never engaged. A conversation you have not started because it matters too much. A forecast you are holding without data. Something you have seen clearly for a year and done nothing about.

That is the item. Not the client family, though the client family matters. The one you wrote down when nobody was looking.

You have been asking families for six weeks to do the thing you have been putting off. The families will do it if somebody asks them.

Somebody has to ask you.
