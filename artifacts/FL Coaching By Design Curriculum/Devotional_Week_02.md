# DAY 6
# What Begins to Change Is the Role
## Most Advisors Are Trained to Give Advice
**"Now it is required that those who have been given a trust must prove faithful."**
1 CORINTHIANS 4:2

Most advisors are trained to give advice. That is the model. Analyze the situation, make recommendations, provide solutions. I was trained that way, I was rewarded for it, and I would not undo any of it.

But when you are working with families around legacy, that approach has limits.

I want to be exact about where the limit sits, because advisors hear this and think they are being asked to be less rigorous. You are not. The limit is not about the quality of the advice. It is about what kind of problem is in front of you.

An advice problem has a right answer that the client does not have and you do. Which entity, which structure, which order of operations. In that situation, withholding your answer is not humility, it is malpractice. Give the answer.

A legacy problem does not work that way. When the question is whether the son should run the business, or what the money is supposed to accomplish, or how much is enough, there is no correct answer sitting in your training waiting to be delivered. The answer is inside the family and it has never been articulated, sometimes not even privately. You cannot analyze your way to something that does not exist yet.

So what begins to change is the role. You are still bringing expertise, but you are not leading with answers. You are helping the family think, clarify, and move forward together.

That sentence sounds modest and it is not. It is a different job with a different skill set and a different definition of a good meeting. In the advice model, a good meeting ends with a decision. In this one, a good meeting often ends with a family understanding something about itself that it did not understand at nine o'clock, and no decision at all.

I know how that reads to a producer. I have been in the rooms where that conversation happens.

Here is what I would say to an advisor weighing it. I knew one who had been managing accounts for decades and did not need another dollar under management. What he wanted was to help families stay whole. He began guiding them through conversations about faith, values, and purpose, and he told me later that for the first time in his career he felt like he was doing work that matters forever. He could have retired. Instead he found a different kind of calling.

That is not everyone's situation and I am not pretending it is. Plenty of advisors reading this are still building, and the income has to work. What I would say to them is that this shift is not a luxury purchased with success. It is a way to create value that no algorithm touches, and families pay for guidance in a way they increasingly do not pay for allocation.

There is a principle in 1 Corinthians 4. Those who have been given a trust must prove faithful.

Notice what is required. Not brilliance. Not the best answer in the room. Faithfulness with something that was handed to you and belongs to someone else.

That is a more accurate description of this work than anything in my professional training. A family's understanding of itself is not yours. It was handed to you, temporarily, because they let you into a place they do not let many people. What you do with that is a question about faithfulness rather than a question about competence, and the two get confused constantly in a profession that measures only one of them.

The expertise is not going anywhere. What changes is when you reach for it.

**3.** Where has your training served you well enough that you have stopped noticing you are applying it?

**4.** Faithfulness with something that belongs to someone else. What has a family handed you that you have been treating as yours to direct?

# DAY 7
# You're Drawing Things Out
## Not Just Putting Things In
**"The purposes of a person's heart are deep waters, but one who has insight draws them out."**
PROVERBS 20:5

There is a principle in Proverbs 20:5. The purposes of a person's heart are deep waters, but one who has insight draws them out.

That is what guidance looks like. You're drawing things out, not just putting things in.

I use that verse more than any other in this work, and I want to walk through why, because the image in it is doing something precise that gets missed when people treat it as a compliment about being perceptive.

Deep waters. Not hidden waters, and not muddy ones. Deep means the thing is genuinely there, in quantity, and it is below the surface for reasons that have nothing to do with concealment. A client is not withholding what matters most to him. In most cases he has never had occasion to bring it up, because nobody in his professional life has ever created a setting where it would be appropriate to say.

Then the verb. Draws them out. That is work performed by the person with insight, not by the person with the depth. The client is not expected to volunteer. If you sit across from a man and wait for him to raise what his money is for, you will wait a long time and conclude he does not think about it, and you will be wrong.

Now put that against the model most of us run. Putting things in is what we are trained to do and what we are paid for. Research, analysis, options, a recommendation. All of it moving in one direction, from you toward the client, and every bit of it well made.

The difficulty is that a recommendation lands on top of whatever is already down there. If a client has an unstated conviction about what the business is supposed to mean for his children, your recommendation either happens to fit it or it does not, and you will not know which until it stalls. Advisors interpret that stall as indecision. It is usually a collision with something nobody said out loud.

Drawing out is not slower in the end. It is slower at the beginning, which is a different thing and it is the thing that gets it cut.

I will give you the mechanics, because this is learnable and it is not a personality trait.

You ask a question that has no technical answer, so the client cannot retreat into the part of the conversation he knows how to have. You do not follow it with a second question, which is the most common error and turns a conversation into an interview. You let the first answer sit, because the first answer is almost always the socially available one. Then you ask about the answer rather than moving to the next topic. What did you mean by that. Say more about that part.

That is the whole technique. It is not sophisticated and I have watched it produce material in twenty minutes that a decade of quarterly reviews did not.

I knew an advisor who had spent years being excellent at putting things in. He was technically strong and the work was good. What he told me after he changed his approach was that families started telling him things in the first meeting that his long-standing clients had never told him at all. Nothing about his competence had changed. He had stopped filling the water.

The insight in that verse is not knowing the answer. It is knowing the answer is already there.
## REFLECT

**3.** Asking a second question instead of asking about the first answer turns a conversation into an interview. Which do your meetings feel like?

**4.** Drawing out is slower at the beginning and not at the end. Who in your firm would need convincing of that, and with what evidence?

# DAY 8
# They Had Answers
## But They Didn't Have Alignment
**"Plans fail for lack of counsel, but with many advisers they succeed."**
PROVERBS 15:22

I worked with a family where this became very clear. They had a number of advisors who were doing good work. Strong technical advice, solid recommendations. But when it came to bigger decisions around the future, things weren't moving forward.

There was hesitation. Some tension. A lack of clarity.

What we realized was that the family hadn't really had the conversations they needed to have. They had answers, but they didn't have alignment.

I have come to think that is the most common condition in high net worth families, and it is nearly invisible, because every conventional measure of the situation reads as healthy. Good counsel is present. The documents are current. Nobody has been negligent. If you audited that family's professional relationships you would find nothing wrong.

And nothing was moving.

The specific error I made early in my career was diagnosing that stall as a decision problem. If a family cannot decide, the reasonable conclusion is that they lack information or lack a recommendation, and both of those have fixes I was trained to supply. So I supplied more. Better analysis, clearer options, a cleaner presentation. It did not help, and I want to be honest that it took more than one family before I stopped.

The stall was never about information. It was about the fact that four people in that room had never said out loud what they actually wanted, and were being asked to approve a plan that would settle it.

Once you see that, the hesitation stops looking like indecision and starts looking like self-protection, which is exactly what it is. Approving a plan you have not discussed is agreeing to an outcome you have not tested against the people it affects. Any sensible person hesitates.

So we changed the approach. Instead of starting with recommendations, we started with questions. What matters most to you. How are you thinking about the future. What concerns you.

And then we listened.

As those conversations unfolded, the family began to understand each other in a way they hadn't before. And once that happened, the decisions became much clearer.

I want to underline what did not change, because this is the part advisors do not believe until they watch it. The recommendations were the same recommendations. Nothing about the technical picture moved. The plan that had been sitting there for months, unsigned, was the plan they eventually adopted.

There is a principle in Proverbs 15:22. Plans fail for lack of counsel, but with many advisers they succeed.

I read that verse differently than I used to. That family had many advisers. By the plain reading, they should have been fine.

What they did not have was counsel in the sense the verse means, which is not the same as being advised. Counsel is a conversation. Being advised is a delivery. A family can be surrounded by six professionals, each supplying excellent material into their own lane, and still be receiving no counsel at all, because nobody is talking with them about the thing underneath and nobody is talking with each other.

Adding a seventh adviser to that family would not have helped. It would have added another lane.

The answers were never missing. The conversation was.

# DAY 9
# Uncomfortable With Silence
## That Space Is Where Clarity Begins to Emerge
**"The hearts of the wise make their mouths prudent, and their lips promote instruction."**
PROVERBS 16:23

Instead of jumping to a recommendation, you ask a question. You create space. You allow people to think and respond.

And that space is where clarity begins to emerge.

I have watched a great many advisors try this and abandon it inside of ten seconds, so I want to describe what actually happens in that room, because the failure is almost always at the same point and it is not a failure of conviction.

You ask something real. Nobody answers. Four seconds pass and the room reorganizes itself slightly. Someone looks at the table. At six seconds, you begin to experience it as your problem. At eight, you rephrase the question, which sounds generous and is in fact a rescue, and the moment is over.

Nothing in that sequence was about the client. The discomfort was yours and you resolved it at their expense.

I say that plainly because I did it for years and nobody told me.

Here is what I now understand about the pause. Silence after a hard question is not absence. It is a person locating something they have not put into words before, and that takes longer than conversation normally allows. Ordinary professional talk runs at a pace that only permits retrieval of things already formed. When you ask a question that has no pre-formed answer, the client has to build one, and building takes time that looks like nothing from the outside.

Fill it, and you have guaranteed that you will only ever receive answers that were already assembled. Those are the answers he has given to everyone else.

There are three of these obstacles and they are related. One is feeling like you need to have the answer right away. Another is being uncomfortable with silence. And sometimes there is a concern that if you are not giving answers, you are not adding value.

That third one is the root and the other two are symptoms. An advisor who is certain that his value is his output cannot tolerate a stretch of a meeting where he produces nothing. Every instinct he has says the client is watching the meter run.

But in reality, guiding a family through a meaningful process often creates more value than simply providing a solution.

I will offer the practical version, since conviction alone does not hold at eight seconds.

Look at the table rather than at anyone. Eye contact converts a pause into pressure. Put your pen down, which signals you are not waiting to capture something. Decide the number before you go in, and make it longer than feels reasonable. If it genuinely stalls, do not rephrase, because rephrasing tells the room the question was flawed rather than difficult. Ask them to write one sentence instead. That breaks almost every block I have encountered and it costs you nothing.

There is a principle in Proverbs 16:23. The hearts of the wise make their mouths prudent.

The order in that verse is worth sitting with. The heart governs the mouth, not the reverse. Prudence in speech is not a technique applied at the moment of speaking. It is downstream of something settled earlier, which is whether you believe your usefulness depends on being the one talking.

Settle that outside the meeting and the eight seconds stop being difficult.

The client is not waiting for you. He is working.

# DAY 10
# Start With One Question Before One Answer
## That Small Shift Can Begin to Change the Entire Dynamic
**"Let your conversation be always full of grace, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone."**
COLOSSIANS 4:6

Start with one question before one answer. Before you respond, ask something that helps you understand their perspective.

That small shift can begin to change the entire dynamic.

I have given a great deal of advice about this work over thirty years and if an advisor took only one thing from any of it, this would be the one I would choose. Not because it is the deepest idea. Because it is the only one that requires nothing from you except a decision made before the meeting.

Everything else in this material has a cost. Learning to sit in silence takes practice. Building relationships with a next generation takes years. Running a family meeting well takes preparation you probably do not currently do. One question before one answer costs you about forty seconds and it can be done tomorrow by an advisor who agrees with none of the rest of it.

That is why I put it last in the session and why I keep returning to it. It is the only entry point that does not require you to reorganize anything.

Let me be precise about the wording, because the shift is smaller than people make it. It is not ask questions instead of giving answers. It is one question before one answer. The answer still comes. You are not being asked to withhold your judgment, become a therapist, or pretend you do not know what you know. You are being asked to change the order.

The order matters because of what an answer does to a room. Once a recommendation is on the table, every subsequent contribution is a reaction to it. The client is no longer telling you what he thinks. He is telling you what he thinks about your idea, which is a much narrower and much less useful category of information, and you have permanently lost access to the first kind for that meeting.

Ask first and you get the unshaped version. It is often disorganized and occasionally it is wrong, and it is the only accurate picture of where that family actually is.

There is a second effect that takes longer to notice. A client who has been asked before being told begins to experience the meeting as his rather than yours. That is where ownership comes from, and ownership is what determines whether a plan gets implemented after you leave the room. I have watched excellent recommendations die because nobody in the family felt like it was theirs, and I have watched ordinary ones get executed with real energy because the family had built them.

I knew a younger advisor who came to sit in on a legacy coaching session with one of my families. He came only to observe and he did not say much. By the end of that meeting the family asked him to manage several million in assets they had never entrusted to anyone. The process also surfaced a need for buy-sell insurance in the family business, which was a second opportunity. He walked in expecting nothing.

Nothing he did in that room was technical. He watched a family get asked things, and by the end of it he had become someone they wanted in the room.

There is a principle in Colossians 4:6. Let your conversation be full of grace, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone.

Read the last clause carefully. So that you may know how to answer. The answering is still the objective and the verse does not apologize for it. What comes first is the character of the conversation, and the knowing comes out of that rather than out of preparation.

You will still answer. The question is only whether you will know what you are answering.
