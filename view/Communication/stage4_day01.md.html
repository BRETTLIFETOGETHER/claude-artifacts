Stage 4: Day 1, the master reading

Day 1

From Providing Answers to Guiding the Process

The real work happens through conversation

"Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry." James 1:19

Most advisors are trained to analyze and recommend. That is what the credential is for, that is what the client is paying for, and I am not going to stand here and tell you it does not matter. It matters. I am a CPA. I have spent a career on the technical side and I still do that work.

But when you are working with families, especially around legacy, the real work happens through conversation. And the shift I am asking you to make is from being the one who provides answers to being the one who guides the process.

Read that again, because it is easy to hear it as a softer version of your job. It is not. It is a different discipline, and most of us were never trained in it.

Here is what it looks like in practice. You walk into a review meeting with a plan you have prepared. You are ready. You know the structures, you have modeled the outcomes, and you have an answer to the question you expect them to ask. Everything about that preparation is aimed at one moment, which is the moment you present. The meeting is built to arrive there.

Now consider what that meeting costs the family. For the entire time you are presenting, nobody in that room is thinking about what they want. They are thinking about what you said. Your competence is filling the space, and there is no room left in it for anyone else's uncertainty.

I have watched a lot of good plans fail, and almost none of them failed because the analysis was wrong. They failed because the people the plan was built for had never been asked what they wanted, and so the plan was built to answer a question nobody in the family had actually asked.

Guiding the process means three things, and I want to be specific about them.

It means creating space. Not offering space, which is what most of us do when we say at the end of a presentation that we are happy to take any questions. Creating space means the space comes first, before the content, when there is still something at stake in what gets said.

It means asking the right questions. Not diagnostic questions that help you fill in a form. Questions that make somebody think about something they have not thought about, or have thought about privately for years and never said out loud.

And it means helping people think more clearly together. Together is the part that gets missed. A family can have five members who have each thought clearly on their own and have never once thought in the same room. That is a family with five plans, and none of them is the family's.

It is a posture of listening and guiding rather than directing. You are still bringing expertise. You are just not leading with it.

There is a principle in James 1:19 that sits underneath all of this. Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to become angry. Look at the order Paul's brother puts them in. Listening is first, and it is the only one of the three he asks us to be quick about. Everything else is slowed down.

That order is not a suggestion about temperament. It is a working sequence, and it is the opposite of how a professional meeting is usually built. We are quick to speak because speaking is what we are paid for, and we have organized an entire industry around rewarding the fastest good answer. Leading the conversation starts with listening, and listening is the thing that has to be quick, meaning it has to be first.

I will say the hard part plainly. If you cannot do this, somebody else will, and they will end up with the relationship. There is an enormous transfer running through your book right now, and the advisors who survive it are the ones whose clients' children know their name. That is not a marketing observation. It is what I have watched happen for thirty years.

You are still the expert in the room. You are simply no longer the one who has to be talking.

Reflect

  1. In your last five client meetings, roughly what share of the talking was yours? Not your best guess about how it should have been. What actually happened.

  2. Where does your sense of your own value depend on being the person who provides the answer?

  3. Name a plan you built that was technically right and never got carried out. What question was never asked at the beginning?

  4. What would change about your next meeting if you decided in advance that you were not going to present anything?
