Stage 5 part C: Days 11 through 15, Week Three

Day 11

It Usually Comes Down to Safety and Trust

They may respond, but they won't really open up

"To answer before listening, that is folly and shame." Proverbs 18:13

It usually comes down to safety and trust. If people do not feel safe, they will not engage. They may respond, but they will not really open up.

I want you to hold those two verbs apart, because the whole session is in the gap between them.

Responding is what a client does in a meeting where they have decided how much to give you. It is cooperative. It is polite. The answers are accurate as far as they go, and you will leave with a full page of notes. I have had two hour meetings where I got nothing but responses and did not notice until I tried to write the summary and found I could not say anything about that family I could not have said beforehand.

Opening up is different, and it costs the person something. They are telling you a thing that could be used, and they are handing you a piece of information that changes how you see them.

Nobody does the second one because you asked nicely. They do it because they have concluded it is safe.

So the practical question is what a person is actually assessing when they make that call, and it is not your credentials. It is three things, and you can influence all three.

The first is whether you will handle it well. Not whether you will agree. Whether the thing they say will be received without a flinch, a correction, or a look exchanged with someone else in the room.

The second is whether it will stay where they put it. In a family setting this is enormous, because they are not only trusting you, they are calculating what you will repeat to their mother.

The third is whether the meeting has room for it. This one is structural rather than personal, and it is the one advisors control most directly and think about least. If the agenda is full and you are watching the clock, the family can see that there is no space for the thing they were considering saying, and they will spare you.

Proverbs 18:13 is unusually harsh. To answer before listening is folly and shame. Not inefficiency. Not a missed opportunity. Shame, which is the language of disgrace rather than of technique.

I think that is right, and I think the reason is that answering before listening is a claim. It says I already know enough about your situation to tell you what to do about it. Made about a family's inner life, that claim is almost always false, and the person on the other side of it knows it is false, which is why the relationship never gets past responding.

I have been guilty of this more times than I would like to write down. The tell is that the meeting felt efficient.

Efficient meetings with families are usually meetings where nobody told me anything.

Reflect

  1. Which of your clients responds to everything and tells you nothing?

  2. Of the three assessments a person makes, which one are you weakest on?

  3. When has a client told you something costly, and what had happened just before?

  4. Where has a meeting of yours been too full for anyone to say the real thing?

Day 12

Not the Right Answer, the Right Environment

It starts with how the conversation is framed

"Like apples of gold in settings of silver is a ruling rightly given. Like an earring of gold or an ornament of fine gold is the rebuke of a wise judge to a listening ear." Proverbs 25:11 and 12

The first goal is not to get to the right answer. It is to create the right environment.

It starts with how the conversation is framed. If it feels like pressure, evaluation, or expectation, people tend to hold back. If it feels like an opportunity to share and be heard, they begin to engage.

The framing happens before the first substantive word, and most advisors have never given it a minute of thought, which means their meetings are framed by default. Default framing is almost always evaluative, because the professional context supplies it for you. A client sitting across from an expert, in an office, with documents on the table, will assume he is being assessed unless told otherwise.

So consider the four things that do the framing, because you control all of them.

The invitation does some of it. What you said this meeting was for is what the family will believe it is for, and if you said we should review the plan, then every answer they give will be aimed at the plan.

The room does some of it. I have watched the same conversation go differently on a different side of a table.

Who is present does a great deal of it. Adding one person changes what every other person is willing to say, and this is not a small effect. It is often the whole effect.

And the first three minutes do more than the other three combined. Whatever happens in the first three minutes establishes the genre of the meeting, and the family will play in that genre for the next two hours.

Here is a test you can run on your own material. Read your standard opening out loud and ask what it invites. Most of ours invite a report. We ask how things have been, and we get a summary, because we asked for a summary.

Proverbs 25 gives an image I keep coming back to. A word rightly given is like apples of gold in settings of silver. What strikes me is that the proverb bothers to describe the setting at all. The apples are the point, and the writer still tells you the silver matters.

Then the second verse adds the condition. The rebuke of a wise judge is precious to a listening ear. Two things are required for it to work. The rebuke has to be wise and the ear has to be listening, and if either is missing the value is not delivered.

That second condition is your job. You cannot make the ear listen, but you can build the setting in which a person becomes willing to.

Most of us have spent our careers on the gold and none of it on the silver.

Reflect

  1. What does your standard meeting invitation tell the client this meeting is for?

  2. Read your usual opening three minutes back to yourself. What genre does it establish?

  3. Who is in the room by habit rather than by decision, and what does their presence change?

  4. When has a family told you something in a hallway or a car park that they would not say inside?

Day 13

Questions That Create Space Instead of Pressure

You're opening it, not directing it

"Let your conversation be always full of grace, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone." Colossians 4:6

It starts with simple, open-ended questions. Not questions that lead to a specific answer, but questions that invite someone to share their perspective. Those kinds of questions create space instead of pressure.

You are opening it, not directing it.

I have found that advisors believe they are already doing this, and mostly they are not, and the reason is that the difference between an opening question and a directing question is not a matter of form. It is a matter of whether you have decided the answer in advance.

You can ask a perfectly open-ended question and still be steering. How are you thinking about the succession is open-ended in structure. If you already believe the son should take over and you are looking for a foothold to say so, it is a directing question wearing open-ended clothes, and the client will hear the direction rather than the question, because people are extremely good at detecting what is wanted from them.

So the test is not on the sentence. The test is on you. Ask yourself before the meeting what answer you are hoping for. If there is one, your question is directing.

The second thing that turns a question into pressure is naming the subject too precisely. A question that arrives with the topic already specified tells the person which drawer to open, and they will open only that drawer. What is most important to you here is a wider question than what is most important to you about the trust, and the wider one is more useful, because what comes out of it is what is actually on top for that person rather than what you nominated.

The third is asking two things at once. A double question is a pressure question, because the person has to choose which half to answer and the choice itself is exposing. They will take the safer half.

Simple is doing real work in that first sentence. Simple means one thing, wide, without a preferred answer.

Colossians 4:6 pairs two ideas that we would not naturally pair. Speech full of grace, and speech seasoned with salt. Grace is what makes it safe. Salt is what keeps it from being bland, and Paul is not describing a person who says nothing in particular pleasantly.

Then he gives the purpose, and the purpose is so that you may know how to answer each person. Not so you can deliver your answer. So you know how to answer this person, which means the speech Paul is describing is calibrated to the individual in front of you, and calibration requires that you have found something out first.

Grace and salt together, aimed at one particular person.

That is a good description of a question worth asking.

Reflect

  1. Before your next client meeting, what answer are you hoping for? Write it down and notice what that does to your questions.

  2. Where do you name the subject too precisely and get back only what you nominated?

  3. Which of your standard questions are really two questions?

  4. What is the widest question you could reasonably ask your largest client?

Day 14

And Then We Stopped and Listened

They began to hear each other without immediately reacting

"Not looking to your own interests but each of you to the interests of the others." Philippians 2:4

I worked with a family where this was very clear. There had been tension between the parents and their adult children, and previous conversations had not gone well. They had turned into either silence or disagreement.

So when we sat down, instead of starting with structure or decisions, we started with a simple question. What would you like your parents to understand about how you are thinking right now?

And then we stopped and listened.

At first there was hesitation. But as one person shared, it created space for the others. And for the first time, they began to hear each other without immediately reacting.

I want to point at the specific mechanism there, because it is easy to read that as a story about a good question and it is really a story about what happened after it.

The question matters, and notice what it does. It does not ask what they want. It asks what they would like their parents to understand, which means the answer is directed at a person in the room rather than at me. That is a deliberate move. It puts the family into conversation with each other rather than into a series of interviews with a consultant, and the difference shows up immediately in who people look at while they talk.

But the load is carried by the four words after it. We stopped and listened. Not I stopped. We, meaning I did not fill it and neither did anyone else, because I had made it clear that this was the moment for one person to speak.

Then the sentence I want you to underline. As one person shared, it created space for the others.

That is the whole thing. In a family that has not talked, the first honest statement is the expensive one, and every statement after it is cheaper. The first person is doing something without precedent and taking the entire risk. The second person is following someone. By the fourth, it is simply what is happening in this room today.

Which means your job in a first family conversation is narrower than it looks. You are not trying to get everybody to open up. You are trying to get one person to, and to make sure that when they do, nothing bad happens to them.

That is where the real skill sits. When the first person says the true thing, somebody will want to respond, and the response is the danger. Not because it is hostile. Because a response, even a warm one, converts a disclosure into a discussion, and the next three people watching will note that speaking here means being answered.

Philippians 2:4 asks each of you to look to the interests of others. Paul says each of you, which is a mutual instruction. In a family conversation, the mutuality has to be manufactured for the first few minutes, because in a room where nobody is used to being heard, everyone is defending. Somebody has to go first and receive nothing back but attention.

They will not do it unless you protect them.

  2. What would you do in the ten seconds after they said something honest?

  3. Where have you let a warm response close down a disclosure?

  4. What question would put that family into conversation with each other rather than with you?

Day 15

If Your Posture Isn't Right, People Will Sense That

You can ask the right question

"The one whose walk is blameless, who does what is righteous, who speaks the truth from their heart." Psalm 15:2

You can ask the right question, but if your posture is not right, people will sense that. They will feel whether you are really interested in understanding them or just trying to move the conversation forward.

This is the reading in this week that I cannot give you a technique for, and I want to be honest about that rather than pretend otherwise.

Everything else in this series is learnable. The questions are learnable. The structure is learnable. The discipline of not filling a silence is learnable, and I have watched people who described themselves as pure technicians become genuinely good at all of it.

This one is different, because posture is not a behavior. It is what is actually true about you, leaking.

And it does leak. Families read it accurately and fast, and they read it through channels you are not managing. How you are sitting after forty minutes. Whether your follow-up question builds on what they said or returns to what you had planned. Whether you look at the person speaking or at the person you think is deciding. Whether there is a small flicker of relief when the meeting turns back toward material you are comfortable with.

You cannot perform your way past this. I have seen advisors with immaculate technique whose families never gave them anything, and I have seen a man ask a clumsy question and get an honest answer because everyone in the room could tell he actually wanted to know.

So the question is not how to appear interested. It is whether you are.

Here is the uncomfortable version. Some of us are interested in the family only to the extent that the family is the route to the assets. That is not wickedness. It is a professional deformation, and it comes from a business model that measures one thing. But it will be sensed, and it will cap the relationship at responding.

Psalm 15 is asking who may dwell in God's sanctuary, and it answers with a list of ordinary behaviors. The one whose walk is blameless, who does what is righteous, who speaks truth from their heart.

Truth from the heart is the phrase I want. Not truth that is accurate, which is a lower standard and one most of us meet. Truth that comes from what is actually inside, which means the statement and the interior are the same thing.

There is no version of that you can install for a meeting.

If you find that your interest in these families is thinner than you would like, the work is not on your technique this week. The work is on whether you care, and that is a longer piece of work with a different kind of help required.

I would rather tell you that plainly than sell you a posture.

Reflect

  1. Are you interested in your largest client's family, or in your largest client's family as a route to something?

  2. What does your posture communicate in the last twenty minutes of a long meeting?

  3. When has someone sensed something in you that you had not said?

  4. Where does your follow-up question return to your plan rather than build on their answer?
