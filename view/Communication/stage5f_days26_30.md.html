Day 26

Repeated Conversations Create Culture

One conversation can create clarity

"And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds, not giving up meeting together, as some are in the habit of doing, but encouraging one another." Hebrews 10:24 and 25

You are helping them build trust over time.

Trust does not come from a single conversation. It develops through consistent, ongoing interaction. When families come back to these conversations regularly, they begin to develop a rhythm. And that rhythm creates stability.

Without it, even a good conversation can fade, and people fall back into old patterns.

One conversation can create clarity, but repeated conversations create culture.

I say that sentence often enough that people repeat it back to me, and I want to make sure the distinction underneath it is doing work rather than just sounding neat.

Clarity is a state. It is something a family arrives at, and like any state it decays. A family can leave a meeting genuinely clear about what they want and be measurably less clear eighteen months later, not because anyone changed their mind, but because clarity that is not restated gets overwritten by ordinary life. New spouses arrive. Businesses change. A grandchild is born, a parent gets ill, and the shared understanding that was accurate in March is now describing a family that no longer quite exists.

Culture is different. Culture is not a state, it is a set of defaults, and defaults do not decay the same way because they are carried in behavior rather than in memory. A family with a culture of talking about this does not need to remember what they concluded. They will be talking about it again in the spring, and whatever has changed will surface then.

That is why repetition is not a lesser version of intensity. It is a different mechanism producing a different kind of thing.

Now, the part that concerns your business directly. If clarity decays and culture does not, then the deliverable you should be aiming at is culture, and culture is the one thing a family cannot install for itself in an afternoon. It is also the one thing that does not walk out the door when you do. A family with a rhythm will still be meeting after you retire.

I would rather leave that behind than the best plan I ever built.

Hebrews 10 puts it as an instruction with an observation attached. Let us consider how we may spur one another on, not giving up meeting together, as some are in the habit of doing.

That last clause is the honest part. The writer knows people drop out. He does not treat it as a scandal or a moral collapse. He names it as a habit, which is what it is, and habits do not form by decision. Nobody in that community stood up and announced they were finished. They simply stopped coming, gradually, for good reasons.

Every family that lost its rhythm did the same thing.

Not one of them decided to.

Reflect

  1. Which of your client relationships has a rhythm, and which is a series of unconnected meetings?

  2. Where has clarity you helped create decayed without anyone noticing?

  3. What would survive in your largest client family if you retired next year?

  4. What has quietly stopped happening in your practice that you never decided to stop?

Day 27

The Difference Was What Happened After the First Conversation

Things drifted back to where they had been before

"Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up." Galatians 6:9

I worked with two families who had very similar starting points. Both had resources, both cared about their family, and both had a desire to do this well.

The difference was what happened after the first conversation.

One family had a meaningful initial meeting, but then did not come back to it. Life got busy, and they assumed they would revisit it later. Over time, things drifted back to where they had been before.

The other family also had a strong first conversation, but they decided to meet regularly. Nothing overly complicated, just consistent. Over time, those conversations built understanding, trust, and alignment.

Years later, the difference between those two families was significant.

I have told that story for a long time and I keep telling it because of one detail that people tend to skip. Both first meetings were good.

That is the whole point of the comparison. If the first family's meeting had gone badly, there would be nothing to learn here. It went well. They left it encouraged, with a real sense that something had shifted, and every one of them meant to continue.

So whatever separated those two families happened after everybody had gone home, in a period where nothing dramatic occurred and nobody made a decision.

I want to name what the drift actually consists of, because it is not passive. Drift sounds like nothing happening, and in fact something specific is happening. The family's old defaults are still fully intact. One conversation did not remove them. They were simply set aside for an afternoon, and they resume operation the moment the afternoon ends. If your family's default is that nobody raises the succession question, that default is still running the day after the best meeting of your life.

So the good meeting did not change the system. It demonstrated that the system could be interrupted. Interruption is not replacement.

That is why the second family's decision matters so much and looks so small. Nothing overly complicated, just consistent. They did not do anything more impressive than the first family. They just did it again, and each repetition weakened the old default a little further, until at some point the new behavior became the default and there was nothing left to drift back to.

Galatians 6:9 warns against becoming weary in doing good and promises a harvest at the proper time, if we do not give up. Two things in it are worth noticing. Weariness is the named danger, not opposition. And the timing of the harvest is not ours to set.

That is a hard combination. You are asked to keep doing something without evidence, for a period you do not control.

Which is exactly what you are asking these families to do, and it is worth remembering that when one of them tells you they are not sure it is working yet.

  3. Where are you weary in this work, and what have you quietly stopped attempting?

  4. What would it take to restart with the family you gave up on?

Day 28

Consistency, Not Intensity

The long-term outcome is shaped by consistency

"Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much." Luke 16:10

The long-term outcome is shaped by consistency, not intensity.

It does not have to be complicated. It might be one or two intentional conversations a year, or regular touchpoints that keep people connected. The key is that it is consistent and purposeful. There is a reason for the conversation, and it builds on what has already been discussed.

Each conversation builds on the last one. That is what creates momentum.

I want to defend the low end of that range, because advisors hear one or two conversations a year and assume I am understating it to make it sound achievable.

I am not. One substantial conversation a year, held reliably, will do more for a family than a three day offsite that happens once. And the reason is in the last sentence. Each conversation builds on the last one.

Building requires two things that intensity cannot supply. It requires that something be carried forward, which means the family has to remember or record where they got to. And it requires elapsed time, because part of what makes the second conversation different from the first is that everyone has lived nine months with what was said, and lived-with material is different from heard material.

You cannot compress that. A family who spends three days together has spent three days together. They have not spent nine months thinking.

Now, the practical implication for you. If each conversation builds on the last, then the connective tissue between conversations is a real deliverable and not an administrative afterthought. Somebody has to hold what was said. In practice this means notes that go back to the family, and it means opening the next conversation by referring to the previous one rather than starting fresh.

That opening move is small and it does a great deal. A family that hears their own words from ten months ago read back to them understands immediately that this is a continuing thing rather than an event, and that understanding does more for their commitment than any amount of encouragement from you.

The other thing consistency does is lower the stakes of any individual conversation. When a family believes this is the meeting where everything gets settled, the pressure distorts everything. When they know there is another one in the spring, a difficult subject can be raised without having to be resolved, and raising without resolving is most of the useful work.

Luke 16:10 says whoever is faithful in very little is faithful also in much. It is usually preached about money, and it is really about the relationship between small and large as such.

The verse assumes something most of us resist, which is that the large thing is not a different category from the small thing. It is the same thing, accumulated.

One conversation a year for fifteen years is not a modest program.

It is fifteen conversations, and I have never met a family that had fifteen and came apart.

Day 29

Assuming Things Are Fine Because There's No Visible Conflict

One is busyness

"Whose tongue utters no slander, who does no wrong to a neighbor, and casts no slur on others." Psalm 15:3

There are a few things that erode this over time.

One is busyness. Life moves quickly, and it is easy to deprioritize these conversations. Another is assuming things are fine because there is no visible conflict. And sometimes people do not know how to continue the conversation, so they stop.

Three eroders, and they are not equally dangerous. I want to rank them, because advisors tend to worry about the wrong one.

Busyness is the one everybody names and the least serious, because it is visible. A family that misses a year because of a business sale knows they missed a year. The rhythm is interrupted rather than lost, and a phone call restarts it.

Not knowing how to continue is more serious and more common than people admit. A family has a good first conversation, and then nobody knows what the second one is supposed to be about. The first one had an obvious subject. The second does not, and rather than convene a meeting with no clear content, everyone waits for a reason. This is solvable and it is largely your job to solve it, because you are the one who knows what the next conversation is for. If you leave a family holding an empty calendar slot, they will fill it with something else.

The dangerous one is the middle one. Assuming things are fine because there is no visible conflict.

That one is dangerous because it is not experienced as neglect. It is experienced as good news. A family looks around, sees everyone getting on, and concludes that the work is done. And they are not being lazy. By every visible measure they are right.

Here is the problem. Absence of conflict is not evidence of alignment. It is equally consistent with alignment, with avoidance, and with a family where nothing has yet been proposed that anyone objects to. Those three look identical from the outside and behave completely differently the day a decision arrives.

I have watched families I would have described as harmonious come apart in eight months, and in every case the harmony was real. It just had not been tested, and nobody had thought to test it while it was cheap to do so.

So the question I would have you ask about your quietest client families is not whether they are getting on. It is when was the last time anything was actually put to them.

Psalm 15:3 describes a person whose tongue utters no slander and who does no wrong to a neighbor. It is a psalm about who may dwell with God, and this line is entirely about restraint. What you do not say.

I take it seriously, and I want to name the shadow side, because it is the exact failure this reading is about. Restraint is a virtue and silence is not the same as restraint. A family can go a decade without a harsh word and without a true one, and from the outside those look the same.

Only one of them is peace.

Day 30

Set a Time for the Next Conversation

Just continue the process

"A new command I give you: Love one another. As I have loved you, so you must love one another." John 13:34

If someone has had some good conversations but has not kept it going, the simplest next step is this. Set a time for the next conversation.

Then think about what you want to continue building on. What needs to be revisited? What needs more clarity?

You do not have to create something new every time. Just continue the process.

Thirty days is a strange thing to end with a scheduling instruction, and I have thought about whether to end somewhere loftier. I am not going to, because it is the true answer and because every advisor who finishes a series like this is at risk of the same thing, which is finishing it.

You have read thirty entries. Somewhere in them you thought of a particular family more than once. You probably wrote their name down. And the honest risk is that the reading itself now feels like the work, in the way that a good first family meeting feels like the work, and you will close this and be busy on Monday.

So here is what I want you to actually do.

Take the family you kept thinking about. Not the largest one, and not the easiest. The one that surfaced repeatedly. Put a conversation in the calendar with them before the end of this week, and make the calendar entry before you work out what you will say. If you wait until you know what to say, you will not schedule it, because there is always more preparation available.

Then look at what you wrote in The Bucket sections. That material was private and it was the part most likely to have been skipped. If you skipped it, go back and do one of them, and do the one you skipped hardest, because that is the one that had something in it.

You do not have to create something new every time.

That applies to your own life as much as to a client's family. Whatever came up in this month about your own household is not asking you to design a program. It is asking you to set a time.

John 13:34 is a strange verse to call new, and Jesus calls it new anyway, because the standard has changed. Not love one another well, or love one another as you love yourself. As I have loved you.

What that looked like was not a moment of intensity. It was three years of showing up with the same people, most of whom did not fully understand what was happening, and continuing anyway.

That is the pattern for this work, and it is the reason the last instruction is not a grand one.

Set a time for the next conversation.
