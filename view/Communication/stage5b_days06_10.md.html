Stage 5 part B: Days 6 through 10, Week Two

Day 6

Not Technical, They're Relational and Emotional

There are a few consistent barriers

"Therefore each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to your neighbor, for we are all members of one body." Ephesians 4:25

There are a few consistent barriers, and most of them are not technical. They are relational and emotional.

I want you to notice how much that sentence rules out. Not one of the reasons a family fails to have this conversation is a reason you were trained to address. There is no barrier here that a better structure removes. There is no barrier that a clearer illustration removes. You can build the finest plan of your career and every one of these will still be sitting in the room.

The first is discomfort. These conversations touch money, expectations, roles, and sometimes deeper things like trust or past experience. People do not always know how to bring those up in a healthy way, so they avoid them.

Read that carefully, because the mechanism is not what most people assume. It is not that the family does not want to talk. It is that nobody has a usable opening line. A sixty-five year old man who wants to tell his children how he thinks about the business does not lack the desire. He lacks a way to start that does not sound like an announcement, a summons, or a diagnosis, and so he waits for a natural opportunity that is never going to occur naturally.

I have come to think of this as the most solvable barrier in the set, and the most consistently unaddressed. You can hand a family an opening. Almost nobody does.

The second is fear. Fear of conflict, fear of being misunderstood, or fear of saying something that might create tension. So people stay quiet to keep things comfortable.

The third is assumption, which we will take up later this week, and the fourth is timing, which is the one that costs the most.

Now here is what I actually want you to do with this. Stop treating a family's avoidance as a temperament and start treating it as a diagnosis. These four are not moods. They are distinct conditions with distinct responses, and the response to fear is not the response to assumption. A family held back by discomfort needs a script. A family held back by fear needs safety built first. A family held back by assumption needs evidence. A family held back by timing needs a date on a calendar.

If you cannot tell which one you are looking at, you will apply the wrong intervention with real sincerity and it will not work, and you will conclude the family is not ready.

Ephesians 4:25 is worth reading slowly here. Put off falsehood, speak truthfully to your neighbor, for we are all members of one body. The reason Paul gives is not that lying is wrong, though it is. The reason is membership. In a body, information withheld in one part is a problem for the whole thing, and silence is not neutral because the parts are joined.

Most family silence is not falsehood in the way we normally use the word. Nobody is lying. But an assumption everyone is operating on and nobody has checked functions exactly like a falsehood inside a body, because it produces the same result. People act on something that is not true.

I have never met a family that fell apart because someone told a lie about the estate.

I have met plenty that fell apart on something everybody assumed.

  3. Which barrier are you personally best at spotting, and which one gets past you?

  4. What would you hand a client who wanted to start this conversation and did not know how to open it?

Day 7

They Assume There Will Be a Better Time Later

But later doesn't always come

"Do not withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in your power to act. Do not say to your neighbor, 'Come back tomorrow and I'll give it to you,' when you already have it with you." Proverbs 3:27 and 28

When people do not know how to start, they tend to wait. They assume there will be a better time later.

But later does not always come.

I have said that sentence in a lot of rooms and it lands differently depending on who is in front of me. Advisors hear it as a scheduling problem. Families hear it as something else, because most of them know exactly which conversation I am describing.

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth understanding rather than just agreeing with. Deferral is the cheapest decision available to a family, because it never requires anybody to say no. Nobody has to refuse. Nobody has to disagree. Everyone gets to remain in favor of the conversation while continuing not to have it, and because everyone is in favor of it, nobody experiences the deferral as a choice.

That is what makes timing the most expensive of the four barriers. The other three at least feel like something. Discomfort feels like discomfort. Fear feels like fear. Deferral feels like agreement.

Watch for the specific language, because it is remarkably consistent. After the holidays. Once this year settles down. When the kids are a bit older. After we get through the sale. These are not excuses and the people saying them are not being evasive. Every one of those statements is true. There is always something, which is precisely the problem, because the supply of legitimate reasons never runs out.

And notice your own part in it. If a client has told you three years running that it is a good idea and you have never put a date on it, you did not observe that pattern. You participated in it. A pleasant answer is easier to receive than a commitment, and it keeps the relationship comfortable, and comfortable is what we default to with people we like.

Proverbs 3 goes after exactly this and it does not use the language of laziness or bad intent. Do not say come back tomorrow when you already have it with you. The rebuke is aimed at a person who has the thing, is willing to give the thing, and defers. That is not a description of a stingy man. It is a description of a busy one.

Which means the proverb is not really about generosity of resources. It is about timing as a moral category, which is a strange idea to most of us. We tend to think of good intentions as good regardless of when they are executed. Solomon does not. Withheld good is withheld, and the fact that you meant to get to it does not convert it into something else.

The families I have watched come apart were rarely the ones who refused this conversation.

They were the ones who agreed with me about it for eleven years.

Day 8

People Stay Quiet to Keep Things Comfortable

Avoiding the conversation may feel easier in the moment

"These are the things you are to do: Speak the truth to each other, and render true and sound judgment in your courts." Zechariah 8:16

Another barrier is fear. Fear of conflict, fear of being misunderstood, or fear of saying something that might create tension. So people stay quiet to keep things comfortable.

Avoiding the conversation may feel easier in the moment. It usually creates more difficulty later.

I want to be careful here, because the way this gets taught is often wrong. It is easy to describe a quiet family as cowardly, and it is not true. Silence in a family is almost always a form of care. A mother does not raise the subject of the business because raising it would hurt her son. A son does not ask about the will because asking would look like he was counting. A daughter does not say what she thinks about the arrangement because saying it would put her mother in the middle.

Every one of those silences is somebody protecting somebody. That is why they are so durable. You cannot shame a family out of a silence that is made of love.

But here is what I have watched happen, over and over, and it is why I keep saying this. The comfort is real and the comfort is temporary, and it is being purchased on credit. The interest is paid later by whoever is in the room when the thing finally has to be decided, usually under time pressure, often after a death, and always with less information than they would have had if somebody had spoken twenty years earlier.

I have sat in those rooms. The people in them are not angry about the money as often as you would think. They are angry that they are finding out now.

There is something else worth naming. Fear of conflict and fear of being misunderstood are not the same fear, and they do not respond to the same thing. A person afraid of conflict needs to know the conversation will be governed, that somebody is running it, and that it will not turn into what the last one turned into. A person afraid of being misunderstood needs the opposite. They need time, and room to say it badly first, because the fear is that a half-formed statement will be taken as a final position.

If you offer structure to the person who needs room, you will make it worse.

Zechariah 8:16 is blunt in a way I appreciate. These are the things you are to do. Speak truth to each other. It is stated as an instruction, not as an aspiration, and it is addressed to a community rather than to individuals, which matters. You cannot speak truth to each other alone. It takes at least two people willing to be in the exchange, and one of them usually has to go first.

That is what you are for.

Somebody in that family has been waiting years for a legitimate occasion, and a meeting with a third party in the room is the most legitimate occasion they are ever going to get.

  4. What conversation are you avoiding with a client because it would be uncomfortable for you?

Day 9

Assumptions Are Rarely as Accurate as People Think

Everyone was operating with a different understanding

"These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road." Deuteronomy 6:6 and 7

I worked with a family where this was very clear. The parents had strong values and a clear vision for what they wanted long term, but they had never really communicated that to their children. They assumed the children understood.

The children had their own assumptions. They were not sure what their parents expected, and they did not feel comfortable asking.

So everyone was operating with a different understanding, but no one was talking about it.

It stayed hidden until a decision had to be made. At that point the lack of communication became very visible, and it created tension that could have been avoided if the conversation had happened earlier.

That family is not unusual. I would say it is the most common situation I encounter, and the parents in it are almost always people who did the harder part well. They held real values. They lived them in front of their children for thirty years. Their assumption was not lazy, it was evidential. They had watched their children grow up inside those values and concluded, reasonably, that the values had been received.

Here is the distinction that family never made, and almost nobody makes. Being shown something and being told something are different transfers, and they carry different content.

What gets transferred by being shown is character. If you were generous in front of your children for thirty years, they know you are generous, and there is a decent chance they will be too. That part works, and it is the part most parents are counting on.

What does not transfer by being shown is intention. Your children cannot watch you and learn what you want to happen to the business. They cannot watch you and learn why the arrangement is what it is, or what you hope it does in their lives, or what you were afraid of when you set it up that way. That information exists in one place, which is your head, and it comes out through speech or it does not come out.

Assumptions are rarely as accurate as people think. Notice that this cuts both ways in that family. The parents assumed the children understood. The children assumed they knew what was expected. Both assumptions were sincere, both were wrong, and neither was ever tested, because testing an assumption requires asking a question that implies you might be wrong about your own family.

Deuteronomy 6 is where I would send a client who believes their children absorbed it. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road. That is not a description of modeling. It is a description of speech, repeated, in ordinary circumstances, over time.

The passage assumes the demonstrated life. Then it says talk anyway.

  3. What is the difference, in one of your households, between what has been shown and what has been said?

Day 10

Conversations That Could Have Been Simple Earlier

The stakes tend to get higher

"I hated all the things I had toiled for under the sun, because I must leave them to the one who comes after me. And who knows whether that person will be wise or foolish?" Ecclesiastes 2:18 and 19

There is also the barrier of timing. People think, we will talk about that later, especially when it comes to things like legacy or transition. But waiting often makes it harder, not easier.

The stakes tend to get higher. Conversations that could have been simple earlier become more complex later.

I want to show you the arithmetic on this, because advisors nod at it and then behave as though the cost of delay were flat. It is not flat. It compounds, and it compounds for reasons you can name.

Take a straightforward question. A father wants his daughter to eventually run the business. At fifty-five, telling her that is a conversation about the future. Nothing has been decided, nothing is irreversible, and if she says she does not want it, there are fifteen years to work out something else. The conversation costs almost nothing because nothing is riding on it yet.

Now run the same conversation at seventy-four. There are three grandchildren in the business. Two of her brothers have made career decisions on the assumption that she is not coming. The estate documents have been drafted twice around an arrangement she has never confirmed. She has a life eight hundred miles away.

Identical question. Completely different conversation, because time has attached consequences to it that were not there before. Every year the family waits, the number of decisions that would have to be unwound goes up, and the range of available answers goes down.

That is the first reason it compounds. Here is the second, and it is worse. As the stakes rise, the incentive to keep avoiding it rises too. A conversation that is easy to have is easy to have. A conversation that has become expensive is one everybody has a reason to defer again. The barrier feeds itself.

And the third reason is that time runs out unevenly. Health does not give notice. I have been called in on situations where the conversation everyone intended to have was now being reconstructed from documents by people who were also grieving.

Solomon is unsentimental about this in a way I find useful. He looks at everything he has built and says he hates it, because he has to leave it to whoever comes next, and he does not know whether that person will be wise or foolish. That is not despair about money. It is a specific complaint, which is that he does not know the answer to a question he could have found out.

He does not say the successor will be foolish. He says who knows.

That is the state most of your clients are in, and it is curable.

  4. What is your honest read on how much time the oldest generation in your largest household actually has?
