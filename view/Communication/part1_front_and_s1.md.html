A Welcome From Tom

When I started in this field I thought the job was clear. Manage the money well, protect it, and pass it on. If the portfolio grew and the documents lined up, I had done my work.

Then I began noticing cracks. Families with perfect plans on paper were coming apart anyway. Children stopped speaking to each other. Businesses that took forty years to build were sold within months. None of it was a planning failure. Every one of those plans would have survived an audit.

What I came to understand is that our work runs on two tracks. There is the technical side, which is the financial, legal, and tax machinery of moving assets between generations, and there is the relational side, which is the family, the values, the faith, and the communication between the people involved. We are trained almost entirely on the first one. We are judged, eventually, on the second.

I should tell you where I come from, because you may be sitting where I sat. I am an accountant. Years ago a firm sent me to a specialist to work out what I was actually good at, and what he came back with was that I was an accountant with a personality. He looked confused when he said it, and I have worked with enough accountants since to understand why. If you are reading this thinking that the relational side is simply not how you are built, I want to be direct with you. These are skills. They can be learned, they improve with practice, and I have watched people who described themselves as pure technicians become the most trusted person in a family's life.

Two things drive everything I do with a client. I want family harmony, and I want a wise transfer of both wealth and wisdom. Not one or the other. Communication is what makes both of them possible, which is why it sits alongside clarity and alignment as one of the three words this whole approach turns on.

I will say one more thing before you begin. There is going to be a family meeting. The only question is whether your client is in the room for it, because when someone dies their family gathers and talks about their affairs whether or not anybody planned it. Everything in these six sessions is about moving that meeting earlier, while the person who built it all is still at the table and able to say what they intended.

Thank you for doing this work. It matters more than the industry has yet worked out.

Tom Conway

Using This Guide

This works alone, in pairs, or as a team

That is not a concession. It is how the series was built.

You may be working through this on your own, with a partner, or with your whole team. All three work, and no session assumes other people are in the room. Wherever a prompt would benefit from discussion, you will see this tag:

Team or pair: discuss. Solo: write it down.

Take the half that applies to you. If you are alone, writing the answer is the exercise, and it is not a lesser version of it. Several of the exercises in this guide are private by design and are done alone regardless of how you are working.

If you are doing this with a group, there is a section at the back called If You're Doing This With a Group containing a team agreement, a roster, and a calendar. Nothing in the six sessions depends on it.

Your privacy, and your clients'

You are never required to disclose sensitive personal or financial specifics. Not your clients', and not your own.

Every Get Started section opens with a reminder of this, and it is meant literally. Where an exercise asks about a client household, describe the situation and the pattern rather than the file. Where an exercise turns personal, and several of them do, you decide what to write and whether anyone else ever sees it. The private exercises in each session are built on the assumption that nobody will read them.

What each session asks of you

Roughly ninety minutes of engagement, plus five short daily readings.

Watch the teaching video first. The Learn section is built around it, and it will not work in reverse. This series does not use fill-in-the-blanks, because the videos are conversations rather than lectures and you cannot fill a blank from a conversation. Instead, each Learn prompt states what Tom and Brett covered and then asks you to apply it to your own book of business.

The Scenarios in each session end with a page reference to the responses in the back. Do not look ahead. The responses are there to sharpen a decision you have already made, and reading them first turns an exercise into a lecture.

A note on what this series is not

This is not a communication skills course. You have almost certainly had one of those. The value here is Tom's specific teaching about families in transition, drawn from decades of sitting in rooms where a great deal of money and a great many relationships were on the table at the same time.

You will be treated throughout as a practising professional. Nothing that you already know will be explained to you.

SESSION 1

The Advisor as Conversation Guide

Shifting from Expert to Facilitator

Introduction

KEY VERSE

"Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry."

JAMES 1:19

Most of what you were trained to do rewards the answer. You are paid to analyze, to model, to structure, and to recommend, and clients come to you because you know things they do not. None of that goes away in legacy work. What changes is where the work actually happens.

There is a commercial reality underneath this and it is worth naming plainly, once, before we move on. A generational wealth transfer is moving through your book right now. The client relationships that survive it are not the ones with the most elegantly constructed plan. They are the ones where somebody asked the questions nobody else was asking, and where the next generation was in the room when it happened. If that person is not you, it will be someone else.

Tom puts the shift in one line. Most advisors are trained to analyze and recommend, but when you are working with families around legacy, the real work happens through conversation. That is not a softer version of your job. It is a different discipline, and it is the one that decides whether the plan you build is ever carried by the people it was built for. You are still the expert in the room. You are simply no longer the one who has to be talking.

Opening Story

The Meeting That Started With Questions

The family had been through this before. Parents in their seventies, adult children in their forties, and a file that had been reviewed by more than one firm. Every prior meeting had followed the same shape. Someone presented. Numbers went up on a screen. Documents were walked through. Questions were invited at the end, and there were never many.

Underneath it, two versions of the same family were running side by side. The parents felt their children did not fully grasp what had been built. The children felt they were not being included, and not quite trusted. Nobody had said either thing out loud.

When Tom sat down with them, he did not open with information. He asked each person how they saw the situation, what concerned them, and what mattered most to them. Then he stopped talking.

It was uncomfortable. There were pauses that ran long enough to feel like a mistake. People were not sure what they were allowed to say. Then someone answered honestly, and someone else answered honestly after that, and the room changed. They began to hear each other in a way they had not before.

The plan mattered. It was just not the starting point. The conversation is what made the plan possible.

Nothing in this session asks you to disclose a client's name, numbers, or circumstances. Describe the situation, not the file.

  1. Think about the last client meeting you led. Honestly, what share of the talking was yours?

  2. When did a client last tell you something in a meeting that you could not have learned from their file?

Building a Healthy Culture

The posture shift does not begin at the client meeting. It begins in your own shop.

Tom teaches Patrick Lencioni's five practices in the context of family systems, and they apply to your practice before they apply to anyone's household: build trust, master conflict, achieve commitment, embrace accountability, and focus on results. Read that list against how your own team operates. Most advisory practices are strong on results and thin on conflict, which produces a room where disagreement gets handled in the hallway afterward.

Here is the practical test. If the associate who sits in your client meetings has never been asked what they observed, you are running a practice that models exactly the thing you are about to ask families to stop doing. The second chair usually sees the person who is not talking. Ask them.

If you are working through this guide with a partner or a team, the way you handle the next six sessions is itself a demonstration. Later sessions will assume you have already had one honest conversation inside your own four walls.

Reflect

  1. Tom says your value in this work often comes from what you ask and how you listen rather than from what you say. Where does that sit uncomfortably against the reputation you have built?

  2. Which client family have you been giving information to for years without ever addressing what is going on underneath it?

  3. Tom uses three openers: how are you thinking about this, what is most important to you here, and what concerns you about the future. Which one would land hardest in your next meeting, and why that one?

The Framework

The Two Sides of Legacy Planning

Every legacy engagement runs on two tracks at once. Tom separates them deliberately, because advisors are trained almost entirely on one of them and evaluated almost entirely on the other.

  • The Technical Side. The financial, legal, and tax aspects of wealth transfer. Structures, documents, valuations, funding, tax exposure, and the mechanics of moving assets from one generation to the next. This is where your credential lives. It is also the part of the work that a competent competitor can replicate.

  • The Relational Side. The family, the values, the faith, and the communication between generations. What the family actually wants, what they have never said to each other, who has been left out of the conversation, and whether the people receiving the plan understand and support it. This is where the work of the last four sessions of the video series lives, and it is the part almost nobody is doing.

Tom's larger distinction runs alongside this. Traditional planning defines outcomes materially and is transactional in nature. Legacy planning defines outcomes relationally and is transformational in nature. The first can be measured on a statement. The second shows up at the family meeting fifteen years from now.

Source: Workbook Legend, Session 2; Legacy Planning Distinctives, Session 1.

Of the last ten hours you spent on your largest client relationship, how many were technical and how many were relational?

The Tool

The Three-Year Question and the Lifetime Satisfaction Question

This is how Tom opens a goals conversation. It is not an icebreaker. It is the instrument that produces everything the rest of the engagement is built on.

He asks the three-year question first. "If we were to meet three years from today, looking back over your life from now until then in any area, what must have happened by then for you to feel good about your progress?"

Then he listens and writes down whatever they say. He takes extensive notes. He does not interpret, redirect, or improve on the answer while it is being given.

Then he asks the spouse the same question. Separately, fully, and with the same attention. This step is the one advisors skip, and it is where most of the useful material comes from.

Then he asks the lifetime satisfaction question. "If you're standing before the Lord at the end of your life, looking back over your life from then until today, what must have happened for you to feel good about your life and to feel confident that you would hear, 'Well done, my good and faithful servant' from our Heavenly Father?"

He writes those comments down as well.

He also asks two questions he puts to everyone, and he will use them even in a first meeting: "What are your three greatest concerns about your life today?" and "What would bring you the greatest relief if it were solved today?"

A note on how this actually goes. When most people are asked their three most important financial goals, the answer arrives without hesitation, because it has been sitting in their head for years without ever being written down. The purpose of the whole sequence is not to reach agreement on a goal. It is to learn what these people actually want.

Source: Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 7.

Which of your top five client relationships have you never asked the three-year question, and what has stood in the way?

Exercise

This one is private. Do it alone, and do not plan to read it out.

Part One. Choose one client household. Write down what you genuinely know about what matters to them that does not appear anywhere in the file. Then, in a separate list, write down what you have assumed and cannot verify.

[eight writing lines]

Part Two. For the same household, write the one question you have never asked them because it felt outside your role. Write what you believe the answer would be. Then write what it would cost you if you are wrong about that.

[eight writing lines]

Part Three. Turn it around. Answer the three-year question for your own life, in your own words, in full. Then answer the lifetime satisfaction question for yourself. When you finish, note how difficult that was and where you hesitated.

[ten writing lines]

This Week's Readings

Five readings, one per day, from Family Legacy Coaching by Design by Tom Conway.

Day One. The Shift Every Advisor Should Make. Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 1. Tom describes noticing cracks in families whose plans were perfect on paper. Read it against your own book rather than in the abstract. Before you close it, name one household of yours where the documents line up and the relationships do not, and decide whether you have ever raised that with them.

Day Two. Understanding the Purpose of Legacy Coaching. Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 2. This is where the three pillars of clarity, alignment, and communication are laid out, and where legacy gets expanded past money into five dimensions. The practical use is triage. Take your five largest relationships and mark which of the three pillars is weakest in each. That mark tells you where your next conversation goes.

Day Three. Listening. Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 3. The distinction Tom draws between hearing and listening is the operating skill for this entire series, and he is direct that it is a skill rather than a temperament. Notice his line about looking for agreement rather than looking to understand. Watch for that pattern in yourself during your next meeting and mark the moment it happens.

Day Four. The Right Heart. Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 4. This chapter is uncomfortable in the useful way, because it is about motive rather than method. Read it slowly. The question it puts to you is whether you care about these families in a way that would survive the account leaving, and your honest answer shapes how the next five sessions land.

Day Five. Opening with Vision Questions. Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 7. Here is the goals conversation in full, including the questions you worked with in The Tool and the sequence Tom follows through the five areas of legacy. Read it the day before your next client meeting, and take the sequence in with you rather than trying to recall it from memory.

Final Thoughts

Take whichever of these fits your situation.

1. With your partner or team, name the one client relationship where the two of you have most been operating as experts rather than guides, and agree who is raising it and when.

2. On your own, name the one client relationship where you have most been operating as the expert rather than the guide, and write the first sentence you will use to change that.

The shift this session asks for is small in the room and large over time. You are not being asked to know less, to prepare less, or to hold back a recommendation the family needs. You are being asked to change where you start. Most advisors open with what they have prepared, because that is what they were trained to trust. The families who make it through a transfer intact are usually the ones where someone opened with a question instead, waited through the silence that followed, and let the answer change the plan.
