SESSION 2

Understanding Communication Barriers

Why Families Avoid What Matters Most

Introduction

KEY VERSE

"Therefore each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to your neighbor."

EPHESIANS 4:25

Session 1 was about the posture you bring into the room. This one is about what you will find when you get there.

You can make the shift completely. You can ask better questions, leave the silence open, and hand the family every opportunity to say the thing that matters. A great many of them still will not say it. Tom is clear that the reasons are almost never technical. They are relational and emotional, and they sit under the surface of families who are otherwise functioning well: discomfort with a subject nobody knows how to raise, fear of the conflict that raising it might start, assumptions each person is certain everyone else shares, and a quiet agreement that there will be a better time for this later.

Your job in Session 2 is diagnostic rather than corrective. Before you can help a family start the conversation, you have to know which of the four is actually in the room, because the response to fear is not the response to assumption. And you need to take Tom's warning about the fourth one seriously, because it is the barrier that costs families the most. Later does not always come.

Opening Story

The Gap Nobody Named

They were, by any measure, a family that had done things right. The parents held strong values and a clear picture of what they wanted to be true of this family fifty years out. They had thought about it carefully. They had prayed about it.

They had never said any of it to their children.

They assumed it was understood. The children had grown up inside those values, so surely they had absorbed the vision along with them. Meanwhile the children were working from their own set of assumptions, uncertain what their parents actually expected of them and not comfortable enough to ask. Everyone in that family was operating from a different understanding of the same future, and no one knew it.

The gap held because nothing forced it open. Then a decision arrived that could not be deferred, and what had been invisible became very visible very quickly. The tension that followed was not about the decision. It was about years of unspoken expectation surfacing all at once, under time pressure, in front of everyone.

Tom's assessment is worth sitting with. The issue was not disagreement. It was a lack of clarity, and every bit of it could have been prevented by a conversation held years earlier when nothing was at stake.

The Framework

Common Points of Failure and Common Points of Success

Tom teaches these as two parallel lists rather than as a single spectrum, because families rarely sit neatly on one. Most carry items from both columns at the same time, and the diagnostic value is in seeing which specific items are present rather than in reaching an overall verdict.

Common Points of Failure

  • No foundation of faith
  • Lack of parental or grandparental involvement
  • Lack of clarity about the family legacy
  • Lack of communication among generations
  • Lack of intentionality, usually described as being too busy
  • Lack of preparation of the next generation
  • Lack of generosity or spirit of stewardship

Common Points of Success

  • A foundation of faith
  • Shared values
  • Intentional modeling by parents and grandparents
  • Regular open and honest communication
  • Intentional preparation of the next generation
  • Commitment to stewardship and generosity

Read the two lists against each other and the pattern is hard to miss. Four of the seven failure points are communication failures wearing different clothes, and three of the six success points are communication practices. Nothing on either list is a planning error, a tax error, or an investment error.

Source: Workbook Legend, Session 1.

The Tool

The Family Legacy Review Questionnaire

This is the instrument Tom uses to bring a hidden gap into the open without asking anyone to be the first person brave enough to speak. It works because it changes the sequence. Every family member reflects privately first, and only then do they compare.

How it is set up. Each person is told plainly that there are no right or wrong answers and that the goal is not agreement but understanding. That framing is not decoration. It is what makes an honest answer possible from someone who has spent years managing the impression they give.

How it runs. Each family member completes the reflection individually, before any conversation. Some families then discuss every question. Others take only the areas that feel most important. Either is correct.

The scale. Each statement is rated from 1, strongly disagree, through 5, strongly agree. The reflection moves through faith and values, and on through the relational, spiritual, and practical angles of the family's legacy, without becoming technical.

Sample statements from the faith and values section:

  • Our faith plays a meaningful role in how our family lives and makes decisions.
  • We talk openly about the values that matter most to us.
  • Our actions generally reflect what we say we believe.
  • We intentionally pass down faith, not just traditions.
  • I feel encouraged to grow spiritually within our family.

The tone rule. The questions are written to create safety rather than pressure. If a question feels uncomfortable, a family member may skip it or return to it later. Families move at the pace that fits them.

What you are actually looking for. Not the scores. The spread. When one family member rates a statement a 5 and another rates the same statement a 2, you have located the gap, and you have located it on paper rather than in an argument.

Source: Family Legacy by Design, Appendix A.

A note on naming: this instrument is distinct from the F.A.M.I.L.Y. Review, which is the technical document inventory covering financial statements, assets under management, mandatory documents, insurance coverage, liabilities and taxes, and yearly cash flow. Both are Tom's. They are not the same tool and should never be introduced to a client under the same name.

Which client household would you hand this to first, and what is the specific spread you expect to see?

2. On your own, name the conversation you have been deferring, and write the date.

The four barriers are ordinary. There is nothing pathological about a family that finds a subject uncomfortable, or fears a conflict, or assumes it is understood, or believes there will be a better moment for this. Those are reasonable human responses, and they are precisely why the gap survives in families who love each other and are otherwise doing well. What makes the difference is whether somebody outside the family is willing to say that the gap exists. That person is not going to be one of the children, and it is very rarely one of the parents.

SESSION 3

Starting the Right Conversations

Creating Safety and Trust

Introduction

KEY VERSE

"To answer before listening, that is folly and shame."

PROVERBS 18:13

You know what the barriers are. Now you have to open the door without slamming it.

Tom draws the distinction sharply. Your first goal in the opening conversation is not to arrive at the right answer. It is to build the right environment, because people who do not feel safe will respond without ever opening up, and you will leave the meeting with a full page of notes and nothing you can use. Safety gets built before the first substantive word, in how the conversation is framed. Framed as pressure, evaluation, or expectation, it produces resistance. Framed as an invitation to be heard, it produces engagement.

That framing is not a technique you can bolt onto an unchanged posture. Tom is direct that you can ask exactly the right question and still get nothing, because people register whether you are interested in understanding them or only in moving the conversation along. The words are the smaller half of it.

Opening Story

One Question, and Then Silence

Consider the meeting from the daughter's side of the table.

She had been to these before. Her parents' previous attempts at this conversation had ended in one of two places, either a silence that everyone agreed to call agreement or a disagreement that ended the evening early. She had learned to arrive with nothing to say, which was safer than arriving with something.

This time the man running the meeting did not open with a structure or a decision or a document. He asked her a question. What would you like your parents to understand about how you're thinking right now?

Then he stopped, and did not rescue the silence that followed.

She hesitated. Nobody had asked her that, and she had never assembled the answer. When she finally started talking it came out unevenly. But it came out, and her brother went next, and then her mother, and for the first time in that family's history people said what they thought without someone immediately reacting to it.

Tom's read on why it worked is unsentimental. The way you start the conversation tends to determine how it unfolds. Start with pressure and you get resistance. Start with openness and you get engagement.

The Framework

The Three Keys to Communication

Tom teaches these three together and in this order, and he does not treat them as a menu. All three have to be present, because any one of them held on its own turns into something the family will not trust.

  • Be Direct. Say the thing. Ambiguity in a family conversation is almost always kindness pointed in the wrong direction, and the family fills the gap with a worse story than the truth. Matthew 5:37; 2 Corinthians 6:11 to 13; Proverbs 25:11 and 12.

  • Be Truthful. Directness without truthfulness is just force. This is the key that governs what you say about your own limits, your own uncertainty, and the parts of a plan you would not choose. Zechariah 8:16; Psalm 15:2; Philippians 4:8; Ephesians 4:25.

  • Be Respectful. Truth delivered without respect will be heard as an accusation and answered as one. Respect is what allows a direct, truthful statement to land as information rather than as a verdict. 1 Peter 4:8 to 11; Psalm 15:3; 1 Corinthians 13:4 to 7; Romans 12:10; Matthew 7:12; John 13:34.

Tom attaches one warning to the set, every time he teaches it: watch out for triangulation. Session 4 takes that up as its tool, because triangulation is where difficult topics go when the three keys are absent.

Source: Workbook Legend, Sessions 5 and 11; Training Manual, Session 5; Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 13.

The Tool

Sample Family Communication and Invitation Letters

Most legacy efforts stall at the invitation. Not because anyone is unwilling, but because nobody knows how to begin, and the family waits for a form of words that never arrives. Tom's framing is that words set tone, and tone builds trust, which makes the invitation itself the first act of the conversation rather than the administrative step before it.

There are five, each written for a specific moment.

  • Invitation to a Family Legacy Conversation. For inviting family members into a conversation focused on understanding and connection. The load-bearing line is that the gathering is not to make decisions or settle anything, but to listen. It closes on the point that this is about connection rather than agreement.

  • Setting the Tone for a Family Meeting. Sent in advance to reduce fear and set expectations. It states that the goal is not to persuade or pressure, that there are no right answers and no decisions required, and that if something feels uncomfortable the family will slow down.

  • Invitation to the Next Generation. Written to affirm dignity, voice, and belonging. It explicitly releases the next generation from having answers or certainty, and asks instead what support would be helpful.

  • Following Up After a Family Conversation. Sent afterward to reinforce trust and momentum. It thanks people for the spirit in which things were shared, acknowledges openly that not everything was resolved, and signals that the conversation continues at the right pace.

  • Introducing a Conversation About Generosity. For opening giving conversations without pressure, by moving the subject away from amounts and decisions and toward what generosity has meant in the family's story.

Tom's four rules for using them. Adapt the language to the family's own voice. Keep messages brief and sincere. Choose warmth over formality. Avoid explaining or defending. The letters are starting points rather than scripts, and the goal is invitation rather than instruction.

Source: Family Legacy by Design, Appendix G.

SESSION 4

Navigating Difficult Topics

Wealth, Expectations, and Conflict

Introduction

KEY VERSE

"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger."

PROVERBS 15:1

Everything to this point has been preparation for the meeting where it gets hard.

Tom's explanation for why these particular subjects carry so much charge is worth taking literally. When a family talks about wealth, they are not talking about money. They are talking about value, responsibility, fairness, and sometimes identity, and the same asset means security to one person, opportunity to a second, and obligation to a third. Those differences have usually never been compared, so when they surface they surface all at once and feel like conflict even when nobody intended one.

Your instinct in that moment will be to respond quickly, and Tom is blunt that the instinct makes things worse. The work is to slow the room down, make sure people feel heard before anything moves forward, and shift the objective from being right to understanding each other. Agreement often comes later. Understanding has to come first.

Opening Story

Fair Was Not the Word

The question on the table was how the wealth would eventually be distributed. The parents had an answer. They had held it for years, thought it through, and never said it to anyone.

Their children had answers too, and no two of them matched.

The tension arrived almost immediately. One of the children said the arrangement did not feel fair. Another said they had carried more of the load for longer and expected that to count for something. Both statements were true from where each person sat, and both were heard by the other as an accusation. Tom is direct about the stakes in that moment. Handled badly, that conversation ends a family, and the ending takes twenty years and gets described afterward as something else.

What he did was slow it down. He did not try to resolve the question of distribution, and he did not adjudicate fairness. He gave each person the opportunity to explain how they were thinking and why, in full, without being answered.

There was defensiveness at first. Then somewhere in the middle of it, people stopped listening for their turn and started listening to the content, and the temperature dropped. They did not agree. They understood each other, which is a different thing and had to come first.

The Framework

Understanding, Not Agreement

This is the distinction that governs everything difficult, and Tom teaches it as three statements rather than as a principle.

  • Agreement is transactional. It settles a question and closes it. It can be reached quickly, it can be reached under pressure, and it can be reached by people who have not understood each other at all. Agreement obtained that way is what fails at the next family meeting, because nothing underneath it was ever resolved.

  • Understanding is relational, and it leads to planning and problem solving. It is slower and it produces something durable. A family that understands why a sibling holds a position can build a plan that survives contact with that sibling. A family that merely has their signature cannot.

  • The way to understanding is empathetic listening. Not sympathy, and not agreement held quietly. Listening that works to grasp why a person thinks the way they think, rather than listening for the opening to reply.

Tom's own summary of where advisors go wrong is that too often people look for agreement rather than looking to understand why the other person thinks as they do. He names a client who intimidated his children and was not seeking to understand them, but to obtain their agreement. That is the failure mode, and it is available to you as easily as to your client.

The Tool

Triangulation, and How Tom Beats It

Tom attaches this warning to the three keys every time he teaches them, because triangulation is what a difficult conversation turns into when it cannot be had directly.

What it looks like. A mother is having a conversation with her son and it is not going well. Rather than continuing it, the son goes and finds his sister and works to bring her round to his side. Now there are three people in what was a two-person problem, and the original conversation has been permanently replaced.

Why it survives. Tom draws on Edwin Friedman's account of emotional triangles, and the mechanics are worth knowing because they explain why goodwill does not dissolve one. Triangles form out of discomfort between people. Once formed, they work to preserve themselves and resist attempts to change them. They interlock and reinforce each other. They make it harder for the people inside them to change how they think or behave. And they push the system's stress onto whoever is most responsible or most engaged, which in a client family is frequently the person you like most.

Why it matters to you specifically. You are the most attractive third side of a family triangle that has ever walked into that house. You are neutral, you are trusted, you are outside the family system, and you are being paid. Every incentive in the room is pointed at recruiting you.

How Tom beats it. Do not become the third side. When someone has a problem with another person, they go to that person, rather than collecting an ally first. Insist that the two parties talk to each other. If one of them is not talking, address that person directly and keep the whole thing in the open rather than allowing a parallel private conversation to run underneath the meeting.

Source: Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 7; Workbook Legend, Sessions 5 and 11.

Introduction

KEY VERSE

"Everything should be done in a fitting and orderly way."

1 CORINTHIANS 14:40

One conversation at a time is manageable. A room is a different discipline.

Tom describes the objective simply. You are helping the family think together, which is something most families have never done. Their conversations happen in pairs, in cars, at the end of holidays, and around specific issues, and bringing everyone into one room with a deliberate structure creates a dynamic none of them has experienced. That structure is not a constraint on the conversation. Without it, the discussion drifts or gets absorbed by one or two voices, and the people you most needed to hear from leave having said nothing.

What surprises advisors the first time is how much is already there. Tom's experience is that families frequently arrive assuming they are divided and discover, once each person has actually spoken, that they were holding the same handful of things all along and had simply never said them in the same room. Your work is less about building alignment than about surfacing it.

Opening Story

What Do You Hope for This Family

The father had prepared himself for a difficult afternoon.

His family had never had a full conversation about their future. There were assumptions running in several directions, some tension he could feel but could not name, and a general uncertainty that had settled over the last few years without anyone deciding to let it. He expected the meeting to expose all of it.

It did not start with decisions. It started with a question put to everyone in the room. What do you hope for this family moving forward?

The first answers were cautious, which he had expected. Then his daughter said something about wanting the family to still be gathering in twenty years, and his son said something close to the same thing in different words, and his daughter-in-law added a third version. He sat there listening to his children describe, one after another, more or less the thing he had privately hoped for and never said, because he had assumed he was the only one who wanted it.

Tom's reading is that the alignment had been there the whole time and had simply never been surfaced. What changed the meeting was not persuasion. Once the family could see they were starting from shared values rather than from disagreement, the rest of the conversation became a different kind of work.

The Framework

The Three Movements of a Healthy Goals Conversation

Tom structures a family conversation in three movements rather than in agenda items. Families spend more time in one than another, and that is normal rather than a sign the structure has failed.

  • Movement One: Listening for the Heart. Each person responds to one or two opening questions. What brings you the most gratitude when you think about our family. What experiences have shaped you most within this family. What do you value most about being part of it. The facilitator's discipline here is total: do not comment on a response, do not correct one, and thank each person for sharing.

  • Movement Two: Clarifying Hopes and Concerns. The conversation turns to the future. What hopes do you have for our family in the years ahead. What concerns or questions do you carry but rarely voice. What do you hope future generations experience because of this family. This movement surfaces excitement and vulnerability together, and both need room.

  • Movement Three: Naming Direction, Not Decisions. Direction without pressure to decide anything. What values feel most important to protect. Where do we feel aligned today. Where do we sense a need for more conversation. Name themes rather than conclusions.

The five ground rules, agreed together before the conversation starts: we listen to understand rather than to respond; we speak for ourselves rather than for others; we avoid interrupting or correcting; we respect differences in perspective; we allow space for emotion without judgment.

Three challenges, and the response to each. If emotions rise, pause, acknowledge what is being felt, and return to listening. If disagreement emerges, remember that understanding does not require agreement. If silence appears, allow it, because silence usually means people are processing.

The Tool

The Family Values Exercise

This is the best-documented live exercise in the whole body of Tom's material, captured as he actually runs it in a room. It works because it moves a family from private opinion to public agreement in four steps, without anyone having to argue for anything.

Step one. Each person works the list alone. Every family member receives the full values list, which runs to roughly eighty items, and marks each one high, medium, or low.

Step two. Each person circles their top six. They take their high items and circle six. If someone does not have six highs, they take from their mediums to make up the number. The constraint is doing the work.

Step three. Everyone shares, and everything goes on the board. Each family member reads out their top six and all of them are written up. Tom's note from experience is that this typically produces a list of twenty to thirty values, sometimes more. Nothing is filtered at this stage and nothing is argued with.

Step four. Each person picks their top six from the board. The family now selects from the combined list rather than from their own. As people share their selections, a mark goes next to each value chosen, and the tally produces the family's top six.

Then the essential step most people skip. Take each of the six and define it by adding the phrase demonstrated by. Family, for example, becomes family demonstrated by communicating with each other regularly, and family demonstrated by keeping in touch with one another. A value without a demonstration is a word on a wall.

Tom's own framing when he finishes: this is a start.

SESSION 6

Sustaining Ongoing Communication

Building Long-Term Trust

Introduction

KEY VERSE

"Let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds, not giving up meeting together."

HEBREWS 10:24 AND 25

Everything in this series can be done well once and amount to nothing.

Tom's position on this is unromantic. Trust does not come from a conversation. It develops through consistent, ongoing interaction, and when a family returns to these conversations regularly they build a rhythm, and the rhythm produces stability. Without it, even an excellent first conversation fades and the family settles back into the patterns it had before. He puts the whole series into a single sentence: one conversation can create clarity, but repeated conversations create culture.

That has a direct implication for how you work. The value you deliver in this material is not the meeting. It is the rhythm you leave behind, and rhythm is the one thing a family cannot install for itself in an afternoon. The long-term outcome is shaped by consistency rather than by intensity.

Opening Story

Two Families, Same Start

Tom has watched this comparison run for long enough to be certain about it.

Two families, similar in the ways that are usually thought to matter. Both had substantial resources. Both cared genuinely about their children and grandchildren. Both wanted to do this well, and both said so credibly. Each held a meaningful first conversation.

Then the paths separated, over nothing dramatic.

The first family did not come back to it. There was no decision not to. Life was busy, the meeting had gone well, and everyone assumed they would pick it up again at some point. Over the following years things drifted quietly back to where they had been, and the conversation became a thing that had happened once.

The second family decided to meet regularly. Nothing elaborate, and nothing that would have looked impressive from outside. They simply kept doing it. Each conversation built on the one before, and understanding accumulated, and trust accumulated with it, and alignment followed.

Years later, Tom's summary is that the difference between those two families was significant, and that it did not come from anything either of them did on the first day.

The Framework

Keys for Passing On a Legacy

Tom teaches seven, and they belong at the end of this series because every one of them is a practice sustained over time rather than an action completed once.

  • Be intentional. Nothing in this work happens by default. What happens by default is the outcome the family was already heading toward.

  • Be willing to be humble enough to look for help. The families who succeed at this bring people in. The ones who do not are usually being led by someone who believes asking would look like weakness.

  • Model your legacy. The second and third generations are watching, and they are calibrating against behaviour rather than against stated intention.

  • Conduct regular family meetings. The word doing the work in that line is regular.

  • Pass on a legacy of generosity. Generosity is learned by observation and practice, and it cannot be installed by a document.

  • Be consistent as a steward and as a leader. Consistency over time is what converts a stated value into a family characteristic.

  • Be willing to work hard at it. Tom does not soften this one, and neither should you when you set expectations with a family.

The Tool

The Family Meeting Binder and the Family Meeting Commitment

Rhythm needs a container. These are the two Tom points families toward, and between them they solve the two ways a rhythm dies, which are losing the thread and losing the will.

The Family Meeting Binder. A single place the family brings to every gathering, holding:

  • The ground rules
  • The family mission statement
  • Key documents, meaning the estate summary, the business structure, and the giving plan
  • Notes from past meetings
  • Prayer requests

The binder does something no reminder can. It creates continuity between meetings, so each conversation starts from where the last one finished rather than from the beginning. It also brings new family members up to speed without anyone having to reconstruct a decade of history for a son-in-law.

The Family Meeting Commitment. A short written statement the family drafts, signs, and keeps at the front of the binder. It covers:

  • Why family meetings matter to us
  • What we hope to accomplish through gathering regularly
  • How we want to communicate with each other, meaning with grace, honesty, and respect
  • A commitment to show up, including when it is uncomfortable

The clause that carries the weight is the last one. Everything else in the document describes a good intention. That one describes what happens on the year somebody does not want to come.

Tom's practical additions for families already meeting well. Rotate the leadership so adult children take turns facilitating. Add a learning component and bring in an outside voice. Include the grandchildren for part of the meeting. Plan a service project afterward.

And the one instruction that matters most: schedule the next meeting before this one ends, even if it is six months out.
