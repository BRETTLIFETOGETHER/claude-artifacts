Stage 5 part D: Days 16 through 20, Week Four

Day 16

You're Not Just Talking About Money

Value, responsibility, fairness, and sometimes identity

"How much better to get wisdom than gold, to get insight rather than silver!" Proverbs 16:16

When you are talking about wealth, you are not just talking about money. You are talking about value, responsibility, fairness, and sometimes identity.

That is why these particular topics create so much tension. They are personal.

I have watched advisors miss this for entire careers, and the reason is straightforward. We are trained to treat money as a quantity, and as a quantity it behaves predictably. It can be divided, valued, projected, and compared. Everything in our professional apparatus assumes that a dollar is a dollar regardless of whose hand it is in.

Inside a family, it is not. The same asset means four different things to four people at one table, and every one of them believes their meaning is the obvious one.

Take a business worth twelve million. To the father who built it, that is not an asset. It is the proof that a decision he made at twenty-nine was correct, and it is the thing he will be remembered by. To the son who came in at twenty-four, it is a career, a set of relationships, and the reason he lives in a town he might not otherwise have chosen. To the daughter who did not join, it is the thing that had her father's attention. To the daughter-in-law, it is a source of risk that her household cannot diversify away from.

Nobody at that table is wrong. They are not even disagreeing yet, because none of them has said any of it out loud. But when the conversation about the future finally happens, four incompatible meanings are going to arrive in the room at once, wearing the language of numbers.

That is what you are watching when a family fight about money looks disproportionate to the amount involved. It is disproportionate to the amount. It is not disproportionate to what the money represents, and the fight is being conducted in the only vocabulary the family has, which is dollars, about a subject that is not dollars.

The word I would draw your attention to hardest is identity. That is the one that turns a difficult conversation into an unrecoverable one. If a person's sense of who they are in this family is bound up in an asset or a role, then a proposal to change the arrangement is not a financial proposal. It is a proposal about them, and they will defend it accordingly.

Proverbs 16:16 puts wisdom and gold side by side and asks how much better it is to get the first. What I notice is that the proverb does not pretend the two are unrelated. It puts them in the same sentence and ranks them, which is a more useful move than treating them as separate departments.

Your clients live in that same sentence. They are not choosing between wisdom and gold. They have the gold and they are trying to work out what it means, and most of them have never had anyone in the room who was interested in the second question.

You could be the first.

Reflect

  1. Take one household. What does the principal asset represent to each family member?

  2. Where have you watched a family conflict that was disproportionate to the money involved?

  3. Whose identity in your largest client family is bound to an asset or a role?

  4. What vocabulary does that family have for talking about anything other than dollars?

Day 17

Security for One Person, Opportunity for Another

Expectations can be very different, even within the same family

"Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. It does not dishonor others, it is not self-seeking." 1 Corinthians 13:4 and 5

Wealth can represent security for one person, opportunity for another, and responsibility for someone else. Expectations can be very different, even within the same family.

And when those differences come out, it can feel like conflict, even if that was not the intention.

I want to stay with that last clause, because it is the practical heart of this week. A great many family ruptures begin with a moment that nobody intended as an attack.

Here is how it goes. Two people who have never compared their assumptions finally say them out loud in the same room. Each statement is a sincere description of how that person sees things. But because the assumptions are incompatible, each statement functions as a contradiction of the other, and a contradiction delivered in front of the family lands as an accusation whether or not it was one.

Nobody attacked anybody. Both people simply told the truth about themselves, and the truths did not fit.

Now watch what happens next if nobody is running the room. The person who feels contradicted responds. The response is defensive, because they were not expecting to have to defend anything. The defensiveness is heard as escalation, so the first person hardens, and within ninety seconds a family that had a difference now has a conflict, and everyone will remember it as the day someone said something.

That is the whole mechanism. It is worth knowing precisely, because if you know it, you can interrupt it.

Consider what the three meanings actually do to a person's behavior. Someone who experiences wealth as security will want to preserve, and every proposal to deploy it will feel like exposure. Someone who experiences it as opportunity will want to move, and preservation will feel like waste. Someone who experiences it as responsibility will be watching for who is being careless, and both of the others will look careless to them at different times.

Those three people are not in disagreement about strategy. They are in disagreement about what the money is for, which is upstream of strategy, and no amount of modeling settles it.

Paul's list in 1 Corinthians 13 gets read at weddings, and it belongs in a boardroom at least as much. Look at what he includes. Not self-seeking. Does not dishonor others. Both of those are precisely what fails in the ninety seconds I just described. The first response is self-seeking not out of greed but out of surprise, and it dishonors the other person by treating their sincere statement as an attack.

Patient is the first word in the list, and it is first for a reason.

If the family can be slow for two minutes at the moment the assumptions collide, most of these conflicts do not happen at all.

Reflect

  1. In your largest client family, who is holding wealth as security, who as opportunity, and who as responsibility?

  2. Where have you seen two sincere statements function as a contradiction?

  3. What happens in that family in the ninety seconds after someone says something surprising?

  4. Which family member has never had their view of what the money is for taken seriously?

Day 18

The Instinct Is to Respond Quickly or Defend a Position

That usually makes things worse

"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger." Proverbs 15:1

When a difficult topic comes up, the instinct is often to respond quickly or defend a position. That usually makes things worse.

The way you respond matters just as much as what you say.

I want to be precise about what is happening in that instinct, because calling it a bad habit is not accurate and does not help anybody fix it.

The instinct is protective. Something has been said that threatens either a person's standing in the family or a plan they have invested in, and the nervous system responds before the judgment does. That is not a character flaw. It is equipment, and it is fast because it evolved to be fast.

What it is not is useful in a room where the actual objective is understanding.

Notice that the instinct has two forms and they look different on the surface. Responding quickly is the form it takes in people who are comfortable talking. Defending a position is the form it takes in people who are invested in an outcome. In a family meeting you will usually see both, and often in the same person, one after the other.

Here is what makes them worse rather than neutral. A fast response tells everyone in the room that the previous statement was not considered, which means the person who made it has learned that this room does not think about what they say. That lesson gets learned in about four seconds and takes months to unlearn. And a defended position converts an exploratory statement into a negotiation, because once one person is defending, everybody else has to work out what their position is so they can defend it too.

Both moves take a room that was exploring and turn it into a room that is arguing, and neither person chose that.

Now the part that concerns you directly. You have the instinct too. When a family member says something that undercuts the plan you built, you will feel it, and the professional version of the instinct is subtle. It sounds like clarification. Let me just explain why we structured it that way. That sentence is a defense wearing a helpful expression, and everyone in the room can tell.

Proverbs 15:1 is one of the most practical sentences in the Bible and it is almost always read as advice about temper. Read it as physics instead. A gentle answer turns wrath away, meaning it changes the direction of something already in motion. A harsh word stirs up anger, meaning it creates motion that was not there.

Wrath is treated as a force with a trajectory, and the proverb says your response is the thing that determines where it goes next.

Which means the moment of highest leverage in a difficult family conversation is not when you speak first.

It is the two seconds after somebody else does.

  2. What does your version of let me explain why we structured it that way sound like?

  3. Who in your largest client family will defend a position first?

  4. When has a fast response of yours ended a conversation you wanted to continue?

Day 19

Understanding Has to Come First

Agreement often comes later

"Be completely humble and gentle; be patient, bearing with one another in love. Make every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace." Ephesians 4:2 and 3

I worked with a family where there was a question about how wealth would eventually be distributed. The parents had a clear idea in their minds, but they had never communicated it. The children each had their own expectations, and those expectations were different.

When the conversation finally happened, there was tension almost immediately. One of the children felt like things were not fair. Another felt like they had taken on more responsibility and should be recognized for that.

If that conversation had been handled poorly, it could have created long-term division.

We slowed it down. Instead of trying to resolve the issue right away, we focused on understanding each perspective. Each person had the opportunity to explain how they were thinking and why.

At first, there was some defensiveness. But as people began to listen, not just respond, something shifted. They did not immediately agree, but they began to understand each other.

Agreement often comes later. Understanding has to come first.

That sentence is the most useful thing I know about difficult conversations, and I want to defend it against the way it usually gets heard, which is as a nice sentiment about being kind before being right.

It is not that. It is a claim about what agreement is made of.

Agreement is transactional. It settles a question and closes it, and it can be reached quickly, under pressure, by people who have understood nothing about each other. You can get a family to agreement in an afternoon if you are skilled and they are tired. I have done it. The signatures are real and the arrangement holds until the first time it is tested, at which point you discover that nothing underneath it was ever resolved and the agreement was a truce with paperwork.

Understanding is relational, and it leads to planning and problem solving. It is slower and it produces something that survives contact with reality, because a family that understands why a sibling holds a position can build around that position. A family that merely has the signature cannot.

So when I say understanding has to come first, I am not asking you to be patient as a courtesy. I am telling you that agreement built on nothing is worthless, and you will be back in that room in seven years.

Look at what actually happened in that family. Nobody's view changed that day. The daughter still felt things were not fair. The son still felt his responsibility should count. What changed is that each of them now knew the other one was not being greedy, which is the thing they had privately concluded and would have gone on concluding for the rest of their lives.

Ephesians 4 asks for humility, gentleness, patience, and bearing with one another, and then names the goal, which is keeping unity. Not creating agreement. Keeping a unity Paul treats as already existing and at risk.

That is the right way round for a family. They are already a family. Your job is not to build something.

It is to keep them from losing what they have while they work out the money.

  4. What would change if you told a client the goal of the next meeting was not a decision?

Day 20

If Things Move Too Quickly, People React Instead of Thinking

Slowing it down creates space for better responses

"To slander no one, to be peaceable and considerate, and always to be gentle toward everyone." Titus 3:2

Pace matters. If things move too quickly, people react instead of thinking. Slowing it down creates space for better responses.

You slow the conversation down. You make sure people feel heard before moving forward. And you help shift the focus from being right to understanding each other.

I have come to think pace is the most underrated instrument an advisor has, and the reason is that it is invisible. Nobody in the room will ever say the meeting was well paced. They will say it went well.

Here is what speed does mechanically. A fast conversation runs on retrieval, because that is all there is time for. Someone speaks, and the next person has about a second and a half, which is enough to produce a reaction and not enough to produce a thought. Reactions come from whatever position that person already held, which means a fast conversation cannot change anybody's mind. It can only sort people into the positions they arrived with.

A slow conversation runs on construction. There is time for a person to notice that what they just heard does not fit what they assumed, and noticing that is the entire mechanism by which a family conversation accomplishes anything.

So when you slow a room down, you are not being gentle with people. You are changing what the room is capable of.

The practical question is how, because you cannot simply announce it. Four things work.

Ask for one voice at a time and mean it, which prevents the fast exchange between two people that sets the tempo for everyone else. Put a question to the room and let the first silence run, because the first silence sets the permitted thinking time for the whole meeting. Use the sentence directly. Let us take a moment and make sure we understand what is being said, which I have used hundreds of times and which works because it gives everyone a legitimate reason to stop. And schedule less than you think, since a full agenda enforces speed no matter what anyone intends.

That last one is where most advisors lose it. You cannot pace a meeting you have overfilled.

Titus 3:2 asks for peaceable and considerate conduct, and considerate is the word I want. It is a word about deliberation. It means giving something consideration, which takes time by definition, and it is a strange word to find in a list of instructions about how to treat people, because it makes thinking into a form of courtesy.

That is right, though. Being considerate of a person means being willing to think about what they said rather than answer it.

A family that has never been given time to think about each other is not a family that disagrees.

It is a family that has never had the chance to find out.

Reflect

  2. Which of your meetings is overfilled to the point where pace is impossible?

  3. What is your version of the sentence that gives a room permission to stop?

  4. Where has a fast exchange between two family members set the tempo for a whole meeting?
