DAY 1

Will I Ever Have Enough?

The question every client asked — and what God actually says

The economy is never fully reliable. God always is.

TODAY'S VERSES

Proverbs 30:8–9 "Give me neither poverty nor riches, but give me only my daily bread. Otherwise, I may have too much and disown you and say, 'Who is the Lord?' Or I may become poor and steal, and so dishonor the name of my God."

Matthew 6:33 "But seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well."

Early in my financial planning practice, I had a client who had never earned more than $8,000 a year. He and his wife were both retired. He came to my office and asked me a question I'd been asked hundreds of times, in dozens of different ways, but never quite so plainly: Will we have enough to live on?

I looked at their statement of net worth. In his wife's name: roughly $250,000 in cash, money market funds, and certificates of deposit. In his name: another $350,000. Over $600,000 in cash. On an income that never exceeded $8,000 a year.

Then he mentioned, almost as an aside, that he had some stock. At retirement he'd invested about $10,000 in a small company. The market value of that stock was now $1,063,000.

$1.66 million. Never earned more than $8,000 a year. My advice to him was to not take advice from anyone — myself included — because I might mess him up.

He had simply followed four basic principles, consistently, for decades. And when he finally asked me "will we have enough?" the answer was not only yes — he had enough to give generously for the rest of his life.

I've thought about him many times. Because his question is everyone's question. Not just people in financial crisis. Not just those with anxiety about money. Everyone. The question "Will I ever have enough?" is the question underneath almost every financial decision a person makes. And where you go to answer it determines everything downstream.

The Three Questions People Actually Ask

After forty years of sitting across from families at every income level — from people barely keeping the lights on to people who could buy whatever they wanted — I've found that people really have only three financial questions. They don't always say them out loud, but they're always operating beneath the surface:

Will I ever have enough?

Will it continue to be enough?

How much is enough, anyway?

Those are related questions, but they're not the same question. And here's what I've learned: none of them can be answered by a number. I know that's not what people want to hear. A number would be so much easier. If someone could just tell you the exact figure that constitutes "enough," you could spend your life working toward it and then exhale.

But that's not how it works. The retired pastor with $1.66 million still asked the question. I've worked with people who had ten times that amount, and they still asked the question. The question doesn't stop if a number is doing the answering — because the question isn't really about a number. It's about security, significance, and trust. And money was never designed to carry that weight.

What the Economy Cannot Do

I first wrote Master Your Money in 1986. Between then and now: a Cold War ended, a technology revolution reshaped daily life, a housing market collapsed, a global pandemic stopped the world for two years, and markets have swung in directions nobody predicted. I've been asked throughout every one of those seasons whether my financial advice still holds. Whether the principles still work when the economy doesn't.

My answer has always been the same: the economy is never fully reliable. It moves in cycles no one fully predicted and toward outcomes no one planned. But the principles I've built my practice on — and this devotional on — are not economic principles. They're biblical principles. And the Bible's track record across economic upheaval goes back considerably further than 1986.

I believe God is more interested in each of us individually than He is in any success or failure of our economic system. He is interested in how I glorify Him wherever I live — under any government, in any economy, in any season. That conviction is what held during 2008. It's what holds now.

The Only Answer That Holds

The answer to "will I ever have enough?" is not a number, and it's not an economic forecast. It's a person. Matthew 6:33 gives you the only formula that actually works across every economic condition: seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well.

That's not a promise that everything goes smoothly. It's a promise that you are not navigating alone. And when you build a financial life on that foundation — when the Owner's priorities shape the manager's decisions — something changes. Not the economy. You. The anxiety that used to come with financial uncertainty begins to quiet. Not because the uncertainty disappears. Because you're no longer the last line of defense against it.

Here's what I noticed when I sat with that retired pastor. He wasn't anxious when he asked me if he had enough. He was genuinely curious. There's a difference that matters. Anxious people ask "will I have enough?" because they need the answer to be yes before they can breathe. He was asking because he wanted to know what to do with what God had given him.

That shift — from "will I be okay?" to "what would you have me do with this?" — is the transformation this devotional is after. Not a bigger balance. A different kind of person. One who handles money from a foundation steady enough that the question changes.

Proverbs 30:8–9 is one of the most honest prayers about money in all of Scripture. The writer doesn't ask for wealth. He doesn't ask for poverty. He asks for enough — and the wisdom to be faithful with it. That prayer is as current as this morning. It's a good place to start.

Reflect

When you think about your financial future, which of the three questions runs loudest — Will I ever have enough? Will it stay enough? How much is enough? Sit with the one that rises fastest. What is it really asking?

The retired pastor accumulated $1.66 million on $8,000 a year, following four principles consistently for decades. Something in that story likely lands on you. What is it, and what does that response tell you about what you believe?

If the answer to "will I ever have enough?" is not a number but a person — God himself — what is one thing that changes about how you approach your finances this week?

Today's Step

The question you're carrying about money is real. Before you do anything else with it today — before you check a balance, review an account, or make a plan — bring it to God directly.

Write one sentence that names the specific financial fear or question you carry most often. Then, underneath it, write this: You are not a number. You are the answer.

Pray the prayer from Proverbs 30: "Give me neither poverty nor riches, but give me only what I need to be faithful."

Then rest there for a moment before you open the spreadsheet.

Closing Prayer

Father, I have asked "will I ever have enough?" more times than I can count, in more ways than I've said out loud. Today I bring that question directly to you.

Teach me that you are the answer — not a number, not an economic forecast, not a better plan. You are the only foundation that holds in every season, under every condition, regardless of what the economy does.

Show me what faithful management looks like in my actual life, with what you've actually given me. Settle the question underneath all my other questions. I seek your kingdom first. Everything else, I trust to you.

Amen.
