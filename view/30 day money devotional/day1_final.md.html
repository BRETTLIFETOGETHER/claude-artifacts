THE OWNERSHIP QUESTION · Who Really Owns It All? (Week 1) Day 1 — The Ownership Transfer: Peace Begins When Mine Becomes His

GOD OWNS IT ALL, SO YOU DON'T NEED TO CARRY IT ALL.

Psalm 24:1 (NIV): "The earth is the Lord's, and everything in it, the world, and all who live in it." Haggai 2:8 (NIV): "'The silver is mine and the gold is mine,' declares the Lord Almighty."

The False Divide Between Faith and Finances

A financial advisor connected to our work told me about his college years, when he was studying finance alongside biblical literature. People pushed back. Not strangers, but people from church. He would explain what he was studying and the response was almost always some version of the same objection. Doesn't the Bible say money is evil? Isn't it contradictory to take both seriously at once? He remembers being surprised that it kept coming from churchgoing Christians, people who believed Scripture but had quietly decided that the financial parts of life belonged to a separate, secular category that ran on its own rules.

What he kept finding as he went deeper was that the divide people assumed was not in Scripture at all. Jesus talked about money more than almost any other subject, not because accumulation is the point of life, but because he understood what money does to the human heart when it is held wrong. The wall between sacred life and financial life kept collapsing the more this young man read. He eventually built a career doing exactly what everyone told him was impossible: merging real faith with daily financial counsel, praying with clients, walking with them through their financial lives. And what he keeps running into, with people at every income level, is the same root problem regardless of the numbers. The divide people carry in their heads between money and God costs them more than any bad investment ever could. That divide is the first thing this journey is built to address.

The Most Fundamental Decision a Christian Can Make

I have been saying for nearly fifty years that the most fundamental financial decision a Christian can make is to settle the question of ownership. Not budgeting. Not debt payoff. Not investing. Ownership. Those other things matter, and we will get to them, but they all sit downstream of this one. Until the question of ownership is settled, every financial decision carries hidden weight, the weight of believing that you are ultimately responsible for outcomes that belong to God.

Scripture does not negotiate on this point. Haggai 2:8 does not hedge. The silver is his and the gold is his. Psalm 24 says the earth is the Lord's and everything in it, not a portion of it, not the religious part of it, but all of it and everyone in it. In 1 Chronicles 29, David stacks up one word after another, greatness and power and glory and majesty and splendor, not to make anyone feel small but to describe an ownership so complete that nothing at all falls outside it. And in Matthew 25, in the parable of the talents, Jesus sets the scene with one quiet phrase that reframes everything: a man going on a journey called his servants and entrusted his property to them. Not their property. His. They were managers of what belonged to someone else, and one day they would give an account of how they had handled it.

When I came to understand this rather than merely assent to it, my own thinking shifted. I had spent years on Wall Street and building a CPA firm, in environments where money is simply assumed to be yours. You earn it, you manage it, you protect it, and more of it means more security. Coming to faith changed the frame entirely. What I discovered, to my surprise, was that the best principles I had ever encountered in professional finance all had their roots in Scripture. They were not competing systems. One had simply borrowed from the other without acknowledging the source. The wall I had lived inside turned out to have been built on a false assumption.

Owners and Stewards Carry Different Things

The distinction matters in practice because owners and stewards carry different burdens. An owner has rights, and along with rights comes responsibility for outcomes. A steward has real responsibilities, but the owner keeps the rights and the ultimate accountability for what happens. When my oldest daughter reached driving age, I gave her the keys to my car. There was never any question that I could take that car back at any time, for any reason. She had responsibilities. I retained the rights.

That is the position every one of us is actually in, whether we feel it or not. The title to your home reflects your right to hold it for a season, not to own it forever. Only God owns it forever. If you walk the edges of your own property some evening and think honestly about how long that ground was there before you arrived and how long it will be there after you are gone, the temporary nature of your claim on it becomes difficult to ignore. You possess it for now. You do not own it in any final sense. Someone entrusted it to you for a time, and one day you will hand it to someone else.

This is why ownership-thinking quietly produces anxiety. When you believe what you have is fundamentally yours, earned by you and secured by you and guaranteed by you, then every threat to financial stability feels like a threat to your identity. A surprise expense is not merely inconvenient. It becomes a crack in a foundation you alone are responsible for holding together. A job change is not merely a transition. It feels like a crisis, because you are the one who is supposed to have all of this under control. Underneath every budget conversation and every savings plan is a deeper question almost everyone carries without ever quite saying it out loud. Will I be okay? If everything is mine, the honest answer is that there is never quite enough, because security that depends on accumulation is always one unexpected event away from feeling threatened.

Stewardship changes the assignment. A steward is fully responsible for faithfulness, wisdom, and care. But the steward is not responsible for guaranteeing the future. That belongs to the owner. When you accept that you are a manager rather than an owner, the weight that has been pressing on you finally has somewhere to go. You do not have to carry it, because it was never yours to carry in the first place.

You Can't Fake It

There is one more thing about ownership worth saying plainly. You cannot fake stewardship. This is both the most uncomfortable truth in this whole conversation and the most freeing one. Your checkbook reveals what you actually believe about whose money this is. Not what you say on a Sunday morning, but how you live from Monday through Saturday, what you spend on without thinking, what you protect most fiercely, what you avoid looking at because clarity feels uncomfortable. These are not only financial habits. They are ownership habits, and they show where the real belief lives.

Very few Christians would argue with the principle that God owns it all. But following that principle all the way to its conclusion carries three implications that most of us would rather not face. The first is that God has the right to whatever he wants whenever he wants it, including, if he chooses, the very resources you have been entrusted with. The second is that every spending decision is a spiritual decision, because there is nothing more spiritual about giving to your church than there is about buying a car or paying off a debt or taking your family on a vacation. Each of those is a responsible use of what belongs to him. And the third follows from the first two. You cannot compartmentalize. There is no secular financial life running on one track while your spiritual life runs quietly on another. They are one track, and the question underneath every decision on it is the same. How do I faithfully manage what he has entrusted to me?

That question is what the next thirty days are built around. Not how to optimize a portfolio or build a perfect budget, though there is real and practical work ahead of us. The question is who really owns it, and what that means for everything else. Everything in the weeks to come, the wisdom and the freedom and the planning and the purpose and the generosity, rests on the answer you begin to settle today.

The advisor I mentioned at the beginning carries this conviction into every client meeting he has. He starts in prayer and he ends in prayer, because he has seen what happens when people genuinely transfer ownership, when they stop treating God's resources as theirs to secure and start treating them as a trust. What arrives is not necessarily more money. It is a settled confidence that they are not carrying this alone. That settling, small as it may feel on the first morning of a thirty-day journey, is where everything else begins.

Think About It

The title to your home reflects your right to hold it for a season, not forever, because only God owns it forever. What does it do to the way you think about your home, your savings, or your income to sit honestly with that for a moment?

If God genuinely owns it all, then three things follow: he has the right to whatever he wants whenever he wants it, every spending decision is a spiritual decision, and you cannot fake stewardship because your checkbook tells the real story. Which of those three sits most uncomfortably with you right now, and why?

Exercise

Take ten minutes with a pen and paper. Write one sentence for each. The area of my financial life where I am most clearly acting as an owner rather than a steward is. The fear underneath that grip is. If God is truly the owner, my next faithful step as a steward might look like. Then read all three slowly, out loud if you can, as an honest prayer. You are not solving anything today. You are naming it, and naming it before God is where the transfer begins.

Declaration

Everything I have is yours. I receive it as a gift, hold it as a trust, and release the outcome back to you.

Next Steps

Option A. Before any financial decision today, pause and ask honestly whether you are acting as an owner or a steward right now. Let that single question slow you down just enough to answer from a different place.

Option B. Find five quiet minutes and name the one financial burden you have been carrying as if the outcome depended entirely on you. Say it out loud to God. This is yours. Show me my next faithful step.

Closing Prayer

Father, the earth is Yours, and everything in it, including the things I have been gripping as if they were mine to secure. Forgive me for the weight I have been carrying that was never mine. Today I want to begin where this has to begin, with the honest acknowledgment that You are the owner and I am the steward, a manager with responsibilities rather than an owner with rights. Give me the grace to hold what You have entrusted with open hands, and teach me over these thirty days to manage it in a way that honors You. In Jesus' name, amen.
