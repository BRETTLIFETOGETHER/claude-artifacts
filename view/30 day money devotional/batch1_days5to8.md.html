THE OWNERSHIP QUESTION · Who Really Owns It All? (Week 1) Day 5 — Will I Be Okay? God's Provision and the Daily Surrender

THE ECONOMY IS NEVER RELIABLE. GOD ALWAYS IS.

Daily Inspiration | Watch today's short video: [URL]

Matthew 6:31-33 (NIV): "So do not worry, saying, 'What shall we eat?' or 'What shall we drink?' or 'What shall we wear?' For the pagans run after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them. But seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well." Isaiah 41:10 (NIV): "So do not fear, for I am with you; do not be dismayed, for I am your God. I will strengthen you and help you; I will uphold you with my righteous right hand."

They Got Off Track, Then They Got Back On

A couple whose story was shared with me through our small group ministry have been married more than forty years, and they will tell you honestly that for the first half of that marriage they managed money on their own wisdom, which proved to be less than sufficient. The husband grew up in a household where debt was simply how you acquired things. By the time it reached him, the culture had shifted completely. If you could make the payment, the thinking went, you could afford the thing. He absorbed that logic without questioning it and lived by it for years.

They had three kids, they were doing what people do, and at some point they found themselves carrying more financial weight than the income could comfortably hold. They were not in catastrophe. They were squeezed, the way a lot of households are squeezed, letting money happen to them instead of managing it with intention. He names entitlement as the word that best describes where he was, the half-conscious belief that he deserved the life he had constructed even when the construction required more debt than wisdom. What eventually turned things was a church-based study built around God's view of money. They worked through it together, got back to biblical principles, made changes, and the picture slowly improved. He is careful not to dramatize it. Things just worked out, he says. They rebuilt margin, reduced what they owed, and found that the framework actually held. Decades later they run a financial small group at their church for people who are exactly where they once were. What they offer is not a polished success story. It is the honest testimony of a couple who got off track, got back on, and found that God's provision had been available all along.

The Question Underneath Every Financial Fear

Master Your Money opens with three questions I believe sit underneath most financial anxiety. Will I ever have enough? Will it continue to be enough? And once you have more than you need, how much is enough? These are not theoretical. They are the questions people actually lie awake asking, even when they know better, even when they have given and saved faithfully for years. Underneath the budgets and the plans is a larger question that almost everyone carries for most of a lifetime, because life keeps changing and the question keeps returning. Will I be okay? What okay means is different for every person, but the question is real and legitimate, and no spreadsheet has ever fully answered it.

I do not want to dismiss the fear. I want to put it in the right context. Over nearly fifty years of counseling Christians through every kind of economic season, I have observed one constant. The economy is never reliable. I have watched inflation spike and collapse, watched markets crash and recover and crash again in patterns almost no one predicted. If your sense of security is anchored to the economy's stability, you will never feel secure, because the economy is not stable and never has been. And here is what makes this so important. The fear does not resolve by getting more. I have worked with people who have a great deal of money and are not content, because the same anxiety, the same conflict, the same struggles simply ride along with the rising income. More does not answer the question. Only a different owner does. If it is all mine, there is never quite enough to feel secure. If it is God's, I can trust the provider to give me what I need.

But God is not wringing his hands over any of this. The same God who told Isaiah not to fear was speaking into circumstances far more precarious than most of us will ever face. The instruction is not to pretend uncertainty does not exist. It is to remember who holds what you cannot control.

Seek First, and the Rest Follows

The answer Jesus gives to the anxious heart is not a strategy for accumulating enough to finally feel safe. It is a reordering. Seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well. Notice that Jesus does not deny that you need food and clothing and shelter. He explicitly says your heavenly Father knows that you need them. The problem was never that you have needs. The problem is the order in which those needs sit in your heart. When provision is first, it becomes an anxious master that can never be satisfied. When the kingdom is first, provision falls into its proper place, still real, still necessary, but no longer the thing your peace depends on.

This does not promise that everything you want will arrive on the timeline you would prefer or in the amounts you would choose. Seeking first the kingdom is not a technique for getting rich. It is the reordering that finally lets a person breathe, because it moves the weight of the future off your shoulders and back onto the God who actually carries it. Scripture says it plainly. Godliness with contentment is great gain. Not godliness plus a large enough balance. Godliness with contentment, which is a settled trust that the provider is good, is itself the gain.

What Daily Surrender Actually Looks Like

The fear has an answer, and the answer is not a single dramatic decision. It is a daily one. Stewardship is not primarily a philosophical position. It is a daily practice, and daily practices are built in small, repeated moments. You do not become a steward by agreeing that God owns everything. You become one by making one daily decision after another that reflects that belief. Ownership is something you believe. Stewardship is a habit, and habits are formed in the small repeatable moments, not in one grand resolution.

An advisor connected to our work carries his earliest money memory into every client meeting. He grew up on a farm in Indiana, and as a boy he had a piggy bank with three sections, one for giving, one for saving, one for living. He does not remember a formal lesson attached to it. What he remembers is the simple act of sorting whatever came in, each section meaning something different. He grew up to become a financial advisor, and one of the first things he explains to clients is that there are only five things you can ever do with money. You can give it, pay taxes with it, repay debt with it, spend it on your lifestyle, or save it. That is the entire universe of options. There are no independent financial decisions. Every yes is also a no somewhere else. The three-section piggy bank and the five-uses framework are the same idea at different scales, and both say the same thing. The way you handle money is a set of daily choices, not one dramatic moment, and the direction of those choices over time is what actually shapes a life.

That is what Matthew 6 is describing. Seek first the kingdom, and the things you need will be provided. Not necessarily in the amounts you would choose or on the timeline you would prefer, but provided. The question will I be okay has an answer, and it does not come from your portfolio or your planning alone. It comes from the character of the one who owns everything you are trying to hold together. The surrender is not a one-time transaction you complete and move past. It is a returning, morning by morning, to the same settled truth. This is Yours. I am the manager. I do not have to carry the weight of the outcome, because the outcome was never mine to secure.

Think About It

When you examine your financial habits honestly, is there an area where entitlement thinking is quietly operating, the belief that you deserve something your income cannot fully support? What story have you been telling yourself about what you are owed?

Looking back at your own life, can you identify a season where God's provision was quiet and steady rather than dramatic? What did it look like, and how clearly could you see it at the time versus in hindsight?

Exercise

The book opens with three questions. Will I ever have enough? Will it continue to be enough? How much is enough? Take ten minutes and write one honest paragraph in response to each, not the answer you know you are supposed to give, but the answer that reflects what you actually feel when you let your guard down. Then read what you wrote and ask which question is carrying the most weight right now, and what it would mean to hand that specific weight to God today.

Declaration

God is not wringing His hands over my financial life. He knows what I need, and He is with me. I will seek His kingdom first and trust Him with everything else.

Next Steps

Option A. Identify the specific financial fear that surfaces most reliably in quiet moments. Write it in one sentence. Then write one sentence that begins, God, I trust You with this because. Let that be your prayer today.

Option B. If you have been off track financially and have known it, choose one concrete step back toward the framework this week. Not a complete overhaul. One step back toward what you know is right, and trust that the path forward will be there when you take it.

Closing Prayer

Father, the question underneath so much of my financial anxiety is whether I will be okay. I confess that I have answered it with numbers and projections rather than with trust in You. Today I want to remember that You are not surprised by any economic season and that Your provision has a long track record in the lives of people who came back to You after getting off track. Give me courage to name honestly the fears I carry about money. Then take them. You told Isaiah not to fear because You were with him. I believe You are with me, and I am choosing today to let that be enough. In Jesus' name, amen.

THE WISDOM QUESTION · What Does the Bible Actually Say About Money? (Week 1) Day 6 — Principles, Not Hacks: God's Wisdom Has a Purpose

BIBLICAL FINANCIAL TRUTH HAS BEEN AROUND FOR THOUSANDS OF YEARS. IT WORKS BECAUSE IT COMES FROM THE ONE WHO DESIGNED EVERYTHING IT GOVERNS.

Daily Inspiration | Watch today's short video: [URL]

James 1:5 (NIV): "If any of you lacks wisdom, you should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault, and it will be given to you." Matthew 7:24-25 (NIV): "Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock. The rain came down, the streams rose, and the winds blew and beat against that house; yet it did not fall, because it had its foundation on the rock."

What He Learned in 2008

A financial advisor in our Kingdom Advisors network had been giving financial advice professionally since 2000, and for the first eight years he operated as what he would later describe as a secular, worldly advisor. He was a Christian by belief, but faith had no functional place in how he ran his practice or managed his own money. He was largely doing what the industry expected, chasing commissions and trying to stay ahead of the market.

Then 2008 arrived. The years from 2000 to 2010 were the worst decade for the stock market since the Great Depression, and the crash hit him professionally at the same time it hit his clients. He found himself carrying significant personal financial stress while dispensing financial advice to others, and the gap between what he was telling people and how he was living was hard to ignore. It was during that season that a friend shared his testimony openly in a secular business setting. That act of openness got his attention in a way a more expected religious context might not have. He ended up in a men's Bible study, found a Christian financial planner, and watched what he described as God single-handedly putting things together for him. He stopped his solo practice, founded a faith-based wealth management firm, and built it around one conviction. The financial principles that actually produce peace and stability are not new. They are thousands of years old. They are in Proverbs and illustrated in the parables of Jesus, and they work not because they are clever but because they come from the one who designed everything they govern. His firm now serves a couple thousand households, and the first thing he establishes with every new client is the same thing. The foundational truths, not the latest strategy.

What I Told a United States Senator

I have been making a similar argument for nearly fifty years, and the clearest version happened unexpectedly. In the early 1990s I was testifying before a congressional subcommittee. Senator Dodd of Connecticut was doing the interviewing, and his question was simple. What would you tell the American family about their money? I thought what I was about to say would make him laugh. So I told him. Spend less than you earn, avoid the use of debt, build some margin into your finances, and set long-term goals so you can prioritize between the short term and the long term. Four things. I stopped and looked up. The senator picked up his pencil and wrote them down, repeated them back to me, and said it seemed to him those would work at any income level. I said he was right, including the United States government.

That exchange stuck with me because it illustrated something I had observed across thousands of client relationships at every income level. The principles that produce financial stability are not complicated and they are not new. They are not the product of sophisticated modeling or one clever advisor. They are ancient, rooted in Scripture, and they work for anyone who applies them, because they are principles rooted in the nature of reality as God designed it. When you add a fifth principle to those four, give generously, you have the complete picture. These five have never changed across the six editions of the book. Every other chapter was updated for new tax laws and new economic realities. The core principles were never dependent on economic circumstances to be true.

Wisdom Is Something You Ask For

Before any of the five principles becomes practical, there is a posture that makes them possible, and James names it exactly. If any of you lacks wisdom, you should ask God, who gives generously and does not find fault. I want you to notice two things in that sentence. The first is that wisdom is not a personality trait reserved for people who are naturally good with money. It is something you pursue, humbly and repeatedly, and it is available to anyone willing to ask. The second is the character of the God who gives it. He gives generously, and he does not find fault. He is not annoyed by your questions. He is not standing over your financial history shaking his head. He invites you to learn, and he answers the asking.

That matters because most people carry a quiet shame about money that keeps them from ever asking for help, from God or from anyone else. They assume they should already know, and the assumption keeps them stuck in silence. James removes the shame. You lack wisdom, so you ask, and the God who owns it all delights to give it. Financial wisdom, in the biblical sense, does not begin with a technique. It begins with a request, offered by a person humble enough to admit they do not have it all figured out.

Why People Chase Hacks Instead

If these principles are this reliable and this available, the obvious question is why so many people are not applying them. The advisor in this story put it plainly. People are always chasing what is shiny or new, looking for the magic solution, the hack that will change everything without requiring the slow accumulation of faithful small decisions. This is not a new problem. Every generation produces its version of the financial shortcut, the scheme that promises to compress decades of patient stewardship into a season of rapid results. The specific shape changes. The underlying appeal does not.

Here is what I have watched in fifty years of sitting across from people in financial stress. The ones in trouble are almost never there because they lacked access to sound advice. They are there because they did not apply what they already knew. The discipline of actually living by the principles felt slower and less satisfying than the alternatives their environment kept offering, so the foundation never got built, and when the storm came, there was nothing to hold. That is the exact picture Jesus paints at the end of the Sermon on the Mount. The wise builder and the foolish builder both hear the same words. The difference is not knowledge. It is whether they put the words into practice. Biblical wisdom is not designed to impress people in the short term. It is designed to keep them standing in the long term. The house on the rock does not look extraordinary when the weather is calm. Its superiority becomes visible exactly when conditions become difficult, and that is when the people who built on it are grateful they did not choose something more exciting.

Think About It

Where do you notice a gap in your own life between the financial advice you would give someone else and what you are actually doing yourself?

Think about someone you know at a very different financial position than your own. Of the five principles, which one would make the most difference for them right now? Which one would make the most difference for you?

Exercise

The five principles are spend less than you earn, avoid debt, build margin, set long-term goals, and give generously. Rate your current practice of each on a scale of one to five, where one means you consistently do the opposite and five means you practice it reliably. Write the number beside each one honestly. Then pick the one with the lowest number and write one sentence. The most concrete change I could make this week to raise my score on this principle would be. Carry that sentence into the rest of this week.

Declaration

God's wisdom about money has a shape, and that shape does not change. I will build on what is ancient and proven rather than chase what is new and untested.

Next Steps

Option A. Pull up your last ninety days of statements and look at the overall picture honestly. You do not need to categorize every transaction. Just look at the direction. Does the pattern reflect someone building on a solid foundation, or someone chasing something that is not working?

Option B. Find one person in your life who is currently making a financial decision based on a shortcut or trend. Pray for them today. You do not need to correct them. Just hold them before God and ask for wisdom in how and whether to speak.

Closing Prayer

Father, we come to this second question with a different focus. Not who owns what, but what wisdom actually looks like when it is applied to money. Thank You that the principles that produce financial peace are ancient and available and do not require sophistication to understand. Forgive us for the times we have chased whatever was shiny rather than returning to what You have established. Give us patience to build slowly on a foundation that will hold, and as we work through each principle, open our eyes not just to understand them but to actually live by them. In Jesus' name, amen.

THE WISDOM QUESTION · What Does the Bible Actually Say About Money? (Week 1) Day 7 — Spend Less Than You Earn: Margin Is the Load-Bearing Wall

THE FIRST PRINCIPLE IS THE LOAD-BEARING WALL. REMOVE IT AND EVERYTHING ELSE FALLS.

Daily Inspiration | Watch today's short video: [URL]

Proverbs 21:20 (NIV): "The wise store up choice food and olive oil, but fools gulp theirs down." 2 Thessalonians 3:8-9 (NIV): "nor did we eat anyone's food without paying for it. On the contrary, we worked night and day, laboring and toiling so that we would not be a burden to any of you. We did this, not because we do not have the right to such help, but in order to offer ourselves as a model for you to imitate."

The Move They Did Not Want to Make

A young financial advisor in the Kingdom Advisors network tells the story of 2022 as the year he and his wife changed nearly everything. They were living in Indianapolis, had been married five years, and were six months into their first pregnancy when they began to sense God calling them to leave. Neither of them wanted to go. By any reasonable assessment, staying made more sense. What followed was months of late nights and long conversations. They came back to the same conviction. When they held tightly to what they wanted, they could feel it pulling them away from where God was leading. When they surrendered their plans, something settled. They made the move, their first child arrived, and in the three years since, the growth in his marriage and career exceeded anything he could have planned.

He tells that story now when he sits down with new clients, because it illustrates something he believes about every area of stewardship. The first step of releasing what you are gripping too tightly is also the first step toward what is actually possible. And when he teaches the five financial principles, the first one, spend less than you make, is where he always begins, because he has watched what happens to people who try to skip it. If you do not do that first one, he explains, you do not have the ability to do any of the other things that need to be done with money. That one makes all the difference.

Why the First Principle Is First

I want to say something plainly that I have spent decades trying to communicate. The answer to most financial problems is not more income. The problem is almost never what comes in. The problem is the relationship between what comes in and what goes out. I have in my files a list I have used in teaching for years of people who earned extraordinary amounts of money and still ended up bankrupt. Every one of them had more money flowing through their hands than nearly any person reading this will ever see, and none of it was enough, because the principle that governs whether any income produces stability was never applied. The spending simply expanded to fill what came in and then exceeded it.

The Bible does not celebrate consumption. It celebrates wisdom, and a wise person does not devour everything that comes in. That is exactly the picture in Proverbs 21:20. The wise store up; the fool gulps it all down. In plain terms, if everything is always gone, you have more than a math problem. You have a margin problem, and margin is what keeps you from living on the edge. What I observed across thousands of client relationships is this. The people who achieve genuine financial stability are not primarily the people who earned the most. They are the people who, at some point, decided the spending would not outpace the income, and then held to that decision through every season. That decision is boring. It is unglamorous. And it is the single most consequential financial choice most people will ever make. The household that spends less than it earns is gathering little by little whether it realizes it or not, and gathering little by little, done consistently, is how ordinary people build extraordinary stability. That something left, however small it begins, is what I mean by margin, and margin is the only mechanism by which the other financial principles become possible to practice.

What Margin Actually Does

I write in Master Your Money about a retired pastor I encountered early in my counseling career. He had never earned more than eight thousand dollars in a single year. He came to me at age eighty, wanting to know if he had enough to live out the rest of his life, with his wife newly in need of full-time nursing care. I walked through my usual questions. Did he have any debt? No, he had never borrowed money. Why not? Because he knew he would have to pay it back someday, and he could not afford to service debt, feed his family, and tithe at the same time. His strategy was not sophisticated. He always tithed, paid his taxes, and lived on what was left. He had never spent more than he earned in his life. And at eighty, on a lifetime of modest income, he had enough.

That man understood something many people with far higher incomes never grasp. Margin is not a product of earning more. It is a product of consistently spending less than you earn over a long period. The math is so simple it sounds obvious. The behavior is so countercultural it requires genuine discipline and a real reordering of what you believe your income is for. The first principle is the load-bearing wall of the entire structure. Margin is what makes it possible to pay off debt rather than service it indefinitely. Margin is what makes saving possible. Margin is what makes generosity sustainable rather than an occasional gesture. Remove the first principle and all the others remain technically possible to believe while being practically impossible to execute. Paul put it in the most concrete terms in his letter to the Thessalonians. He worked night and day so that he would not be a burden to anyone. He was not describing a strategy for wealth. He was describing the discipline of carrying your own weight, and that same commitment, applied to a household, is simply this. The spending will not outpace the income. There will be something left.

Margin Is Not the Goal, It Is the Door

It is worth saying clearly that spending less than you earn is not an end in itself, and it is not a call to a joyless, tightfisted life. Wisdom is not the refusal to ever enjoy anything, and it is not the compulsion to devour everything either. It sits between those two, and the point of the discipline is not the discipline. The point is what margin makes possible. When there is room between the income and the outgo, a household can breathe. It can absorb a surprise without a crisis. It can respond when a need appears. It can eventually give with real freedom rather than guilt, and save toward something that matters rather than merely survive from one month to the next. Margin, in other words, is not the destination. It is the door every other good financial thing has to walk through. That is why the first principle comes first. Not because frugality is a virtue in itself, but because without margin, every other faithful intention stays locked outside, unable to become a practice.

Think About It

When you think honestly about your financial life, is your stress primarily an income problem or a margin problem? What would change if you treated it as a margin problem regardless of the income level?

The retired pastor who never earned more than eight thousand dollars a year had a simple strategy: tithe, pay taxes, live on the rest, and never borrow. What would it look like to apply that same simple strategy, in updated form, to your current household? What would you have to stop spending to make the math work?

Exercise

Track every dollar you spend for the next seven days. Not to judge your choices, but to get an accurate picture of the relationship between what comes in and what goes out. At the end of seven days, calculate whether you spent less than you earned this week. If yes, identify what created that margin and how to protect it. If no, identify the single largest category where spending exceeded what you intended, and write one concrete change you could make next week to begin closing the gap. One change, one week. That is how the first principle gets built into a life.

Declaration

I will not spend today what I will need tomorrow. Margin is not a luxury. It is the foundation on which everything else I want to build must stand.

Next Steps

Option A. Before your next purchase this week, ask one question. Does this purchase leave margin, or does it consume it? You do not need to refuse every purchase. Just let the question interrupt the automatic yes long enough to make a conscious choice.

Option B. If you are not currently spending less than you earn, identify one recurring expense this week that you could reduce or eliminate. Not the largest one necessarily, just the one that feels most changeable right now. Name it, write it down, make one call or one cancellation.

Closing Prayer

Father, the wisdom here is so simple it almost sounds too simple. Spend less than I earn. Gather little by little rather than consume everything that comes in. And yet I live in a world that works against this at every turn, where every easy payment option tells me that what I want now should not have to wait. Give me the discipline to believe what is obvious and to actually live by it. Show me where the margin is leaking out of my finances, and give me courage to close the gaps rather than ignore them. Help me carry my own weight and not become a burden to my own future, and hold to this first principle through every season ahead. In Jesus' name, amen.

THE WISDOM QUESTION · What Does the Bible Actually Say About Money? (Week 2) Day 8 — Avoid the Slavery of Debt: Freedom Is Easier to Protect Than Recover

DEBT ALWAYS MORTGAGES THE FUTURE. FREEDOM IS EASIER TO PROTECT THAN TO RECOVER.

Daily Inspiration | Watch today's short video: [URL]

Proverbs 22:7 (NIV): "The rich rule over the poor, and the borrower is slave to the lender." Romans 13:8 (NIV): "Let no debt remain outstanding, except the continuing debt to love one another, for whoever loves others has fulfilled the law."

When Retirement Meant Death

An advisor connected to our work spent nearly two decades succeeding by every external measure. He worked for a great company, and the promotions kept coming. What he did not see at the time was the pattern underneath the success. In the pursuit of how much is enough, he kept chasing more, and the chase pulled him away from home. He spent months in hotels across the country while his wife raised their three children largely on her own. And even as the income rose, the debt rose with it. They were spending more than they were making, he says, just like a great many households do, and the financial strain settled into their marriage as a quiet, constant weight.

The moment he names as the turning point came on a business trip. Someone asked him a simple question. What is the number you need in order to retire? His honest answer was death. He could see no end to the working, because he was carrying every kind of obligation a household can carry: a mortgage, credit card balances, student loans, medical debt, leased cars. There was no version of the future in which he stopped. Then he began, in his words, to do his finances God's way. It was not fast. It took about seven years. But they paid off everything they owed, including the mortgage, and by the ages of forty and forty-one they were completely debt-free. In the years since, he describes a freedom he could not have imagined during the season when retirement felt like a synonym for the grave. What debt had quietly taken over eighteen years, faithful stewardship gave back, one obligation at a time.

What Scripture Says Plainly

Debt is normal in our culture. It is expected, even promoted. Scripture speaks about it with unusual clarity. The borrower is slave to the lender. I want to be careful with a verse like that, because it is easily misheard. It does not mean you are a bad person if you have debt, and it does not mean all debt is inherently sinful. It means debt has power. It reduces your options. It increases fear. It can quietly shrink your generosity, because money already committed to a lender cannot be given to anyone else. And it is not only the numbers. There is an emotional drag to debt, an undercurrent of anxiety and sometimes shame, and it can affect the very tone of a home. Even debt that looks manageable on paper can slowly become a kind of bondage, not because the payments are impossible, but because the obligation is always there, shaping what you can and cannot do.

So these Scriptures are not condemning anyone. They are warning us, and they are naming honestly what debt tends to do over time. Biblical wisdom always tells the truth, and the truth here is protective. It is a warning to avoid debt where you can and an encouragement to climb out where you are already in. Paul draws the same line from a different direction when he writes to let no debt remain outstanding except the debt of love. The picture is of a life kept as free as possible from obligation, so that the one thing you owe everyone, love, has room to be paid.

Debt Mortgages the Future

The deepest problem with debt is what it does to time. Debt always mortgages the future. Every dollar borrowed is a claim placed on income you have not yet earned, a piece of tomorrow spent today. That is why debt so often shows up in this teaching alongside the question of freedom, because the two are directly connected. To go into debt is to hand a portion of your future freedom to a lender in exchange for something you want now. Sometimes that trade is wise and carefully considered. Very often it is not considered at all. The culture the man in this story absorbed said that if you could make the payment, you could afford the thing, and that quiet logic is how a household ends up owing on cars, cards, and a lifestyle that the income was never actually large enough to sustain.

The reason the subtitle of today's reading says freedom is easier to protect than to recover is that this is simply how debt works in a life. Staying out of debt requires ongoing discipline, but climbing out of debt requires that same discipline sustained for years, often through real sacrifice, while the interest keeps working against you the entire time. The man who reached freedom by forty did not get there quickly or painlessly. It took seven years of doing things differently. His story is genuinely hopeful, and it should be. But the easier path, wherever it is still available to you, is to protect the freedom you have rather than to spend it and then spend years buying it back.

Not Condemnation, but a Plan

If you are carrying debt right now, hear this clearly. This is not condemnation. It is clarity, and clarity is a gift, because a named problem is a solvable one. The point of a day like this is not to make anyone feel ashamed of the balances they carry. It is to give a reason to make a plan and to start climbing out. The path is not complicated, though it is not easy. It begins where the man in this story began, by deciding that the pattern will change, and then taking the first concrete step, the same way you would take any first step of faithfulness, before all the conditions feel ideal.

For most households, the practical starting point is the highest-interest consumer debt, the credit cards and similar obligations where the cost of carrying the balance is greatest. Eliminating a balance that charges eighteen or twenty percent is not a timid financial move. It is one of the highest guaranteed returns available to anyone, because every dollar of that interest you stop paying is a dollar kept. From there the work is simply to keep going, one obligation at a time, protecting the margin from the first principle so that it flows toward payoff rather than dispersing into new spending. Debt did not accumulate in a day, and it rarely leaves in a day. But it does leave, for the household that decides freedom is worth the years it takes to recover it.

Think About It

Proverbs 22:7 says the borrower is slave to the lender. Setting aside whether your debt is manageable on paper, where do you feel the weight of it, in your options, your peace, your generosity, or the tone of your home? Name the specific place it presses.

The man in this story could not imagine retirement because he could see no end to the obligations he carried. If your current debt continued at its present pace, what would your financial life look like in ten years? And what is one decision you could make this week to change that trajectory?

Exercise

List every debt you currently carry except your mortgage. For each one, write down the balance, the interest rate, and the monthly payment. This is not a comfortable exercise, and it is not meant to be. It is meant to replace a vague sense of pressure with a clear, honest picture, because you cannot make a plan against a number you have never written down. When the list is complete, circle the debt with the highest interest rate. That is almost always where the climb out begins, not necessarily the smallest balance, but the one costing you the most to carry.

Declaration

I will not mortgage my future for what I want today. Where I am in debt, I will make a plan and begin to climb out. Where I am free, I will protect that freedom as a gift.

Next Steps

Option A. If you carry consumer debt, choose your first payoff target this week using the list from the exercise. Decide on one specific amount, above the minimum, that you will direct toward that debt this month, and set it up before the month begins.

Option B. If you are currently debt-free or nearly so, protect it. Before your next significant purchase, ask honestly whether financing it would trade a piece of your future freedom for something you want now, and whether that trade is one you actually want to make.

Closing Prayer

Father, thank You that Your warnings about debt are not meant to shame me but to protect me. You know how quickly an obligation can take the peace out of a home and the freedom out of a future. Where I have borrowed against tomorrow without weighing the cost, forgive me, and give me the resolve to begin climbing out, one honest step at a time. Where I still have freedom, help me hold it as the gift it is rather than spending it for something that will not last. Teach me to owe no one anything except the debt of love, and to trust that the life kept free of these weights has more room in it for You and for the people You have given me. In Jesus' name, amen.
