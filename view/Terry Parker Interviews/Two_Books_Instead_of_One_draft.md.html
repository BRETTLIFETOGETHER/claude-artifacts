Two Books Instead of One

A Fable About Everything We Try to Keep

Draft one. Working text for cutting.

Prologue: The Desk I Built

I had built the desk myself, or near enough.

Twelve years of eighty-hour weeks and four names on the door, mine third from the left, and the desk was walnut and eleven feet wide and I had earned every inch of it. My wife thought it was absurd. She said it looked like something you would land a small plane on. She was right, and I did not care, because people came from across the city to sit on the other side of it. They came with a problem and I gave them a strategy and they paid me for the strategy, and that was the arrangement, and I had never once questioned it.

I want to be honest about what I believed then, because it will matter later.

I believed that the world was full of people who had worked hard for things and other people who wanted to take those things from them. I believed my job was to stand between the two. I believed that being right was most of the battle and that being right with documentation was all of it. And I believed, without ever having said it out loud, that a man's job was to accumulate a wall of things that could not be taken away from him, and that once the wall was tall enough he would finally be able to relax behind it.

I was thirty-four years old. I had never met anyone who thought differently, so I assumed nobody did.

Then a man named Ray sat down in the chair across from me three separate times over about fifteen years, and each time he took something out of my hands, and I want to tell you what it cost me and what it bought.

Visit One: The Red Book

He came in with a red book and dropped it on the walnut.

"He used my notes," he said. "Every page of them."

Ray was not an impressive man to look at. He had the build of someone who had once worked outdoors and then spent twenty years not doing that, and he wore the same brown jacket every time I saw him for a decade and a half. He had come to me a year earlier to set up a small organization, the kind of thing I did in an afternoon and billed four hours for. He taught people how to handle money. He had a set of notes he used, a stack of mimeographed pages he'd hand out at the end of an evening, and by then a few thousand people had sat through it.

I picked up the red book. It was cheaply bound, maybe two hundred pages, and the author's name on the cover was Denton, which meant nothing to me. I opened it to the middle and read half a page and something moved in my chest that I can only describe as appetite.

This was the part of the work I loved. Not the counseling, not the structuring. This. Somebody had taken something and I was going to make him give it back.

"Leave it with me," I said.

I took it home that night and read it beside my wife with a legal pad on my knee. She was reading something else and occasionally saying things to me that I did not hear. By eleven o'clock I had found forty. By midnight I had found two hundred. When I finally stopped I had three hundred and fifty separate places where this man had taken sentences that belonged to my client and printed them under his own name, and I had the page numbers for every one of them.

Fifteen thousand copies in print. Two hundred and fifty dollars a violation, statutory. I did the arithmetic twice, because the first number seemed impolite, and the second time it came out the same.

I did not sleep well and it was not from worry.

I called him at eight in the morning.

I told him we had an airtight case. I told him we would get an injunction inside of a month and the books would come off every shelf in the country. I told him the damages number and I let it sit in the silence for a second, the way you do. I told him the only question remaining was how much of this man's life he wanted, and that he should think carefully, because I could get most of it.

There was a pause on the line.

"Let me think about that," Ray said. "I'll call you back."

He did not call me back.

A week went by, which did not concern me. Two weeks, which did. At a month I called him myself, mildly irritated, because I had an associate holding time and I do not like holding time.

"Oh," he said, as though I had reminded him of a chore. "I'm not going to do anything about that."

I asked him why not.

I want to be precise about my tone here, because I have thought about it many times since. I was not curious. I was not asking a question. I was correcting a client who had misunderstood his own position, and there is a particular voice lawyers use for that, patient on top and impatient underneath, and I used it.

He said, "You can't copyright God's principles."

I started to answer. I had three answers ready and all of them were good. And before I could get to the first one he said the thing that has never once left me.

"Besides," he said. "Now there's going to be two books instead of one."

I sat there with the phone against my ear and my legal pad in front of me on eleven feet of walnut, three hundred and fifty tick marks running down the page in my own handwriting, and I understood for the first time in my adult life that I had spent an entire evening carefully counting something that a wiser man than me considered a gift.

I did not say any of that. I said something professional and I hung up. Then I sat there for a while.

Here is what I could not get past, and it was not the theology. It was the arithmetic. I had done arithmetic all night and arrived at three hundred and fifty. He had done different arithmetic and arrived at two. And his was the number that was actually true, because a year later there were two books teaching the same thing to two sets of people, and mine would have produced one book and one ruined man and a fee.

I had been trained to protect. It had never occurred to me to ask what I was protecting it from, or whether the thing was even the kind of thing that could be held.

Visit Two: The Woman Who Kept the Books

Four years later he was back in the chair, and by then the thing he had started was real.

Not big. Real. He had an office above a print shop and a mailing list and a woman named Verna who did everything else. Verna had come in two days a week to type and had somehow become the entire administrative function of the organization, and she did it on a manual typewriter with carbon paper, three copies of every letter, and when the checks came in around Thanksgiving she took the box home with her because there were more of them than would fit in a day.

He came to me about the corporate filings. We finished those in ten minutes and then he sat there, which was unusual for him, and finally he said, "I want to do something for Verna."

I said fine. I said, what's her salary.

He told me. It was low. Not shameful, but low.

"So give her a raise," I said. "Eight percent, ten if the giving holds up. Do it in January so it doesn't look like a Christmas thing. I'll draft the letter, it's twenty minutes."

He shook his head slowly, in the way he had, which was never argumentative and was somehow worse than argumentative.

"Her car died in March," he said. "She's been getting a ride from her sister-in-law, who works the other side of town, so she comes in at nine forty and leaves at four to make the pickup. She hasn't said a word about it. I found out from the print shop."

I said, "So give her a raise and she can buy a car."

"No," he said. "Don't give her a raise. Buy her a car."

I explained why that was wrong. I was good at explaining things and this was a target-rich environment. A car is a one-time expense and a raise is permanent, so the raise is worth more to her over any reasonable horizon. A car creates precedent, and the moment somebody else's transmission goes you own a fleet. There are tax consequences to a vehicle transfer that there aren't to compensation. And most of all, and I said this with some feeling, it is not the employer's job to know what an employee's problems are. That is what money is for. You pay people fairly and you let them be adults about the rest of it.

He listened to the whole thing. He was a good listener, which I had begun to find irritating.

Then he said, "A raise says I know what you're worth. A car says I know what you need."

And then he said the part I have used a hundred times since without ever once giving him credit, which I suppose is its own kind of joke.

"If I give her a raise," he said, "she'll spend eight months deciding whether she can afford a car, and she'll buy the wrong one, and she'll still be tired. She's not a line on a page. She's the reason the thing runs."

We bought her a car. It was a used sedan, four years old, and I structured it as cleanly as I could and it was still messier than a raise. And I will tell you what I noticed, because I did notice, and it took me another decade to understand what I was noticing.

Verna stayed nineteen more years.

I have watched a great many organizations since then. I have watched people pay market rate and lose everyone, and I have watched people pay under market and keep everyone, and the difference was never the number. The number is what you owe. The number is the floor of the relationship, not the relationship. Every one of us knows the difference between being compensated and being known, and we can tell in about four seconds which one is happening to us.

That was the second thing he took out of my hands, and I resisted it harder than the first, because the first one had only cost me a fee. This one cost me the belief that I could discharge my obligation to a human being by paying it.

Visit Three: The Drawer

The third visit was different, and I remember it more clearly than the other two, because it was the only time I ever saw him look tired.

By then I had left the firm, or most of the way left it. I had spent years doing the legal work for the thing he had built and for two or three others like it, mostly at night and mostly for nothing, and one day I had gone to my partners and told them I wanted to spend real time on it. They were decent about it. They cut my draw by a third and kept my name on the door, which is about as decent as that conversation gets.

I was running my own piece of it by then out of a drawer. That is not a figure of speech. There was no office and no telephone number and no letterhead. There was a file drawer in my desk, and in the drawer were the folders, and that was the entire organization. If you had wanted to visit us there was no address I could have given you.

And it was not working.

That year I went to see everyone. I flew to four cities in six weeks on my own money and sat in four conference rooms and explained the whole thing to four sets of very polite people. I told them what we could do. I told them we could take a gift that wasn't money, a piece of a company, a building, a piece of land, and turn it into something they could actually use, and that nobody else in the country was set up to do that. They took notes. They walked me to the elevator. Two of them wrote afterward to say what a valuable meeting it had been.

Nobody called.

I came home and I told Ray about it over coffee, and I did not dress it up, because I had gotten past the point of dressing things up with him. I said we had been at this a long time. I said I had a drawer and a stack of folders and a reduced income and four organizations who thought it was a wonderful idea and had done nothing about it. I said the thing I had really come to say, which was that a reasonable man would stop.

He drank some of his coffee and looked out the window for a while.

"Well," he said. "If God is in this, the money will come in. If he's not in it, shut it down and go do something else."

I remember being angry.

Understand what I had wanted from him. I had wanted encouragement, which is what you want from a friend, or a strategy, which is what I would have offered in his chair. And instead he handed me permission to quit, delivered so casually that it was clear he did not consider quitting a tragedy.

I drove home furious. And somewhere on the way it turned into something else, and I have never been able to reconstruct exactly where.

Because here is what he had actually done. For thirteen years I had been carrying the outcome. Not the work, the outcome. Every unreturned call had been mine to fix and every empty month had been mine to explain, and it had never occurred to me that the result of the thing might not be my department. He handed it back to me as a genuinely open question. Maybe this is God's and it will go. Maybe it isn't and you should go sell insurance, and either one of those is a fine Tuesday.

I did not shut it down. That is not the point of the story and I want to be careful not to make it the point, because the shape of that ending is too tidy and life did not offer it to me that tidily.

The point is that I stopped carrying something I had never actually been strong enough to carry, and I had been carrying it for thirteen years, and I had not known it was heavy until I set it down.

What happened afterward is a longer story and belongs somewhere else. But the drawer is not a drawer anymore, and the number of families it has served is a number I do not say out loud, because when I say it people hear the number instead of the story, and the story is a man in a brown jacket telling me it would be perfectly all right if none of it ever worked.

Epilogue: The Other Chair

Ray has been gone some years now. I sat with him near the end, in a room with the blinds half down, and we did not talk about any of this. We talked about his grandchildren and a fishing trip he had taken in 1971.

I still have a desk. It is smaller. That was not a spiritual decision, it was a smaller office.

But I have noticed something in the last few years that I did not expect, and it is the reason I have written any of this down. People come and sit in the chair across from me, the way they always have, and they bring me a problem, and increasingly the problem they bring is not the problem. A man came in last spring about the structure of a sale and what he wanted to talk about was his son. A woman came in about a trust and what she wanted was for somebody to tell her that thirty years of building the company had not been a waste because she had missed things.

And I find that I have very little strategy to offer them. What I have instead is three sentences that a man said to me across this same distance, in a voice so unbothered that I mistook it for simplicity, when it was the opposite.

You can't copyright God's principles.

Don't give her a raise, buy her a car.

If God is in this, the money will come in.

Every one of those sentences took something out of my hands. My ideas. My people. My outcomes. I fought him on all three and I lost all three, and I have never once gotten any of them back, and I would not take them back now if you set them on the desk in front of me.

I used to think the chair on that side of the desk was where the person with the problem sat.

The Model: Ideas, People, Outcomes

Everything in the preceding pages is one lesson taught three times, at rising cost.

The first release is your ideas, and it costs you credit. What you know, what you built, what you figured out first. The instinct is to protect it, and protection is not irrational, it is simply expensive in ways the balance sheet does not record. The question that dissolves it is not who took this from me but did the thing advance. Those two questions feel similar and they lead in opposite directions. One produces an injunction. The other produces two books instead of one.

The second release is your people, and it costs you control. Paying someone fairly is the floor of a relationship and most of us treat it as the ceiling. A raise answers the question of worth. It does not answer the question of need, and it does not require you to know anything about the person. Knowing costs more, it does not scale, it creates precedent, and it is the entire difference between an employee who is compensated and an employee who stays nineteen years.

The third release is your outcome, and it costs you security. This is the one nobody volunteers for, because it looks like giving up. It is the opposite. Carrying the result of your work is a job you were never actually strong enough to hold, and most exhausted people are exhausted from carrying it rather than from the work itself. The permission to stop is what makes it possible to continue.

One question sits underneath all three, and it is the only one worth taking away.

What are you still holding that you were never meant to own?
