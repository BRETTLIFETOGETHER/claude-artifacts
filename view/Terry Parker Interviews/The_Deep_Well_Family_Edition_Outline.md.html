Chapter-by-chapter allegory outline for the family edition

The Deep Well

A Family Story About Everything They Tried to Keep

Family edition, allegory form. Chapter outline. Maps to the six-release master. Same session and day counts as the advisor and secular editions.

Why allegory here and realism there

The advisor book can be literal because an advisor recognizes his own office. A family cannot. The moment a family story names a number, every reader compares it to their own and finds a reason the book is not about them. Too much and it is a story about rich people. Too little and it is quaint.

Water solves that. Everyone needs it, nobody can live without it, and the amount is never the point. A well is inherited, contested, and finite, and the only real question about one is who gets to draw from it. That is the family question with the money taken out.

Narrator: the second son. Not the heir. The one who watched, who was not consulted, and who is now old enough to have children of his own and has just noticed his own hand on the rope. He is telling this to them.

That choice matters. A father narrating his own release becomes self-congratulation on page one. A son narrating it can love his father and see him clearly at the same time, which is the exact posture the reader needs and rarely has.

Prologue · The Rope in My Hand

Present day. The narrator, now the age his father was, standing at the well with his own grown children behind him, aware that he has just said something his father used to say and that he said it in the same voice.

Establishes the frame and the stakes. Ends before the memory begins.

Roughly 800 words.

Chapter One · The First Thirty Feet

Release: ownership. Cost: the belief that he built it.

The father, in his prime, on the day a neighbor asks permission to draw. He gives it, generously, and the narrator notices that the giving and the permission are the same act, and that his father enjoys both.

The turn comes from the grandmother, who mentions offhand that the well was already sixty feet down when her husband found it, and that nobody knows who dug the first thirty. The father dismisses it. The narrator does not.

The image: a man standing on top of work he did not do, describing the view.

Where it lands for a family: the founder's assumption that the enterprise began with him, and what it does to a family organized around that belief. The wife who worked, the parents who paid, the market that turned, the people who stayed.

Scripture anchor for the companion: Deuteronomy 8:17-18.

Chapter Two · He Knew Where the Water Was

Release: knowledge. Cost: being the only one who knows.

The father can read ground. He walks a field and knows. It is the thing he is most proud of and the thing he has never explained, and when the narrator asks how he does it, the father says you will know when you are older, which both of them understand to mean something else.

Midway, a hired man asks the same question and gets a real answer, because the hired man is not a threat. The narrator sees this happen and does not say anything for thirty years.

The image: a father teaching a stranger what he will not teach his son.

Where it lands: most families transfer assets and withhold the wisdom that produced them, then are astonished at the result. The reason is rarely selfishness. It is that the knowing is the last thing the father has that the children still need.

Scripture anchor: Deuteronomy 6:6-9.

Chapter Three · Amos Carried the Water

Release: people. Cost: the comfort of the arrangement.

Amos has carried water for the family for twenty-two years, and the narrator has never once wondered where Amos gets his own. His mother notices before anyone else that Amos's youngest is sick and that the walk is long.

The father's response is the chapter, and it is not a raise. It is something specific, inconvenient, and slightly embarrassing to everyone, and it changes the household in ways nobody attributes to it for a decade.

This is the chapter where the father is at his best, and putting it third is deliberate. The reader has to love him before chapter four.

The image: the family that owns the water discovering they have never asked who carries it.

Where it lands: how a family treats the people who serve it is the single clearest message children receive about what money is for. The assistant, the foreman, the caregiver, the in-law who is not quite in.

Scripture anchor: Job 31:13-15.

Chapter Four · What He Never Said

Release: control. Cost: leverage.

The hinge, and the darkest chapter.

The well is failing and the father knows. He says nothing for two years. During those two years he is more generous than he has ever been, more decisive, more consulted, and more beloved, and the narrator later understands that all of it was purchased with the silence.

The confrontation, when it comes, is not about the water. It is the eldest son saying the true thing: you did not tell us because while we did not know, you were the only one who could decide.

The father's defense is the one every reader has used. They were not ready. It would have frightened them. There was nothing they could have done.

The image: a man protecting his family from information that would have made them useful.

Where it lands: telling your children the truth about the money costs you leverage, and leverage is what most founders are actually protecting when they say the children are not ready. This is the family version of the advisor's compensation conflict, and it is sharper, because nobody is even pretending the incentive is financial.

Scripture anchor: Ephesians 4:15.

Chapter Five · Before the Dry Year

Release: timing. Cost: the comfort of the present.

The drought is coming and everyone can see the signs. The father wants to wait until the need is undeniable, partly from prudence and mostly because deciding now means deciding while there is still something to lose.

The narrator's wife, who has been in the family eleven years and is the only adult in it not related by blood, says the thing that turns the chapter: you are not waiting for information, you are waiting for the decision to become easy, and it never will.

They dig in the good year. It is expensive, it is premature, it looks foolish to the neighbors, and it is the reason the family survives what comes.

The image: breaking ground in a season of rain.

Where it lands: the eighteen months before a sale, an inheritance, or a death. The window is always open when nobody feels urgency and closed by the time everyone does.

Scripture anchor: Proverbs 22:3.

Chapter Six · Dig Your Own

Release: outcome. Cost: security.

The father is old. The narrator expects the well to be divided, and has privately worked out how, and has an argument prepared about fairness that he has been rehearsing for years.

Instead the father takes them to unfamiliar ground and tells them to find their own water. He will advise. He will not decide, he will not fund it, and he will not tell them where to dig, and when the eldest asks what happens if they choose wrong, he says that then they will have chosen wrong, and that he will still be here.

It is the least generous-sounding thing he ever does and it is the most generous thing he ever does, and the narrator does not understand that for a long time.

The title of the family edition sits in this chapter. Advise, do not control. The old well stays open to anyone who asks. It simply stops being the thing that holds the family together, and the family holds together anyway, which is the discovery.

The image: a father standing at the edge of a field, watching, saying nothing, staying.

Where it lands: succession, inheritance, and the difference between an heir and a dependent. The discipline required to release money is the same discipline required to hand a child real authority, and a founder who cannot do the second will not manage the first.

Scripture anchor: Genesis 26:22.

Epilogue · Room

Back to the present. The narrator's own children, his own hand on the rope, and the sentence he almost said.

He does not resolve it. He puts the bucket down and asks them a question instead, and the book ends on the question rather than the answer, because the reader is holding a rope too.

The Model · Six Things a Family Holds

The teaching section, set after the story. Names the pattern plainly for the reader who wants it stated.

What we own and did not build. What we know and have not taught. Who serves us and goes unseen. What we know and have not said. What we can decide now and are postponing. And who decides when we are gone.

One question underneath, the same one carried across every edition: what are you still holding that you were never meant to own?

Afterword · The Well Was Real

Short. The six principles came from three men in Atlanta in the nineteen seventies, and here is who they were.

Same structure as the advisor edition's afterword, and the same dial: Terry decides how much of his name appears on it.

Build notes

Six chapters, roughly 3,500 words each, plus prologue, epilogue, model, and afterword. Twenty-four to twenty-six thousand words. Longer than the advisor fable because a family reads together and slower.

The forty-day companion runs on identical numbering to the advisor and secular editions, seven-seven-seven-seven-six-six, with every day ending in a family exercise rather than an individual one.

Chapter four is the one to write first. If it works, the book works. If it reads as a fable about a wise father, the book has failed and should be restructured before anything else is drafted.

Open decision: whether the father is named. Unnamed is more allegorical and harder to sustain across six chapters of dialogue. I would name him something plain and never name the family, so the reader supplies their own.
