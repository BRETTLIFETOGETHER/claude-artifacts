The Deep Well

Chapter Three

Amos Carried the Water

I gave you the short version of Amos once, at a table, with people listening, and the short version made your grandfather sound generous. He was generous. But I told it badly, and I told it in a way that let all of you off, and I am going to tell it properly now because the whole rest of this depends on it.

Amos came up the lane at a quarter past five, six mornings a week, for twenty-two years.

He was a small man with a bad shoulder from something that happened before I was born and that nobody ever explained to me. He carried on a yoke, two vessels, and he made four trips before the household woke and two more in the afternoon, and in the summer he made eight. He had a way of setting a full vessel down on the flags without a sound, which I have tried to do as a grown man and cannot.

He knew every one of us by our feet. He would be in the back passage with his back to the door and he would say good morning and my name without turning around, and it was not a trick, it was twenty-two years.

Now here is the sentence I want you to sit with, and I am not going to dress it up.

I was seventeen years old before it occurred to me that Amos also needed water.

Not once. In seventeen years, watching a man carry water into my house every morning of my life, it never entered my head to wonder where he got his own, or how far he walked for it, or who carried it. I did not decide not to wonder. There was no decision. He was part of the architecture of the morning, like the flags in the passage, and you do not ask where the flags go at night.

I am telling you that plainly because I want you to notice that I was not a cruel boy. I was a perfectly ordinary boy in a household that was good to people, and the not-wondering cost me nothing and required nothing, and it went on for seventeen years.

It was my mother who noticed, and she noticed in the way she noticed everything, which was sideways and about three weeks before anyone else.

Amos's youngest, Bram, had been sick since the turn of the year. Not dangerously, but persistently, the kind of thing that hangs on a small child through a wet spring and wears out a household. My mother knew this because she asked, which is a thing my mother did and the rest of us did not.

And she worked out, over some weeks, without ever being told, that Amos's wife had been walking to the low spring for their own water since Bram got sick, because Amos was leaving at half past four now instead of five to be at our gate on time, and she was doing it with the boy on her hip because there was nobody to leave him with.

The low spring was a mile and a bit, and the last part of it was a climb.

My mother said this to my father in the kitchen on an evening in April with two of us in the room, and she said it in about four sentences without any weight on them, which was her way of putting something down in front of a man and letting him pick it up.

I was seventeen and I had an idea immediately, and I said it out loud, because I was seventeen.

I said we should pay him more.

My father did not answer me for long enough that I understood I had said something stupid without yet understanding what.

Then he said, "More would be fair."

And I said, well, then.

And he said, "Fair is what I owe him. I have been fair to Amos for twenty-two years and his wife is carrying water up a hill with a sick child."

I did not have anything to say to that. I have had thirty years to think of something and I still do not.

He said one more thing, and it is the sentence I have carried longest. He said, "If I give him more money, he will decide what to do with it, and he will decide badly, because he will decide the way a proud man decides. He will put it toward the boy or the roof or his brother's debt and he will not put it toward himself, and in a year his wife will still be on that hill."

What my father did was survey the ridge.

He did it quietly, over about two months, with a man from the far side of the district whom none of us knew, and he did it in the evenings, and when Hollis asked what he was doing on the ridge he said he was looking at the ridge.

There was water under it. Not much. Not enough for a farm and nowhere near enough for stock, but enough for a household, at about forty feet, on the eastern shoulder, which was a nine-minute walk from Amos's door.

He hired the digging out of his own pocket, and it took most of a summer, and it cost him something I did not learn the size of until after he was dead, at which point I sat down on a step and stayed there a while.

And when it was finished he did not keep it.

He deeded it. He had it drawn up properly, the well and the ground it stood on and a right of way to it, in Amos's name, and he did it in the way you would do it if you were slightly ashamed of yourself, which I think he was.

The day he told him was the most uncomfortable morning I have ever spent in that house.

My father was not good at this. He was good at deciding and terrible at explaining, and he had not slept, and he told Amos in the back passage at twenty past five with the yoke still on the man's shoulders, and he got about two thirds of the way through it and then stopped and just handed him the paper.

Amos did not say thank you.

I want you to understand that I have thought about that for a very long time and I have stopped believing it was ingratitude. Amos stood there in the passage with a yoke on and a piece of paper in his hand and he said, after a while, "Twenty-two years."

And my father said yes.

And Amos said, "And nobody asked."

He was not angry. He said it the way a man says a thing he has known for two decades and has never had an occasion to say out loud. Then he set the vessels down without a sound and went home, and he did not come the next morning, and my mother would not let anyone go after him, and he came back the morning after that.

Here is what it did to our household, and none of us saw it at the time.

Amos stopped coming six days a week. There was no discussion about it. It went to three, then to two, and within a year he came when there was heavy work and not otherwise, and he was paid the same, which my father never once mentioned to anybody.

Which meant we carried our own water.

Nell was six and she carried a small vessel with both arms in front of her like a cat. Hollis, who was twenty and had opinions, resented it audibly for about eight months. I resented it silently for longer than that, and I want to be honest with you and say that some mornings I stood at that well in the cold and thought unkind things about a man with a bad shoulder who now had his own water.

My mother said nothing about any of it. She simply went first, every morning, before any of us were up, and that was the end of the argument.

Ten years later, when the thing happened that I will tell you about next, this household knew how to draw its own water and knew what the rope felt like and knew the notch on the stone. Every one of us. That was not a plan. Nobody arranged it. But it is the reason we were not helpless, and it came out of a summer's digging on a ridge for a man who was not family.

And Bram, who was three years old and sick on his mother's hip, grew up nine minutes from clean water and grew up strong, and thirty years on he is the man who put down the second shaft with your uncle Hollis, and he would not take payment for it, and Hollis did not insult him by insisting.

Now I have to give you the other half, and you are not going to like it, and neither did I.

My father never asked Amos.

He never asked him what he needed. He never asked him whether he wanted a well on the eastern shoulder or would rather have had the money or would rather have been asked. He noticed, or rather my mother noticed and he was told, and then he went away by himself for two months and came back with a finished thing and put it in the man's hands in a back passage at twenty past five.

It was magnificent and it was unilateral, and those were not two separate facts about my father. They were one fact.

And nobody in that house saw the danger in it, because when a man decides alone and decides beautifully, you do not go looking for the flaw in the deciding. You are too busy being grateful.

He also did not ask my mother.

She is the one who found it, she is the one who put it on the table, and she learned what he had done at roughly the same time Amos did. I did not think about that for forty years. She did. She thought about it the whole time, and she said so exactly once, in a kitchen, ten years later, and by then it was about something much worse.

So that is Amos, properly told.

Do not put your grandfather up on a shelf. He does not fit up there and it is no honor to him. He was a man who looked at somebody who had been invisible for twenty-two years and then spent a summer and a great deal of money on him and gave the whole thing away and was embarrassed about it.

He was also a man who, having seen the need, went and solved it without ever once turning to the person in front of him and saying: tell me what you need.

Both. At the same time. Out of the same root.

The Translation

Every household has an Amos, and the defining feature is not that he is treated badly. It is that he is not wondered about.

The assistant who has been outside the office for eleven years. The woman who cleans. The foreman. The caregiver who knows more about your mother's last two years than you do. The in-law who is at every table and in none of the decisions. These people are not mistreated in most families. They are simply part of the architecture, and nobody asks where the flags go at night.

Children learn what money is for by watching how a household treats the people who serve it, and they learn it far earlier and far more completely than from anything said to them directly. It is the most reliable transmission mechanism in a family and almost nobody uses it deliberately.

Job, defending his life, does not begin with his charitable giving. He begins with his staff. If I have denied justice to any of my servants, whether male or female, when they had a grievance against me, what will I do when God confronts me? What shall I answer when called to account? Did not he who made me in the womb make them? Did not the same one form us both within our mothers? That is the standard, and notice it is not a standard about wages. It is a standard about whether their grievance had anywhere to go.

Two distinctions from this chapter are worth carrying.

Fair is the floor. A raise answers the question of what a person is worth. It does not answer the question of what they need, and it does not require you to know a single thing about them. That is precisely why it is the instrument most of us reach for. It discharges the obligation without incurring the relationship. There is nothing wrong with paying people well, and paying people well is not the same as seeing them.

And the harder one: doing good for someone is not the same as consulting them. The father in this chapter got the answer right. He got it right without ever asking the question, and he got it right in a way that told a man he had been carried for two months' worth of decisions he was not part of. Amos's response was not ingratitude. It was the accurate observation that twenty-two years had passed and nobody had asked.

Hold those two together, because the second one is the seed of everything that goes wrong later. A man who decides alone and decides magnificently is very hard to correct, and a household that has been well served by his solitary judgment will not correct him. The generosity buys the silence. Nobody intends that and it happens anyway.

There is one more thing in this chapter that only shows up ten years later. What the father gave away, he no longer had. That well on the eastern shoulder belonged to Amos, in writing, and when this family needed margin, the margin was gone because it had been given.

I do not know how to make that come out tidy and I am not going to try. It was right and it was expensive and the expense arrived a decade after the decision, which is how these things generally work.

The Family Exercise

Write down the names of the people who serve this household and are not in it. Everyone in the room writes their own list, without conferring.

Compare the lists. The gaps between them are the point.

Then, for one name that appears on every list, answer three things: how long have they been with us, what happened in their life this year, and what do they need that we have never asked about.

If you cannot answer the second question, that is the finding.

Three Questions

Who has been part of the architecture of my life for so long that I have stopped seeing them?

When I have helped someone recently, did I ask them what they needed, or did I decide and deliver?

What have my children learned about money from watching how this household treats the people who work in it, and would I be willing to hear their honest answer?
