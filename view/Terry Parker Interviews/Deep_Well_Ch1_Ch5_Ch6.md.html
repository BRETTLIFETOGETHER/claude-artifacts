The Deep Well

Chapter One

The First Thirty Feet

The summer I was twelve, Dunmore came up the lane to ask for water, and I watched my father give it to him, and I have thought about that afternoon more than about almost anything else in my life.

Dunmore's own supply had gone brackish. He was not a man who asked for things. He stood at the gate with his hat in his hands and did the whole speech he had clearly worked out on the walk over, about how it would only be until autumn and how his boys would come and draw it themselves and not trouble anyone.

My father let him get all the way to the end of it.

Then he said, "Dunmore. Take what you need."

And Dunmore's face did something, and my father put a hand on his shoulder, and there was a moment there that I did not have language for at twelve and would not have language for until I was past forty.

Because I was watching two things happen at once and I could only see one of them.

The one I saw was generosity, and it was real, and I am not going to take it away from him.

The one I did not see was that my father enjoyed it. Not smugly. He was not a smug man. But there was a straightening in him that afternoon, a settling into something, and it came from the fact that the water was his to give and Dunmore's to receive, and that this had been established in front of a boy at a gate on a summer afternoon.

Every family has an arrangement like that somewhere, and the people inside it almost never see it, because from the inside it just looks like kindness.

He called it his well.

Not aggressively. He would say I sank that well, or we would not have got through the dry years if I had not put that shaft down, and nobody in the house ever thought twice about it, including me, for about a decade.

He had earned the right to say it as far as any of us knew. He had deepened it in his thirties, which was a real piece of work and nearly killed a hired man. He had cut the notch in the stone at the rim, the shallow one about the width of a thumb, so a man could stand in the dark and know by feel how much rope he had let out. He had built the wall around it and the cover and the frame. He maintained it obsessively.

And when he said my well, what he meant, and what all of us heard, was: I am the reason this family has water.

My grandmother took it apart in about nine words, on a bench, shelling something, without looking up.

I was fifteen by then. I had said something to her about the well and about my father sinking it, repeating the household line the way you repeat a household line, and she made a small noise.

I asked her what.

She said, "It was down sixty feet when your grandfather found it."

I said that was not right.

She said, "It was a stone ring and a lot of bramble and he cleared it in the first spring we were here. He put another twenty in it. Your father put the rest." She moved something from one bowl to another. "Nobody knows who dug the first thirty. Somebody did."

I sat there.

She said, and this is the part that got in under my ribs and stayed there, "It was here before us and it will be here after, and every man who has ever owned it has thought he made it."

I made the mistake of taking that to my father.

I was fifteen, so I did not take it to him gently. I took it to him the way a fifteen-year-old takes a correction to his father, which is to say I presented it as a discovery, and I presented it at supper.

He said, "Your grandmother is eighty-one."

I said she seemed clear about it.

He said the stone ring was nothing, that anyone could clear brambles, that the shaft as it stood was his work and his father's and that whoever scratched a hole in that ground four hundred years ago had not put down a foot of what was actually load-bearing.

All of which was true.

And then he said, "It does not matter who started it. It matters who keeps it."

That is not a bad sentence. I have used it. He said it to close a conversation he did not want to be in, and I have said it for the same reason, and both times it worked, and both times it was a way of stepping around something rather than through it.

Because he did not say she was wrong. He said it did not matter.

Here is what I understood much later about why it mattered.

A man who believes he made the thing has a particular relationship to everyone who uses it. He is not a steward of it. He is its author, and the people who drink from it are receiving something from him personally, and their gratitude is owed to him personally, and his judgment about it is final because it is his.

Change one word and the whole structure moves. A man who believes he inherited the thing and improved it is holding something that came from somewhere and is going somewhere, and he is one span in a longer bridge, and his judgment is not final because the thing was never fully his to begin with.

Both men can be generous. Dunmore got his water either way.

But only one of them can be argued with.

There is something else in that story that I did not see until I was old, and my mother saw it the whole time.

My grandmother said your grandfather put another twenty feet in it. She said your father put the rest.

She did not say what she put in it.

She came to that place as a young wife and buried two children in that ground and kept a household through the year the roof went and the year after that, and when I was fifteen she was the only living person who knew the actual history of the water, and she was on a bench with a bowl, and nobody was writing any of it down. Including me. I was fifteen and I took her nine words and used them to win an argument at supper and I never once went back and asked her the rest.

She died the following winter.

I do not know who dug the first thirty feet. I could have known nearly everything else and I did not ask.

The Translation

Nearly every family enterprise carries a founding story with one man in it, and nearly every one of those stories is missing people.

The wife who worked so the business could survive its third year. The father-in-law who cosigned. The parents who paid for the education. The partner who left early and quietly. The market that turned in exactly the right quarter. The employees who stayed through the bad stretch for reasons that had nothing to do with compensation. A founder rarely denies any of this if you ask him directly. He simply does not include it when he tells the story, and the story is what the children hear four hundred times.

The consequence is not a matter of fairness. It is a matter of authority.

You may say to yourself, "My power and the strength of my hands have produced this wealth for me." But remember the Lord your God, for it is he who gives you the ability to produce wealth. Moses says that to people about to become prosperous, and notice the tense. He is not warning them about greed. He is warning them about a sentence they will say to themselves later, when things have gone well, and he is warning them because that sentence changes what kind of man is holding the money.

A founder who authored it cannot be corrected. A founder who received it and improved it can. That is the entire difference, and it shows up thirty years later in whether the family can talk to him.

Two practical things follow.

The founding story is a document and it should be assembled while the witnesses are alive. Not a tribute video. An actual account, with the people in it who are missing from the current version, gathered from the people who remember. Most families do this in the two weeks after a funeral, from memory, badly. My grandmother knew the history of that water and I used nine words of it to win an argument at supper, and then she died.

And there is a test for whether a family is talking about ownership honestly. Ask each person to name three people, other than the founder, without whom this would not exist. If the founder cannot produce three, that is the finding, and it is not a character flaw. It is simply a man who has told the short version so many times that he has come to believe it, which is what happens to all of us.

The Family Exercise

Everyone writes, separately: name three people without whom this family would not have what it has. The founder writes too.

Then compare. Read them aloud in order of age, youngest first.

Then one further question for the founder alone, in front of everyone: what did you receive that you did not build?

Three Questions

When I tell the story of how we got here, who is in it, and who is missing?

Do I hold this as its author or as one span in a longer bridge, and how would the people around me answer that about me?

Who is still living who knows something about our history that I have never asked them?

Chapter Five

Before the Dry Year

Three years after the kitchen, the signs came, and everybody in that house could read them, because by then everybody in that house had been taught to.

That was the one good thing to come out of the worst night of our lives. My father showed Hollis the knots on the rope at dawn, and within a month he was showing all of us, and within a year Nell at eighteen could read that rope better than I could. He never announced a change of policy. He was not capable of that. He just stopped being the only one who knew things, one thing at a time, and it was the closest to an apology he ever came.

So when the water dropped again in the spring I was thirty-four, six people knew it in the same week.

What we could not agree on was what to do, and the argument went on for most of a year.

My father, who was old now and had stopped pretending otherwise, wanted to wait.

He had reasons and again they were good ones. The drop was within the range of an ordinary dry spring. Sinking a second shaft on unproven ground was enormously expensive and we did not have it lying about, and the last time anyone in the district had done it speculatively they had put a hole in the ground for eleven weeks and found clay. And there was the ridge, which he did not mention, and which none of us mentioned, and which was Amos's, in writing, because he had given it away twenty years before.

That is worth stopping on. The margin we would have used was gone. It was gone because of the best thing my father ever did, and it had been gone for two decades, and nobody had ever counted it as a cost because it had never come due.

Hollis wanted to dig immediately. Hollis was forty-one and had been carrying the place for six years and had a particular horror of ever being on the wrong side of a fact again.

I wanted somebody else to decide, which is what I usually wanted.

It was Mira who ended it, at a table, in October.

She had been in that family eleven years by then. She was not in the kitchen the night it all came out, three years earlier, because she was a daughter-in-law and it had not occurred to anyone to call her in, and she had heard it from me afterward, upstairs, in pieces. She has never once mentioned that. She did not have to. My mother had mentioned it for her, in a sentence, and I had spent three years understanding it.

The argument had gone around twice. My father was explaining, patiently, for the third time, that we did not yet have enough information.

And Mira said, "You are not waiting for information."

Everyone looked at her.

She said, "You have all the information you are going to get. Nobody ever gets more than this. You are waiting for the decision to become easy, and it does not become easy, it only becomes late."

My father did not answer her. But he did the thing he did when something had gone in, which was to go very still and look at the middle of the table.

Then she said the other half, and this is the half that actually moved him.

"If you dig now, it is a gift. If you dig when the well fails, it is a rescue. Your children will remember which one it was, and so will you."

We dug in a good year, and it was terrible.

It looked ridiculous. It rained in November. The well came back up nearly to where it had been the previous spring, and the district decided we had lost our nerve, and one man said something at market that Hollis did not tell me about for six years.

The first shaft found nothing. Fifty-one feet, seven weeks, and dry sand, and I stood at the top of that hole on the last morning and understood that we had spent an enormous amount of money on a hole.

My father took that better than anybody. He had a long look at the spoil and said, "Well. Now we know where it isn't," and went in for his lunch.

The second shaft, in the lower ground, at thirty-eight feet, came in.

The dry year came four years later and it lasted two and it took the old well down to a depth where you could hear the bucket land wrong.

I do not need to tell you what happened, because you are sitting in a house that is still here.

What I want to tell you instead is what it was like to have already done it. There was no crisis. There was no meeting. There was no bad night. There was a spring where the old well got shallow and a family that walked forty yards further, and the thing that had been the great and expensive folly of our lives turned out to be a Tuesday.

That is what acting early feels like from the inside. It does not feel like wisdom. It feels like nothing happened.

Which is why almost nobody does it.

The Translation

There is a window in every family's life when a decision is still voluntary, and it closes long before the need arrives, and it closes quietly.

Before the sale. Before the diagnosis is certain. Before the parent needs care. Before the founder stops being able to explain what he means. In each case there is a stretch, usually eighteen months to five years, when the family could decide something together, generously, without pressure, and could take a year to do it well.

Almost no family uses that window, and the reason is not ignorance. Every family in that stretch can see what is coming. The reason is that during the window the decision costs something and afterward it does not, because afterward there is no decision left to make.

The prudent see danger and take refuge, but the simple keep going and pay the penalty. Notice that Proverbs does not say the simple could not see it. It distinguishes between seeing and taking refuge, which are different acts, separated by a cost.

Three things from this chapter are worth taking.

The distinction between a gift and a rescue. The same money, the same act, and two entirely different meanings, and the only variable is when. A family that acts early is a family that chose. A family that acts late is a family that was cornered. Your children can tell the difference and they will file it accordingly, and so will you.

The person who can say it is often the one who married in. In-laws see the family clearly because they arrived as adults and were never taught not to look. They also usually have no standing, which is why what they see stays unsaid. If your family has been circling something for years, the person most likely to be able to name it is the one who has been in the room for a decade and has never been asked a direct question. Ask one.

The dry hole is part of the price. Fifty-one feet and seven weeks and nothing. Acting early means acting on incomplete information, which means sometimes being wrong, in public, expensively. A family that requires certainty before it will move has simply chosen to move late and call it prudence.

And the last thing, which is the hardest to teach. When it works, it will not feel like anything. Nobody will thank you. The disaster you prevented will not have a name. That is the actual experience of having done it right, and it is why the reward for acting early has to be internal, because there will not be an external one.

The Family Exercise

Name the thing this family is currently waiting on. Everyone writes it down separately first, because the exercise is worthless if the founder names it and everyone agrees.

Then, together, answer one question: what specifically are we waiting for? Write the answer as an event, not a feeling. If nobody can name an event, you are waiting for the decision to become easy.

Then set a date. Not for the decision. For the meeting at which the decision gets made. Put it on a calendar in the room before anyone leaves it.

Three Questions

What am I waiting on, and what exactly would have to happen for me to stop waiting?

If we do this now, is it a gift? If we do it when we have to, what is it?

Who married into this family and can see us clearly, and when did I last ask them a direct question?

Chapter Six

Dig Your Own

My father was eighty-one when he called the three of us out to the far ground, and I want you to know exactly what I was carrying when I walked out there.

I had an argument prepared. I had been working on it for about nine years.

It concerned the division of the water. Hollis had run the place since his forties and would inherit the house, and I had made a life elsewhere and Nell had married and come back, and there was a version of the arithmetic that was fair and a version that was traditional and they were not the same version, and I had rehearsed mine in the way you rehearse a thing you have no intention of ever saying out loud.

I assumed we were walking out there to be told.

He took us to ground I did not know. Rough stuff on the far side, no good for anything, that nobody in our family had ever done anything with.

He stood in the middle of it and he said, "There is water under here somewhere. I do not know where."

Nobody said anything.

He said, "I am not going to divide the well. Anyone who wants water from it will have it, for as long as it lasts, and that includes the three of you and it includes the Dunmores and anyone else who asks. It stays open."

Then he said the part I have never got over.

"But you are going to find your own. I will tell you what I know. I will walk this ground with you as many times as you want. I am not going to tell you where to dig, I am not going to pay for it, and I am not going to decide anything."

Hollis said, "What if we choose wrong?"

And my father said, "Then you will have chosen wrong."

He let that sit for a second. Then he said, "And I will still be here."

I want to be honest with you about how that landed, because if I make it sound like a beautiful moment I will have lied to you and taught you nothing.

I was furious.

It felt like being dismissed. I had spent nine years working out what was fair and he had made the entire question irrelevant in about forty seconds, and it felt to me, standing on that rough ground at thirty-nine years old, like a man declining to do the last thing a father is for.

Hollis started walking the ground that week.

Nell did something none of us expected, which was to take over the old well. Not to own it. To keep it. She rebuilt the wall, she kept the cover sound, she kept it open, and for the next thirty years anyone in that district who needed water knew whose gate to come to, and it was hers, and she was the only one of the three of us who understood immediately what our father had actually done.

And I did nothing for six years.

I dug at forty-five. I dug because Mira asked me one evening why we were still hauling from a well on my brother's land when we had a place of our own and I had two children who had never once drawn their own water, and she did not say anything else, because she never needed to say anything else.

I found it at fifty-two feet, in the second place I tried, and my father was ninety and could not walk out to see it. So I brought him a cup of it.

He drank it and he did not say anything for a while.

Then he said, "Where."

I told him where and he nodded slowly and said, "I would not have looked there."

That is the only thing he ever said about my well and it is the best thing anyone has ever said to me.

Bram put down the second shaft with Hollis. He was thirty-three by then, a man with hands like a stone wall, born nine minutes from clean water on the eastern shoulder of a ridge because my father spent a summer and a great deal of money on a household that was not his.

Hollis tried to pay him. Bram said no. Hollis, to his great credit, did not insult him by insisting.

I do not think my father ever connected those two events. I think he dug a well for Amos in one decade and got a shaft dug for his son in another and never once put them side by side, and I am not certain the connection is even the point. Things come back or they do not. He did not do it in order to get anything.

But it came back.

He died in the spring. Nell was with him.

The last thing he asked her to do was to check the rope.

I have thought about that a great deal and I do not think it was a metaphor. I think he wanted to know where the water was. He was interested in it right up to the end, the way he had been interested in it his whole life.

But I have taken it as a metaphor anyway, because I am the one still standing here holding it.

There is a place in the old accounts where a man digs a well and the local people quarrel with him over it, so he moves on and digs another one, and they quarrel about that one too. So he moves again. And the third time nobody comes, and he digs, and he names the place Room, and he says: now the Lord has given us room, and we will flourish in the land.

I did not understand for most of my life why that was in there. It seemed like a small thing to record.

It is not a story about water. It is a story about a man who stopped fighting for a well and went and made another one, and about the fact that there is more water in the ground than anyone thinks, and about how much room there turns out to be once somebody is willing to walk.

The Translation

This is the release the whole book has been walking toward, and it is the one that sounds least like generosity.

The founder in this chapter does four things and only the first is warm. He keeps the old well open to everyone. Then he declines to divide it, declines to fund what comes next, declines to say where to dig, and declines to decide. To three adult children who have spent decades organizing their expectations around what he would eventually hand over, it feels like a refusal.

It is the opposite. He is giving them the one thing he cannot give and also hand over: the experience of having found water themselves.

Isaac moved on from there and dug another well, and no one quarreled over it. He named it Rehoboth, saying, "Now the Lord has given us room and we will flourish in the land."

Three distinctions matter here.

Advise and control are different acts, and the difference is where the decision sits. He offers everything he knows and reserves the decision to them. Most founders reverse it. They keep the knowledge and hand down the decision, which produces children who can execute and cannot judge.

Access is not ownership. The old well stays open and stops being the thing that holds the family together, and the family holds together anyway. That is the discovery, and no founder believes it in advance. Every one of them privately suspects that the money is the reason everyone comes, and the only way to find out is to stop using it that way.

Releasing is not abdicating, and the whole thing turns on one sentence. Then you will have chosen wrong. And I will still be here. Remove the second half and it is abandonment. The second half is what makes the first half survivable, and it is also expensive, because it commits him to watching them fail without intervening and staying anyway.

Two more things this chapter should teach a family reading it.

Not everyone digs, and that is not failure. One took over the old well and kept it open for a district for thirty years, and she understood the whole thing before either of her brothers did. A family that only honors the children who build something new will lose the ones who keep what exists.

And it takes years. Six, in the narrator's case, spent resentful. A founder who releases and then watches for eighteen months and concludes it did not work has simply not waited long enough. The horizon on this is a decade.

The Family Exercise

For the founder, alone, in writing: name one decision currently yours that could become someone else's within twelve months. Not a task. A decision, with real consequences, that you would not be able to reverse.

Then name the date it transfers.

For everyone else: name one thing you have been waiting to be given, and answer honestly whether you have been waiting because you cannot proceed without it or because being given it would mean something.

Then read both lists aloud.

Three Questions

What am I holding that I have told myself I am holding for their sake?

If I stopped being the source, would they still come? What would it cost me to find out?

Who in this family is keeping something rather than building something, and have I ever told them that it counts?
