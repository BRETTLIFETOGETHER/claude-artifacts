The Deep Well Toolbox

Experiential Tools, Capture Protocol, and Journey Catalog

Status: proposed. Third-party resources are marked and are not licensed.

Part One · The Ten, Built Out

Each tool below carries four things: what happens, facilitator notes, what gets captured, and why it works. The capture line matters as much as the exercise, because a family meeting that changes something and records nothing has to start over next year.

1 · The Rope

What happens. A physical length of rope, knotted at intervals, sits on the table. Whoever holds it speaks. When they finish they hand it to someone, and they choose who, and they cannot hand it back to the person who gave it to them.

Facilitator notes. Do not explain the symbolism. The book has done that and explaining it again makes it a gimmick. Start with the youngest person holding it. Watch what happens the first time the founder has to physically give it away, because most founders will keep talking after they have handed it over and the room will notice before he does. Do not correct him. Let the rope do it. If the meeting gets heated, put the rope down in the center and call five minutes.

Capture. Record the audio. Note the handoff order, because who a person passes to reveals the alliance structure of a family faster than any conversation about it.

Why. A founder who has to release an object in order to stop talking learns something in his hands that will not reach him through argument. It also solves the ordinary problem of family meetings, which is that the same two people talk for ninety minutes.

2 · The Three Names

What happens. Everyone writes, privately, three people without whom this family would not have what it has. Read aloud youngest first. The founder writes too and reads last.

Facilitator notes. Youngest first is not politeness, it is the only sequence in which the youngest will say anything true. Give four minutes of silence before anyone speaks, and enforce it, because the first name is easy and the third is the one that matters. If the founder names only himself and two business partners, do not comment. Let the list stand next to his wife's list.

Capture. Photograph the lists. Every name becomes a node in the family record, and the ones that appear on multiple lists are your interview subjects for tool three.

Why. It removes the founder's exemption without anyone having to challenge him. The gaps between lists do the arguing.

3 · The Missing Witness

What happens. The family identifies the oldest living person who knows something about their history that nobody has recorded. Inside the meeting, they set a date, name who will conduct it, and name who will operate the camera.

Facilitator notes. Do not let this become a resolution. It has to leave the room with a date and two names on it. The most common witness is a mother or grandmother whose contribution has never been in the story, and the second most common is a long-tenured non-family employee. If the family cannot name anyone, ask who taught the founder to do what he does.

Capture. This is a capture tool. Video where possible, audio at minimum, transcribed. Ten questions, one closing reflection, and a listen-for list: stories, defining moments, wounds and turning points, family phrases, unfinished conversations.

Why. Every family does this eventually, two weeks after a funeral, from memory, badly. The value of doing it early is not sentiment. It is that the witness can still be asked follow-up questions.

4 · The Failure Ledger

What happens. The founder writes three significant failures with operational detail. Not confessions. What he decided, what he was thinking, what he missed, what it cost. He reads one aloud. All three go into the family record.

Facilitator notes. The instruction is technical, not emotional, and you have to protect that. A founder who turns this into an apology has changed the exercise into something about him, and the room will comfort him and nothing will transfer. If he offers a failure that resolved well, ask for one that did not. Prepare him privately beforehand. This is the one tool that should never be sprung.

Capture. All three ledgers verbatim, tagged by decade and by domain. These become the most-used entries in the family record within five years.

Why. Most founders have transmitted every success and no failures, which leaves the next generation with no calibration for risk. They know things can work. They have no idea what it looks like when they don't.

5 · Teach One Thing

What happens. The founder teaches one real skill or judgment to one named person, in the room, in an hour. How he reads a deal, a person, a market, a field. Not values. Method.

Facilitator notes. Choose the person in advance and choose the skill in advance, or the hour evaporates into storytelling. Push for the unglamorous thing: how he decides whether to trust someone, how he knows when a number is wrong. Ask the learner to teach it back in five minutes at the end. The teach-back is where you find out whether anything moved.

Capture. Record it. This is the single highest-value asset in the entire family record, and it is the one that cannot be reconstructed after the founder is gone.

Why. Families transfer assets and withhold the judgment that produced them, then are surprised by the outcome. This is the antidote and it takes one hour.

6 · The Invisible List

What happens. Everyone independently names the people who serve this household and are not in it. Compare. Then take one name appearing on every list and answer two questions: what happened in their life this year, and what do they need that nobody has asked about.

Facilitator notes. The comparison is the exercise, so do not let anyone read their list before everyone has written. If the family cannot answer the first question about anyone, that is the finding and it should be named gently and once, then left alone.

Capture. The names, with tenure. Add a standing field to the family record for people who serve the family, because most families have no such list anywhere.

Why. Children learn what money is for by watching how a household treats the people who serve it. It is the most reliable transmission mechanism in a family and almost nobody uses it on purpose.

7 · The Sealed Envelope

What happens. Each person writes one thing they know that the others do not, and the real reason for the silence. Envelopes are sealed and stacked in the center. Nobody opens anything. The family then decides together whether they want them opened, now or at a later meeting. That decision is the exercise.

Facilitator notes. This is the tool that can damage a family and it should never be run at a first meeting or without a facilitator. Say the rule twice before anyone writes: nothing gets opened without unanimous consent. If one person says no, the answer is no, and that person is never identified. If the family votes to open, do it at a separate session with time protected afterward and nothing else on the agenda. If someone discloses something serious in the discussion, stop the exercise and deal with the disclosure.

Capture. Capture the decision, not the contents. Contents are never recorded, never photographed, and never discussed with the advisor.

Why. It makes the shape of a family's silence visible without exposing anyone. A stack of six envelopes on a table is an argument no one has to make.

8 · What Are We Waiting For

What happens. Everyone names the thing this family is currently waiting on, separately, before anyone speaks. Then together, the family writes the answer as an event rather than a feeling. Then sets a date for the meeting at which the decision gets made.

Facilitator notes. Enforce the event requirement. Not when we're ready, not when the market settles, not when the kids are more mature. An event has a date or a threshold. If nobody can name one, say the sentence plainly: we are waiting for this to become easy, and it does not become easy, it only becomes late.

Capture. The named event, the date, and the person accountable. This is the first entry in the family's decision log.

Why. Every family can see what is coming. Seeing and taking refuge are different acts, separated by a cost, and this tool makes the cost visible while it is still voluntary.

9 · The Handover

What happens. The founder names one decision, currently his, that transfers to a named person within twelve months, with a date, in front of everyone. Real, consequential, and not reversible.

Facilitator notes. Prepare him privately or it will not happen. Reject the first offer if it is symbolic, and it usually is. A committee seat is not a decision. Signing authority up to a threshold is. Ask one question: after this transfers, could they make a choice you would hate, and would it stand? If the answer is no, it is not a handover.

Capture. The decision, the recipient, the date, and one line on what happens if it goes badly. Reviewed at the next meeting as the first agenda item.

Why. Everything else in a family meeting is conversation. This is the only item that changes anything, and a family that has run tools one through eight and skips this one has had a very good weekend and altered nothing.

10 · Dig Your Own

What happens. Each member of the next generation names one thing they will build that is not the family enterprise, and states what they need, which may only be advice. The founder responds with what he knows and does not respond with what to do.

Facilitator notes. Brief the founder on the constraint before the session and give him a phrase to use when he wants to instruct: here is what I know, and it is your call. He will need it four or five times. If a proposal is genuinely bad, that is not your problem to fix in the room.

Capture. Each stated venture, what was requested, and what was offered. Revisit annually. The pattern over three years tells you more about a family's health than any assessment.

Why. It converts release from a principle into a Tuesday. It also surfaces something no founder expects, which is how many of his children have been quietly waiting for permission rather than resources.

Part Two · The Capture Protocol

Every tool above produces something. Without a protocol it produces a nice memory.

Three tiers of material.

Open record. Lists, names, decisions, dates, ventures, the failure ledger, the teach-one-thing recording, the missing witness interview. Shared across the family, stored permanently, indexed.

Restricted. Individual assessment responses, the founder's item, anything said in a one-to-one. Visible only to the person and, with written permission, the facilitator.

Never recorded. Sealed envelope contents. Anything disclosed in distress. Anything a participant asks to be off the record, which they may do at any time without explanation.

The digital family profile. Built from the open record, it holds the founding story with its corrected cast, the people who serve the family, the failure ledger, the teach-one-thing library, the decision log with dates and owners, the giving history and the where criteria, the next generation's ventures, and the interview archive. It grows by one meeting a year, which is enough.

The ethics rule, stated inside the tool where families will read it. Private spiritual and relational disclosure is never treated as sales intelligence. It is confidential, permission-based, and securely stored. An advisor who has facilitated one of these meetings knows things about a family that no advisor should ever act on commercially, and the moment a family suspects otherwise the entire instrument is dead, in that family and in every family they talk to.

Part Three · Fifteen More Tools

Ordered from lightest entry point to heaviest.

11 · The First Memory. Each person describes their earliest memory involving money, in three minutes. Purpose: surface the formative moment nobody has examined. Why: almost every adult money behavior traces to something that happened before age twelve, and families are astonished to learn they have been operating on five different childhoods.

12 · The Table Map. Each person draws the kitchen table of their childhood home. Who sat where, who spoke, what was never discussed. Purpose: make family communication patterns visible without discussing family communication. Why: it is disarming, it takes ten minutes, and the founder's drawing usually explains everything about the meeting you are currently in.

13 · The Number Nobody Says. Everyone privately writes their estimate of what the family is worth. Reveal the spread, never the individual guesses. Purpose: show the information gap in one number. Why: the spread is frequently enormous and it is the fastest possible demonstration of chapter four. Use only where the founder has agreed in advance and can handle it.

14 · The Enough Line. Each person writes their own number for enough, for their own household, and what they would do above it. Purpose: convert a vague virtue into arithmetic. Why: families discuss generosity endlessly and almost never discover that they disagree about sufficiency, which is the actual disagreement.

15 · The Values Card Sort. Physical cards, forced ranking to a top five individually, then a family convergence with real trading. Purpose: produce a values list somebody argued over. Why: every family values statement written by consensus in one sitting is unusable. Forced trading produces one that means something.

16 · Give It Away. Real money, a meaningful amount, divided equally among all members including teenagers. Each person allocates it and defends the choice to the family. Grants actually go out. Purpose: teach the where question by making people do it. Why: it is the most enjoyable tool in the box and the one children remember for thirty years.

17 · The Site Visit. The family goes together, in person, to one place it funds. No presentations, no gala. Purpose: move giving from spreadsheet to relationship. Why: families that visit give differently within a year, and no amount of teaching produces the same shift.

18 · The Shadow Day. One member of the next generation shadows the founder through a full working day, including the boring parts and the difficult conversation. Purpose: transfer judgment by proximity. Why: most heirs have seen the outcome of their parent's work and never the texture of it.

19 · The Reverse Interview. Children interview parents on camera using a fixed question set. Purpose: capture the founding story with the roles inverted. Why: parents answer their children differently than they answer a professional, and the recording outlives everyone in the room.

20 · The Empty Chair. A physical empty chair at the table. Who is not here, and what would they say about this decision. Purpose: introduce absent stakeholders. Why: the absent are usually the estranged sibling, the deceased parent, or the generation not yet born, and all three are shaping the meeting invisibly.

21 · The Twenty-Year Letter. Each person writes to a family member who will exist in twenty years and does not exist yet. Sealed, dated, stored. Purpose: lengthen the horizon. Why: people write things to the future they cannot say to the present, and the letters become the most treasured item in the family record.

22 · The Ask. Each person practices asking the family for something they actually want. Everyone, including the founder. Purpose: normalize asking. Why: in most wealthy families only one person is permitted to want things out loud, and that arrangement is corrosive and entirely unexamined.

23 · The One-Page Constitution. One page, one sitting, no lawyers. What we believe, how we decide, what we will not do. Purpose: produce a rough governing document fast. Why: families spend years and large fees producing beautiful documents nobody follows. A rough one written in ninety minutes gets used.

24 · The Obituary of the Family. Not an individual's. The family's, in fifty years. Purpose: force a corporate identity statement. Why: it is the only exercise that makes a family articulate what it is for, and the founder's version and the children's version are rarely the same document.

25 · The Annual Letter. The founder writes one page a year to the whole family: what happened, what he learned, what he got wrong. Purpose: build a rhythm rather than an event. Why: it is the cheapest tool here and over twenty years it produces an archive nothing else can match.

Part Five · Next Generation and Multigenerational

The central design rule: never run a rising generation program without a parallel founder track. The problem is almost never the next generation's competence. It is that they have no authority and no information, and no curriculum supplies either. Educate them without changing the founder and you produce capable adults with nothing to decide, which is worse than doing nothing, because now they know.

Paired cohorts. Rising gen and founders meet separately on the same weekends and together on the last morning. The founder track's only deliverable is one transferred decision. The rising gen's only deliverable is one venture that is not the family enterprise.

The cousin consortium. Third generation and beyond, where the founder is dead and the cousins barely know each other. Different problem entirely: not release, but whether a shared thing is worth keeping. The tool is a vote nobody is punished for.

Cross-family peer cohorts. Rising gen members from six unrelated families, together. Wealthy heirs will say things to peers they will never say to a parent or a paid advisor, and this is the format almost nobody offers because it requires trust across families.

Rites of passage by age. Thirteen, eighteen, twenty-five, thirty. A defined conversation, a defined disclosure, and a defined responsibility at each. Most families disclose everything at once at the reading of a will, which is the worst possible design.

Grandparent to grandchild, direct. Skipping a generation removes the authority problem entirely. A grandfather can teach a nineteen-year-old what he cannot teach his own son, and both of them know why.

Part Six · What Ships in the Toolbox

Physical. The rope. The values cards. Sealed envelopes. A hardbound family record book. Blank ledger cards. The empty chair marker.

Print. The book in its edition. The forty-day companion. Assessment booklets, one per person. The facilitator guide. The interview question sets by role.

Digital. Scored assessments and the spread grid. The family profile with its three-tier permissions. The decision log. The interview archive with transcripts. The annual review template.

Training. Facilitator certification, stackable onto the advisor track, with the sealed envelope and the handover as the two supervised modules, since those are the two where an untrained facilitator does real damage.
