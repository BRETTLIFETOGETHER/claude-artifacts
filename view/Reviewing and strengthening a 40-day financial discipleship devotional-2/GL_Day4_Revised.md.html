DAY 4

Giving Breaks the Power Money Has Over You

The open fist is not a loss — it's a liberation

WHEN I GIVE, I BREAK THE POWER MONEY HAS OVER ME.

TODAY'S VERSES

Matthew 6:24 (NIV) "No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money."

Luke 6:38 (NIV) "Give, and it will be given to you. A good measure, pressed down, shaken together and running over, will be poured into your lap. For with the measure you use, it will be measured to you."

A financial advisor I know — a man who has spent thirty-four years working with families on wealth and giving — told me about a couple he'd watched for years from across a pew. Wealthy people. Good people. But in all that time, genuinely stingy. Not mean-spirited, just closed. Their giving was minimal and guarded, year after year.

Then the tax law changed, and required minimum distributions kicked in. They had to start pulling money out of their IRA whether they wanted to or not. They didn't need the money. They didn't want the money. And the advisor explained: the cleanest way to avoid taxes on it is to give it directly to charity.

Something opened.

He told me what happened next: they came in the following year with a handwritten list of thirty or forty charities — $1,000 here, $1,000 there — giving away every penny of their required distribution. About $100,000 a year. "They come in with lists," he said, and the way he said it you could hear the delight. "They're enthusiastic. They're joyful." Thirty years of closed hands, and then a tax law — of all things — cracked the door. And something God had been waiting to do in them finally had room to move.

I tell that story not because tax efficiency is the point. I tell it because of what the advisor noticed: it wasn't the money that changed. It was the people. The same couple who had been reserved and closed for decades was now animated, generous, alive. The giving didn't cost them something. It freed them from something.

The Clenched Hand and the Open One

A closed hand is a protecting hand. It's doing something understandable — guarding what it holds. But a closed hand also can't receive anything, and it can't give anything. It's occupied entirely with defense.

An open hand is different. It's vulnerable, in one sense — you could lose what's in it. But it's also free. It can give. It can receive. It can hold things lightly rather than desperately. And there's something physiologically true about this that one advisor put to me in a way I've never forgotten: "There's not much space here when I'm grasping. But when I open my hands, there's actually a tremendous amount of space."

I think about the people I've worked with over the years who carry their finances with genuine lightness — who can make a financial decision without spiraling into anxiety, who can give without resentment, who can face a setback without feeling like the ground has disappeared beneath them. Almost without exception, they are generous people. Not because generosity makes life easy, but because the practice of giving regularly resets their relationship with money. They hold it open-handed. And so it doesn't hold them.

Two Masters, One Decision

Jesus isn't being dramatic when he says you cannot serve two masters. He's making a clinical observation about how human devotion works. Something will function as master in your financial life. Something will set the terms — will determine what you protect, what you fear losing, what you can and can't let go of.

And money is a persuasive candidate for that role, because it's practical. It can be counted and tracked and moved around. It gives you something to do when you're anxious. It offers the feeling of control, at least temporarily. But money makes a poor master for the same reason it makes a poor savior: it can't love you back. It can't tell you you're enough. It can't provide the peace it promises. And it demands constant maintenance.

Giving regularly is one of the most effective ways I know to prevent money from becoming master. Not because of what you give — but because of what the act of giving declares. Every time you choose to give, you are choosing, in that moment, which master you serve. You are practicing the open hand. You are weakening money's claim on your devotion.

That's what happened with the couple on the advisor's list. A door opened — almost by accident, through a tax requirement — and they walked through it. And walking through it changed them. Thirty years of building something no one talked about out loud. One decision to release it. And now they're joyful.

The Measure You Use

Luke 6:38 is often read as a prosperity promise — give and you'll get more back. But I think that reading misses the deeper point. Jesus says the measure you use will be measured back to you. He's describing how generosity forms the soul over time. A generous measure — a big, open-handed posture toward your resources — creates a person who can receive generously, who notices abundance rather than scarcity, who experiences provision rather than deprivation. A tight measure creates the opposite.

Generous people don't just give more. They experience life differently. They notice gifts they would otherwise miss. They hold blessings without needing to clench them. The measure you use doesn't just describe your giving — it shapes you.

The couple with the lists had lived by a tight measure for decades. Then, almost despite themselves, they switched. And what the advisor noticed wasn't that they gave more money. It was that they became more themselves — enthusiastic, alive, free.

Giving generously has nothing to do with your income level. It has everything to do with a heart posture. When I open my fist and give, something in me changes. And that change is worth far more than what I gave.

THINK ABOUT IT

Think back to a time you gave something significant — not spare change, but something that required you to actually open your fist. Did you notice anything change in you afterward? What was it?

The couple in the story had closed hands for thirty years, then something cracked open. What do you think was actually keeping them closed — and what does that tell you about what might be keeping you closed?

Jesus says you cannot serve two masters. When you look at your financial decisions honestly, which master has been setting the terms — God or money? What evidence would you point to?

  • If I held that area with an open hand for one week, here's what I'm actually afraid would happen:
  • One specific act of giving that would directly challenge that grip — not symbolic, but real:

The Master Question (5 minutes)

Sit quietly for two minutes. Ask yourself: In my last three significant financial decisions, whose voice was loudest — God's or money's? Don't answer quickly. Let the honest answer surface.

Then: What would one decision look like this week that clearly establishes which master I serve?

CLOSING PRAYER

Jesus, you said I cannot serve two masters. Help me choose — and keep choosing — you. Loosen what I've been clenching. Let my giving be an act of declaring who is Lord, not a performance of generosity. Free my hands and free my heart. Make me someone who holds what I have with open hands — so that what I have doesn't hold me. Amen.
