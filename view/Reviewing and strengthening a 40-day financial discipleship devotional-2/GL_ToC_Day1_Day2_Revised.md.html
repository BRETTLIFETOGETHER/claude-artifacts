DAY 1

Generosity Is the Secret to Contentment

Not its cost — its answer

GENEROSITY AND FINANCIAL FREEDOM ARE INEXTRICABLY LINKED.

TODAY'S VERSES

Matthew 6:21 (NIV) "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."

Acts 20:35 (NIV) "It is more blessed to give than to receive."

Just before I began writing Generous Living, Judy and I flew to Japan to visit our daughter Karen and her husband Mark, who were living there as missionaries. From Japan we traveled to Hong Kong, where Chinese New Year was beginning — the streets mobbed, vendors everywhere, laundry strung between high-rise buildings that stretched up as far as you could see.

In the middle of all that crowd and noise, I kept thinking about Karen and Mark's apartment. Five hundred square feet. The shower stall in the kitchen. No central heating, which meant it could be twenty degrees Fahrenheit inside the apartment in the morning before the space heaters kicked on. By any measurable standard, they had almost nothing.

But Karen was content. Genuinely, visibly, unmistakably content. Not performing contentment, not resigned to it — actually at peace. She and Mark were living on very little, giving generously to their work, and they had a quality of freedom and joy that I noticed every time I was around them.

Meanwhile, I had recently been on a different flight, seated next to a young woman who looked about twenty years old and deeply unhappy. She spent thirty minutes systematically working through an in-flight shopping catalog with a credit card — ordering something from nearly every page. She put the phone down. I looked at her face. She looked exactly as miserable as she had at the start.

Those two images — Karen in her cold apartment and the young woman with everything in the catalog — sat side by side in my mind as I began writing. And they illustrated the central thing I wanted to say: the amount of money you have has nothing to do with contentment or financial freedom. The secret is somewhere else entirely.

The Secret

After twenty-five years of working with families across every income level — people with very little and people with enormous wealth — I came to a conclusion I've staked my professional life on: generosity and financial freedom are inextricably linked.

The people who are genuinely free — who carry their money lightly, who can make financial decisions without anxiety, who are actually content — are almost always the most generous relative to their incomes. Not the wealthiest. The most generous. That pattern is too consistent across too many lives to be coincidence. It's the way God designed the system.

Karen wasn't content because she had enough. She was content because she had given herself to something larger than her own accumulation. The young woman in the catalog wasn't miserable because she had too little. She was miserable because she was looking for contentment in the wrong direction.

You can't purchase contentment. You can't budget your way into freedom. You can't invest your way into peace. These things come from a different direction entirely — from open hands, not clenched ones.

Where Your Heart Is

Jesus says it simply in Matthew 6:21: where your treasure is, your heart will follow. That sentence cuts both ways. It means that what you spend your money on shapes what you love — your spending is discipleship. But it also means the reverse is possible: redirect your treasure, and your heart begins to follow.

Giving is one of the most direct ways to do this. When you give — when you actually release something you've been holding — you interrupt the slow tightening that accumulation produces. You redirect your treasure. And the heart, which follows treasure, begins to loosen.

Acts 20:35 gives you the promise at the end of the experiment: it is more blessed to give than to receive. That sentence gets quoted so often it can lose its force. But stop and sit with it. Jesus didn't say giving is morally superior. He said it produces more blessing — more of what we're actually after. More flourishing. More of what the young woman was trying to find in the catalog.

That's not a guilt statement. It's an invitation to try something that seems backwards and discover that it works.

An Invitation, Not a Demand

I want to say this clearly at the start: I'm not going to pressure you. I've spent a career watching what happens when people are guilt-driven into giving — they give reluctantly, resentfully, or not at all, and it poisons their relationship with generosity for years. That's not what I'm after.

What I'm after is what Paul describes: the person who gives what they have decided in their heart to give, not under compulsion, and experiences in that giving a joy they couldn't have anticipated. God loves a cheerful giver. He is not after reluctant compliance. He is after a free heart.

Generous living is possible for every person, at every income level, in every season. It doesn't start with a larger income. It starts with a question: am I willing to find out if what Jesus said is true? Am I willing to try the direction that seems backwards?

I believe it is true. Karen proved it to me in a cold apartment in Japan. And I'd like to show you what it looks like in your own life.

If I could boil down everything I have ever learned into one sentence, it would be this: generosity and financial freedom are inextricably linked. My daughter in a cold apartment with a shower in the kitchen taught me more about that than most of my wealthy clients.

THINK ABOUT IT

Think about the two images: Karen content in very little, the young woman miserable with everything in the catalog. Which one more closely resembles your relationship with money right now — and what do you think explains the difference?

Think back to a time in the last year when you felt most at peace about money. What was actually going on — financially and spiritually? What does that tell you about where contentment actually comes from for you?

Jesus says where your treasure goes, your heart follows. Look at where your money went last month. What does that tell you about what your heart is actually after?

CLOSING PRAYER

Father, thank you for the people in my life who have shown me what generous living looks like. Forgive me for the times I've looked for contentment in the wrong direction. Teach me that what Jesus said is true — that where I put my treasure, my heart will follow. Open my hands, settle my heart, and show me what this looks like in my actual life. In Jesus' name, amen.

DAY 2

Joy, Not Pressure

God loves a cheerful giver — not a reluctant one

GOD DOESN'T WANT YOUR GIVING TO FEEL HEAVY. HE WANTS IT TO BE JOYFUL.

TODAY'S VERSES

2 Corinthians 9:7–8 (NIV) "Each of you should give what you have decided in your heart to give, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver. And God is able to bless you abundantly, so that in all things at all times, having all that you need, you will abound in every good work."

Psalm 16:11 (NIV) "You make known to me the path of life; you will fill me with joy in your presence, with eternal pleasures at your right hand."

I've been in a lot of church services over the years. And I've watched something happen when the offering moment arrives that I think tells you everything about the relationship most people have with giving.

The room shifts. Not dramatically — just slightly. Shoulders tighten. Eyes go to phones. You can feel people calculating, bracing, or tuning out. Someone up front offers the standard language — "we're not asking you to give what you don't have, we're asking you to be faithful" — and it lands with the warmth of a legal disclaimer. The basket comes around and money goes in, sometimes generously, but often with the energy of someone paying a bill they'd rather not think about.

I understand why. For most people, giving has been wrapped in pressure for so long that the two feel inseparable. Pressure from needs they see. Pressure from expectations in their church or community. Pressure from guilt about what they haven't given. When giving comes packaged with that kind of pressure, the heart instinctively tightens. You may still give, but it feels like obligation. You may be generous, but it feels like loss.

God is not trying to form pressured givers. He is forming cheerful ones.

Decided in the Heart

I met a woman recently who had spent years attending a generosity conference put on by an organization I deeply respect. She told me her husband — a sharp, analytical man, the kind who runs the numbers on everything — had been skeptical from the moment they registered. He kept waiting for the ask. The pitch. The moment when the room would fill with emotional music and someone would suggest a giving amount.

It never came. No ask. No QR code at the end. No implied obligation. And she told me that when they drove home, her husband was almost disoriented by it. He said: "They never asked us for anything." And then something unexpected happened — freed from the defensive posture the anticipated ask had created, they both found themselves thinking openly about generosity for the first time in years. Not about what they owed, but about what they wanted to do. The conversation was different because the pressure was gone.

That's the opening Paul creates in 2 Corinthians 9:7 with four words: "decided in your heart." Not decided by the pastor. Not decided by the basket. Not decided by the room. Decided in your heart. That phrase restores something important: agency. You don't have to be pushed into generosity. You get to choose it. You get to bring it before God. You get to arrive at a decision from a place of conviction rather than a place of being cornered.

That's where cheerful giving begins — not in the amount, not in the method, but in the decision. A thoughtful, prayerful, genuine decision that says: this is what I want to do, because I believe it's right, and because I trust the God who invited me to.

What God Doesn't Want

Paul is specific: not reluctantly, not under compulsion. God does not celebrate reluctant giving. He doesn't endorse giving that comes from feeling cornered. He is not trying to squeeze generosity out of you by pressure.

I think about this often. There is a kind of giving that happens in certain church moments that isn't really giving — it's social compliance. You give because everyone around you is giving, or because you'll feel odd if you don't, or because someone described a need compellingly enough that not giving feels cruel. That's not what God is after. He doesn't want your money if it comes with a resentful heart. He wants your heart — and then your money follows naturally.

If your giving is consistently producing obligation rather than joy, something has gone wrong upstream. Not necessarily in the giving — but in the posture behind it. The goal isn't to give less. It's to give from a freer place. The goal is to develop the kind of heart that gives with joy, because it has understood something true: giving actually produces more blessing than receiving.

I've found in my own life and in the lives of clients that the difference between giving that produces joy and giving that produces resentment almost always comes down to one question: was it decided, or was it demanded? When I decide to give — when I bring it to God, think it through, and choose it freely — the act produces something in me. When I give because I feel trapped, or guilty, or pressured by a public moment, something else happens entirely.

The heart notices.

Joy Has a Source

Psalm 16:11 belongs here because it tells you where joy comes from. Not from circumstances. Not from giving a certain amount. From God's presence. "In your presence there is fullness of joy."

This matters for generosity because many people give from a place of scarcity — they feel like they might not have enough, and giving feels like it deepens that scarcity. But when joy is anchored in God's presence rather than in your balance, giving stops feeling like subtraction. It starts feeling like participation. You're giving from a place of abundance — the abundance of knowing you're held, that God provides, that the act of giving is itself an expression of trust.

That's the transformation I've watched happen in people's lives. Not that they suddenly have more money and therefore give more easily. But that their relationship with money changes — because their relationship with the source of joy changes — and giving becomes lighter. Almost natural, in the best seasons. Not something done to fulfill an obligation, but something that flows from who they're becoming.

God is not after reluctant compliance. He is after joyful participation. The question isn't whether you're giving enough. It's whether the giving you're doing is producing something good in you.

THINK ABOUT IT

When you think about giving, what emotion rises first — joy, obligation, pressure, relief when it's done, or something else? Where do you think that emotion comes from?

The woman's husband spent the whole conference waiting for the ask — and when it didn't come, something opened in him. Have you ever experienced something similar? A moment when pressure lifted and generosity felt genuinely free?

Paul says God loves a cheerful giver. What would need to change in you — not in your circumstances — for your giving to become consistently cheerful?

EXERCISES

  • The biggest source of giving pressure I feel is: (expectations from others / guilt about the past / public moments / what I think God requires / something else)
  • I tend to give most cheerfully when: (one honest sentence)
  • A giving decision I made that felt genuinely decided — not demanded — and what it produced in me:
  • One step toward more joyful giving this week:

Decide in Your Heart (5 minutes)

Take two minutes in quiet. Ask God: What do I actually want to give right now — not what I feel obligated to give, but what I would choose if no one was watching and there was no pressure? Write down what comes. Then ask: What's keeping me from that?

CLOSING PRAYER

Father, you are my joy and my provider. Free me from reluctant giving and form in me a cheerful heart that trusts you. Let my generosity be a decision, not a demand — something I bring to you freely, from a heart that believes your presence is enough. Fill me with joy in your presence, and let that joy overflow. In Jesus' name, amen.
