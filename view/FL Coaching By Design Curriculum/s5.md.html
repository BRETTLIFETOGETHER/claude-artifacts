Session 5 of the Foundation title

SESSION 5

The Conversations That Matter

Why Communication Breaks Down and How to Guide It

INTRODUCTION

KEY VERSE

"The tongue has the power of life and death."

PROVERBS 18:21

There is a particular kind of silence in a family that has something it needs to say. Everyone in the room knows what the subject is. Nobody raises it. The meeting proceeds efficiently through matters of much smaller consequence, and everyone leaves feeling that it went fine.

Advisors learn to recognize that silence and then, very often, learn to work around it, because the alternative is to be the person who names it.

The usual explanation for why these conversations fail is that families are conflict averse or poorly led or carrying old wounds, and sometimes all of that is true. Tom's explanation is different and more useful. Communication breaks down in these families not because people care too little but because the subject matters too much. When you are talking about values, expectations, roles, or the future, you are not exchanging information. You are exposing identity, and people protect that instinctively.

Which means the failure is not a character problem. It is a design problem, and design problems have solutions.

Communication breaks down not because people do not care, but often because it matters so much.

OPENING STORY

The Conversation Nobody Started

Everyone in the family knew the conversation had to happen. That was never the issue.

There were expectations about the future that had never been articulated, held by different people in different forms, and the longer they went unstated the more weight they accumulated. Tom describes this stage as widely recognized and entirely unaddressed. Not one person was willing to start it.

Eventually it started anyway, in the way these things do, without anyone choosing the moment.

It was not guided. There was no structure to it and nobody was holding the room. People reacted quickly. Assumptions that had been sitting underneath for years came out sideways and arrived as accusations rather than as information. The temperature rose and stayed up.

Afterward, everyone agreed it had gone badly.

That is the part Tom emphasizes, and it is the part with the longest consequences. The damage was not confined to the subject at hand. What the family learned from the experience was that these conversations go poorly, and so they became less willing to attempt another one. One bad conversation had made the next one harder, and the one after that harder still.

So Tom took it back to the beginning and ran it differently.

Not with conclusions. Not with anyone's position stated up front. He started with understanding, asked each person to say how they were thinking about it, and did not allow interruption. Then he slowed the whole thing down.

Same family. Same subject. Same set of genuinely difficult facts.

Completely different experience.

The topic had never been the problem.

TOM CONWAY

THE FRAMEWORK

The Three Keys to Communication

Tom uses these as the ground rules he establishes before a family talks, and they hold up because each one carries a scriptural warrant he can point to rather than a preference he is imposing.

  • Be Direct. Say the thing. Ambiguity in a family conversation is not kindness, it is deferral, and the deferred version arrives later with interest. Matthew 5:37, 2 Corinthians 6:11-13, Proverbs 25:11-12.

  • Be Truthful. Accuracy about facts and about your own position. Most family breakdowns do not involve lying. They involve people saying a softened version of what they think and then being frustrated that nobody responded to what they meant. Zechariah 8:16, Psalm 15:2, Philippians 4:8, Ephesians 4:25.

  • Be Respectful. Directness without respect is just force, and it produces compliance rather than understanding. 1 Peter 4:8-11, Psalm 15:3, 1 Corinthians 13:4-7, Romans 12:10, Matthew 7:12, John 13:34.

The three work as a set. Any two without the third fails predictably. Direct and truthful without respect wounds. Truthful and respectful without directness never gets to the subject. Direct and respectful without truth is pleasant and useless.

Watch Out for Triangulation

Tom flags this separately and with some urgency, because it is the single most common structural failure in family communication and it is nearly invisible while it is happening.

Triangulation is when two people discuss a third rather than one person addressing the other directly. It feels productive to everyone involved. Information moves, sympathy is exchanged, tension is temporarily relieved. What it actually does is remove any possibility of the original issue being resolved, while steadily hardening the position of the person who is absent.

Advisors get recruited into this constantly. A family member calls you about another family member. The call is genuinely useful to you and it feels like trust. It is also the moment you become part of the structure rather than a guide to it.

THE TOOL

The Three Moves

Built from Tom's questions in this session.

There is no named instrument in the source for guiding a live conversation. What there is, stated plainly by Tom, is three things that change the dynamic significantly on their own. Treated as a sequence rather than a list, they function as a tool.

Move One: Create space for each person to speak. Not an invitation to speak, which the quiet member of the family will decline. An allocation. Each person says how they are seeing it, in turn, before anyone responds to anyone. State the structure at the start so that being asked to wait is not experienced as being silenced.

Move Two: Listen without immediately responding. The discipline here is on you and on the family equally. Nobody answers the previous speaker. When a person finishes, the next person begins, and the responding happens later. This feels artificial for approximately four minutes and then stops feeling artificial.

Move Three: Slow the pace. Reactivity is a function of speed. When a room is moving quickly, people defend. When it slows, people think. You control the pace almost entirely through your own tempo, the length of your pauses, and whether you fill the gaps.

Sequence matters. Space without slowness produces a rushed round of statements. Slowness without space lets the loudest person occupy more of it. The listening rule is what prevents the first two from becoming a debate with better manners.

How to open it. Tom's reset instruction is to choose one conversation, set a clear intention, and focus on listening first, with no requirement to solve anything. Say that out loud to the family at the start. Telling a room that nothing is being decided today is what allows the room to speak freely, and it is also the promise you must actually keep.

THIS WEEK'S READINGS

Five readings, one per day, from Family Legacy Coaching by Design by Tom Conway with Brett Eastman. [Page ranges pending final layout of the companion book.]

Day One: Strategy Development and the Planning Team

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 10

Stage 2 of the process, including who belongs on the planning team and the trusted advisor conversation.

Coaching insight. Read this for the communication problem hiding inside a technical chapter. A planning team is a group of professionals who have never been in a room together, each holding part of the picture, each having advised the client separately. The coordination failure that follows is a communication failure, and someone has to be responsible for it. Decide whether that is you.

Day Two: Family Meeting Guiding Principles

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 12, opening sections

The principles Tom holds when a family meets, and why the meeting needs rules established before it needs an agenda.

Coaching insight. Notice that nearly every principle is set before the meeting rather than during it. This is the whole discipline. Anything you have to introduce mid-meeting will be read as a reaction to something that just happened, and therefore as taking a side. Ground rules established in advance are structural. The same words introduced at minute forty are a judgment.

Day Three: Running the Meeting

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 12, later sections

Mechanics: who is in the room, who speaks, what is on the table, and how it closes.

Coaching insight. Pay attention to how much of the outcome is determined by decisions made before anyone sits down. Advisors who find family meetings frightening are usually improvising in real time, which is genuinely frightening. The full treatment of this is a six-session title of its own, called Meetings.

Day Four: Developing Family Vision, Mission, and Values

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 13, opening sections

How a family arrives at a shared statement, and why the process matters more than the document.

Coaching insight. The example of a family whose vision statement was about being the reason people look up rather than about wealth is worth studying for its ordinariness. It is not literary. It came out of a room. A statement a family would not have written on its own is a statement they will not use.

Day Five: Every Voice in the Room

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 13, later sections

Involving every generation in the work of defining what the family stands for.

Coaching insight. The structural point here is about votes. When a founder holds one voice among ten rather than presiding, something changes in what the next generation is willing to say. Consider which of your client patriarchs could tolerate that arrangement, and what it would take to propose it.

The families who communicate well are not the ones with less to discuss. They are the ones who learned, usually through one conversation that went better than expected, that the subject was survivable. That single experience is what changes a family's willingness, and it can be engineered.

Which puts you in an unusual position. You cannot make a family talk. You can make the first attempt more likely to go well, and going well once is most of what is required.

The full treatment of this is a six-session title of its own, called Communication.
