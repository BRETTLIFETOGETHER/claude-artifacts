All eighteen Scenario Responses, the answer key for the Foundation title

SCENARIO RESPONSES

All eighteen responses, in session order.

Go back and write your own answer first if you have not. These are not the only correct handling of each situation. They are how Tom approaches them, and the reasoning is worth more than the move.

SESSION 1

Response to Scenario One

The eleven-year client who arrives with a folder full of distribution questions.

What is actually happening is that he has translated a legacy question into the only vocabulary anyone has ever offered him. Nobody in eleven years has asked him a different kind of question, so when the subject finally surfaced he reached for the trust document, because that is what legacy has always meant in your meetings. The folder is not the problem. The folder is the evidence.

Tom's logic here is that you do not correct the framing, you widen it, and you do it before the technical material gets on the table. Once you open the trust, the meeting is about the trust. His move is to start with purpose rather than assets, which sounds soft until you notice it is a sequencing decision rather than a tonal one. The question he uses is direct enough to survive a business owner's patience: what do you want to be true of your family over time. Then the harder companion to it, which is what else are you passing on besides the money.

The mechanism is that thirty at thirty-five is an unanswerable question in isolation. There is no correct age. There is only an age that fits what the father is trying to accomplish with his children, and he has never articulated what that is, which is precisely why the question has been sitting unresolved. When you ask him what he wants his children to understand about the money, the distribution question usually answers itself within twenty minutes, and it answers itself in his voice rather than yours.

Next move. Leave the folder closed. Open with the purpose question, spend the first thirty minutes there, and tell him plainly at the top that you will get to the trust and that you want ten minutes on something first. Then take his answer, in his own words, and use it to frame the distribution decision at the end of the same meeting.

Response to Scenario Two

The couple certain their four children already know what the family stands for.

Underneath their confidence is a reasonable inference from real evidence. They raised these children, the relationships are intact, nobody has behaved badly. What they have done is conclude from the absence of conflict that there is alignment, and those are different things. Tom names assumption as the primary obstacle for exactly this reason. A family in conflict knows it has a problem. A family running on assumption does not, and it will not until a decision forces the issue.

The move is not to challenge their belief, which would sound like an accusation about their parenting and would be heard that way regardless of your tone. It is to make the question testable. Tom's framing separates understanding from agreement, and it works here because you are not suggesting the children disagree. You are suggesting nobody has checked, which is a much easier thing for a parent to accept.

The specific mechanism is that values are not transferred by proximity. Tom's point is that what gets passed on most clearly is often not what was intended but what was lived, and children draw conclusions from what they observed rather than from what was meant. Four children watching the same parents over thirty years will arrive at four readings, and none of them will have been corrected, because nobody ever said any of it out loud. That is not a failure of parenting. It is what happens in the absence of a conversation.

Next move. Ask their permission to put the same question to each of the four separately, and be explicit that you are expecting to find agreement. Then run it. If the answers converge, you have confirmed something valuable and cost them an hour. If they diverge, the family learns it from their own children rather than from you, which is the only version they can act on without defensiveness.

Response to Scenario Three

The fifty-eight-year-old owner who wants to deal with it after the liquidity event.

He is not deflecting and treating him as though he were will lose you the argument. Tom's third common misunderstanding is precisely this one, the belief that legacy is a later conversation, and he is careful to say that the delay is usually sincere. The owner has time, statistically speaking, and he is in the strongest stretch of his career. Every instinct he has says finish the thing in front of you.

Tom's counter is not urgency, it is accuracy. The transfer is already underway. His framing is that legacy is not a future event but something being formed right now in how a person lives and what he prioritizes, which means the question is not whether to start but whether to start on purpose. His children are watching the strongest three years of the business happen right now and drawing conclusions from it. Something is being passed on this year whether or not anyone has scheduled it.

There is a second mechanism worth putting in front of him, and it is the one that tends to land with owners. After the sale, the number is fixed and public within the family, and every conversation about values will be read as a conversation about allocation. Before the sale, the same conversation is abstract enough to be honest. He has a window that closes at the closing, and it does not reopen.

Next move. Do not ask again for the full conversation. Ask for one hour, framed narrowly, on what he wants his children to understand about the business he built. Owners will give an hour to that when they will not give a day to legacy planning. If he says later a second time, accept it without argument and put a note in your calendar to raise it in ninety days, because Tom's own experience with a family and the fifth child idea ran eighteen months from first ask to yes. Patience is part of the technique rather than a substitute for it.

SESSION 2

Response to Scenario One

The client who asks you directly what you would do about his daughter's role.

The thing to notice is that both of your instincts are correct. Answering does end the conversation, and refusing to answer is a dodge that he will read accurately. The trap is treating this as a choice between the two, which is how most advisors experience it and why most default to answering.

Tom's approach is one question before one answer, and the emphasis belongs on before rather than instead. He is not asking you to withhold your judgment permanently. He is asking you not to lead with it, because a recommendation delivered into a room where the family has not yet said what it thinks becomes the position everyone else has to react to. Once your view is on the table, the daughter's father is no longer thinking about his daughter. He is thinking about your view.

The mechanism is that you have information he does not, and he has information you do not, and the second kind is decisive here. You know what the role should be structurally. He knows what his daughter is like at Thanksgiving, what she said to him in the car six months ago, and what he has never told her about why he built the business. Your recommendation without that material is a well-formed answer to a question that has not been fully stated.

Next move. Say that you do have a view and you will give it, and that you want to understand two things first. Then ask how he is seeing it, and what feels most important to him about it. Listen without moving toward your answer. Most of the time the conversation goes somewhere you did not anticipate and your original recommendation changes. When it does not, give it at the end of the meeting, where it lands as a conclusion the two of you reached rather than as advice he received.

Response to Scenario Two

Eight seconds of silence after a question about the next twenty years.

What is happening in the room is that a good question is working. The silence is not a failure of the question, it is the sound of two people encountering something they have not thought about in that form before. Tom's second obstacle for advisors making this shift is discomfort with silence, and this is the moment it shows up.

His logic is that space is where clarity begins to emerge, and that filling it is the thing that prevents the emergence. The instinct to rescue the moment is an instinct to protect yourself rather than the client, which is worth naming privately because it changes what you do about it. Nobody in that room is suffering except you. The husband shifting in his chair is thinking, and your associate looking at you is inexperienced.

The specific mechanism is that the first thing a person says after a hard question is usually a deflection, and the second thing is usually true. If you fill the gap at eight seconds you get neither. If you hold it, you get the deflection at fifteen and the real answer somewhere after that. Tom's instruction to slow the pace is not about a gentle atmosphere, it is about giving people time to think rather than react, and thinking takes longer than the room feels like it should.

Next move. Hold it. Look at the table rather than at either of them, which removes the pressure of eye contact without removing your attention. At twenty seconds, do not answer your own question and do not rephrase it, which signals it was a bad question. Say something that gives permission without filling the space, along the lines of taking whatever time they need on it. Then wait again. If it genuinely stalls, ask them each to write one sentence rather than say it, which almost always breaks the block.

Response to Scenario Three

The partner who says it looked like you did not do very much.

He is not wrong about what he observed, and the useful first step is to concede that. From the outside, guidance looks like less work than advice, because the work moved from the visible part of the meeting into the questions and the restraint. The problem is that his observation is being made by the person who allocates your time, which means this is a business conversation rather than a philosophical one.

Tom's framing is that guiding a family through a meaningful process often creates more value than providing a solution, and the way to make that case internally is to make it concrete rather than to defend the posture. Do not argue that listening has value. Show him what came out of the hour that would not have come out of a recommendation meeting, and name the specific thing the family said that nobody in the firm knew before.

The mechanism your partner is missing is the difference between transactional and transformational work. A transaction ends, and the relationship with it. What was happening in that room was the construction of the reason a family stays through a generational transition, and the return on it shows up years later in a retained relationship rather than this quarter in a completed recommendation. That is a real argument and it has numbers attached, but it is a patience argument, and patience arguments have to be made before the results arrive rather than after.

Next move. Give him one sentence on what you learned in that hour that changes what the firm will recommend, and one sentence on what it would have cost to learn it later. Then invite him to sit in on the next one, not to be persuaded but because watching a second one is what makes it legible. Advisors rarely change their view of this work from a description of it.

SESSION 3

Response to Scenario One

The client who agrees to the values conversation and moves the invite three times.

He is not resisting you and he is probably not avoiding the subject. He is triaging, and the values conversation keeps losing to things with deadlines attached, which is what happens to every item in a busy person's week that has no external forcing function. His enthusiasm in the meeting is real. It simply does not survive contact with his calendar.

Tom's observation is that families skip the foundation and go straight to the structure, and that when they do, the structure does not always hold. That is the case to make, but the practical problem here is not persuasion, it is scheduling design. This conversation has been positioned as optional by its format rather than by anything he believes, because it has no agenda, no deliverable, and no consequence for missing it. The two technical decisions he made easily in the same period each had a document attached and a date.

The mechanism is that unstructured time gets displaced by structured time regardless of importance. The fix is to give the conversation the same properties as the things that survive his calendar. Attach it to a deliverable, put a boundary on it, and make it produce something at the end. Tom's own practice does this, which is why the Goals Conversation sits inside the Family Legacy Review as a step in a process with a deliverable rather than as a conversation he hopes to have.

Next move. Stop asking for the values conversation. Propose ninety minutes with a specific name, a specific output, and a specific date, framed as the first step of a defined piece of work rather than as a discussion. Bring the five areas as the agenda. If it moves a fourth time, ask him directly and without edge whether this is something he wants to do, and accept the answer.

Response to Scenario Two

The wife who agrees with everything her husband says, for six years.

You are right that you cannot tell, and you are right that guessing wrong is costly in both directions. If it is genuine agreement and you probe it, you have implied a division that does not exist. If it is accommodation and you let it stand, you have just built a values statement on one person's answers and put both their names on it.

Tom's method is to create space for each perspective to be heard, and the operative word is create. An invitation to speak is not space, because the person who has been deferring for six years will decline an invitation. What changes the dynamic is allocation, where each person answers in turn before anyone responds to anyone, stated as the structure at the start rather than introduced when she goes quiet. Introduced mid-meeting it is a spotlight. Established at the top it is a format.

The mechanism worth understanding is that her agreement may be entirely genuine on the substance and still be uninformative, because she has never been asked in a setting where a different answer was structurally possible. You are not looking for disagreement. Tom's distinction between understanding and agreement applies here to you as much as to them. You are trying to find out what she thinks, and whether it matches is a separate question you do not need answered today.

Next move. Do not address it in this meeting, which would put her on the spot in front of him. Restart the exercise with an explicit structure at the top of the next one, each person answering in turn with no responses until both have finished, and go to her first on at least two of the five areas. If that does not move it, ask the client's permission to run the goals conversation with each of them separately, framed as standard practice rather than as a response to anything.

Response to Scenario Three

Three weeks from closing, and the client wants to deal with values afterward.

Your read is correct and the timing argument is real. After the closing there will be more advisors, more opinions, and a number that everyone in the family knows, and every conversation about values will be heard as a conversation about who gets what. Before the closing the same conversation is abstract enough to be honest. That window is closing on a date you both know.

The difficulty is that pushing now has a genuine cost and you would be the one imposing it. Everyone involved is operating at capacity on a transaction with a hard date, and an advisor who insists on a values conversation three weeks out will be experienced as not understanding the priority, fairly or not. Tom's caution about moving to decisions too quickly cuts against him here, but his broader logic about patience cuts against you.

The mechanism that resolves it is scope. The full Goals Conversation across five areas is not available in the next three weeks and asking for it will fail. What is available is one narrow question that has to be answered before the wire hits regardless, which is what the family wants this money to do. That is not a values exercise, it is a transaction question, and it is a legitimate one to raise inside a closing.

Next move. Ask for forty-five minutes on a single question, framed as part of the transaction rather than separate from it. Get his answer, in his words, written down. Then book the full conversation for six to eight weeks after the closing and put it in the calendar now, while the urgency of the deal makes him agreeable, rather than after, when the pressure lifts and the item loses its forcing function.

SESSION 4

Response to Scenario One

The father certain his three children are on the same page, when two of them are not.

You have a confidentiality problem and an assumption problem stacked on top of each other, and only one of them is solvable in the meeting. What the children said to you privately is not yours to move, and the first thing to settle internally is that you will not move it, because the moment you do you have converted every future private remark in that family into something that gets reported.

Tom's approach does not require you to disclose anything. His framing is that assumption is the obstacle, and the intervention is to make the assumption testable rather than to contradict it. You are not telling the father he is wrong. You are pointing out that nobody has checked, and offering to check. That is a proposal a confident father can accept without loss, because he expects to be vindicated.

The mechanism that makes this urgent rather than academic is what Tom describes about differences that surface later attached to a decision. Right now the divergence is inert. It is a set of unstated interpretations sitting in three separate heads. When the business transition arrives, those same interpretations will attach themselves to a deadline and a dollar figure, and at that point they stop being differences and become positions, which are considerably harder to move.

Next move. Ask his permission to have a structured conversation with each of the three, framed as ordinary diligence before a transition and not as a response to anything you have heard. Ask the same questions of each. Then bring back what they tell you in that setting, which they will have given you knowing it is coming back, and which therefore is yours to carry. If he declines, do not press it and do not go around him.

Response to Scenario Two

The mother who says pleasantly that this is probably not the right time.

Two things are happening simultaneously. A real difference just surfaced, and the family's protective mechanism engaged within seconds to put it away. The relief on two other faces is the tell. This is not one person avoiding a subject, it is a system doing what it has done successfully for years.

Tom's instruction is that avoiding differences because they feel uncomfortable is one of the common obstacles, and that once you can see something you can work with it. But he also holds that the purpose is understanding rather than agreement, and that distinction is what gives you a move here. The mother is not objecting to understanding. She is objecting to what she believes comes next, which is a fight about a decision. If you can separate those two things out loud, the objection frequently dissolves.

The mechanism is that she is protecting the room from an outcome you were not proposing. Nobody stated that the difference had to be resolved today, and in her experience raising something like this means resolving it or damaging something. Naming the alternative explicitly, that nothing is being decided and you would like to understand it before it comes back on its own, changes what she thinks she is agreeing to.

Next move. Agree with her, genuinely and out loud, that this is not the moment to work it through. Then ask for four minutes rather than the remaining forty, on understanding only, with each person saying how they see it and nobody responding. Say plainly that no decision is being made today. If she declines again, let it go completely, and raise it with her privately afterward rather than in the room.

Response to Scenario Three

The family that wants to decide now and sort the details later.

The deadline is real, everyone is tired, and the person who said it is being sensible rather than evasive. That matters, because if you treat this as avoidance you will be arguing with someone who is not doing the thing you are accusing them of.

Tom's position is that moving to decisions before there is enough shared understanding produces weak alignment, and the word weak is doing the work. The decision will get made and it will hold on paper. What it will not do is survive contact with the first disagreement about implementation, because there is no shared understanding underneath it to fall back on. Six months from now the same conversation returns, and it returns with a decision already made, which is a much worse position for everyone.

The mechanism to put in front of them is specific rather than general. Ask what happens when the first thing goes differently than expected. A decision resting on shared understanding gets adjusted. A decision resting on exhaustion gets relitigated, and the relitigation includes the original decision. That is not a reason to miss a deadline. It is a reason to spend twenty minutes now rather than a month later.

Next move. Do not oppose the decision. Ask for twenty minutes before it, with each person stating what they understand the decision to mean and what they expect to happen next. The gaps show up immediately and usually take less time to close than anyone feared. If they overrule you, make the decision with them, document what each person understood it to mean, and put a review date on the calendar. Being overruled is not a failure. Being overruled without a record is.

SESSION 5

Response to Scenario One

The son who corrects his sister on a fact, and the ten minutes of silence that follow.

He was right about the fact and that is the least important thing in the room. What happened is that the conversation demonstrated its actual rules within twelve minutes, and every person present now knows them. Speaking here carries a risk of correction. The sister absorbed that first and the others absorbed it by watching.

Tom names interrupting and correcting before fully understanding as a common mistake, and the reason it is so damaging is that the cost is not paid by the person interrupted. It is paid by everyone who was about to speak. His three moves exist precisely to prevent this, and the second one, listening without immediately responding, is the one that failed here. Not because anyone was hostile, but because the rule was never established.

The mechanism is that a family conversation runs on whatever precedent gets set in the first fifteen minutes, and that precedent is set by what goes uncorrected rather than by what anyone announces. The father not noticing is not incidental. In most families the person with the most authority sets the tone by what he lets pass, and his silence read as endorsement.

Next move. Intervene within a minute rather than at the end, because after five minutes it becomes a public rebuke rather than a rule. Do not address the son and do not adjudicate the fact. Restate the structure for everyone, that each person finishes before anyone responds, positioned as something you should have set at the start. Then go back to the sister by name and ask her to finish what she was saying. Getting her back into the conversation in that same meeting is what determines whether she is in the next one.

Response to Scenario Two

The daughter who calls two days early and asks you not to mention it.

This is triangulation and it will not feel like it. It will feel like being trusted, and the information will be genuinely useful, and her request will seem reasonable, which is exactly why Tom flags it separately and with some urgency. The structure is the problem rather than the content.

His warning is that two people discussing a third removes any possibility of the original issue being resolved while steadily hardening the position of the person who is absent. Applied here, you would walk into that meeting holding a private account of her brother that he does not know you have, from a person who has told you and not him. Every judgment you make in the room afterward is shaped by material he cannot answer, and you have become part of the family's structure rather than a guide to it.

The mechanism that makes this hard is that the call is not malicious and refusing it looks rigid. She is anxious about a meeting and reaching for the person she thinks can help. The move is not to refuse the call. It is to decline the secrecy while keeping the relationship, and to redirect the content toward the person it concerns, which is Tom's three keys operating on you rather than on the family.

Next move. Listen to the whole call without agreeing to the condition, and be honest before she gets far in that you cannot hold something back from her brother that shapes how you handle the meeting. Then ask the question that matters, which is whether she has said any of this to him. Offer to help her say it, in the meeting or before it. If she asks you to drop it entirely, do, and go into the meeting having heard something you will not use.

Response to Scenario Three

The workable resolution you can see twenty minutes in.

Your instinct is well trained and it is pointed at the wrong target. You are solving the problem the family named, and the reason it took them years to name it was never that they lacked a solution. Tom's observation is that people move too quickly toward solving an issue instead of fully exploring it, and that doing so often produces more tension rather than less.

The specific damage is not that your resolution is wrong. It is probably right. The damage is that it arrives before the family has understood each other, which means the resolution becomes yours and the understanding never happens. Tom's marker for a well-guided conversation is movement toward understanding rather than toward quick resolution, and he is explicit that success is not immediate agreement. A family that accepts your solution without having heard each other has resolved a decision and learned nothing about how to have the next one.

There is a second cost that is easy to miss. This family has something difficult on the table in the first twenty minutes, which is unusual and hard-won. If the meeting ends early with a solution, the lesson they take is that raising hard things produces efficient outcomes, which sounds fine until the next hard thing does not have a workable solution attached.

Next move. Hold your resolution. Ask each person what they understand the other to be saying, which is slower than it sounds and almost always reveals that they do not. Let the hour be uncomfortable. If the conversation arrives at your resolution on its own, which happens more often than advisors expect, it belongs to them. If it does not conclude, say so plainly and schedule the next one. On the question of what the client is paying for, the honest answer is that he is paying for a family that can hold this conversation again without you.

SESSION 6

Response to Scenario One

The father who agrees in principle and declines every specific proposal for two years.

Each reason is individually reasonable and the pattern is the answer. Two years of reasonable reasons is not a scheduling problem. Something is being protected, and until it is named nothing you propose will be accepted, because the proposals were never the obstacle.

Tom identifies waiting too long as the first common failure and is careful about why it happens. Sometimes it is genuine uncertainty about how to involve the next generation meaningfully, which is Scenario Three and looks different from this. Sometimes it is something closer to identity. A man who built something has usually built himself into it, and involving the next generation is the first concrete signal that the arrangement is temporary. That is not a failing and it does not respond to argument.

The mechanism worth understanding is that you cannot address this by improving the proposal. A better designed involvement plan is the fifth reasonable thing he declines. What changes it is naming the pattern without naming a motive, which lets him supply the reason himself, and people defend a position they were accused of far longer than one they described.

Next move. Stop proposing. Say plainly and without edge that you have brought this five times over two years and he has had a good reason each time, and that you have started to think the timing is not what is in the way. Then stop talking. Whatever he says next is the actual conversation. If he does not say anything, leave it and return in a quarter. Do not go to the children.

Response to Scenario Two

The client who wants a financial education program for his adult children.

Take the engagement. He is asking for the right thing at the wrong scope, and refusing or renegotiating it up front risks the whole thing over a distinction he cannot see yet. What he has done is unusual and worth protecting, which is to volunteer that his children need preparation and to put money behind it.

Tom's second common failure is focusing only on financial understanding without addressing values and purpose, and the reason it fails is mechanical rather than philosophical. Heirs who understand the trust and not the intention behind it end up administering something competently while having no idea what it is for, which is the same position the family in Session 1 was in with considerably better documentation. Financial literacy is one of the nine areas in the interview and six of the others have nothing to do with money.

The way to widen it is not to argue for the additional content. It is to build the diagnostic in, because the case makes itself. Interview each of the children before designing the curriculum, framed as making sure the material is pitched correctly, which is true. What comes back from the three greatest concerns question and the greatest relief question will tell you what they actually need, and it will let you go back to the father with his own children's words rather than with your theory.

Next move. Agree to the program. Propose that you spend an hour with each child first so the material fits them, which no reasonable client refuses. Secure his permission and set confidentiality with everyone before you start. Then design what they need, deliver the investment and tax content he asked for, and let the purpose material sit inside it rather than beside it.

Response to Scenario Three

The client who is willing and genuinely does not know how, with four different children.

This is the best version of this problem and the failure mode is doing too much. He is open, he is asking, and the temptation with an hour is to lay out the whole architecture: seven steps, six areas, interviews, a family meeting, a training plan. He will agree to all of it in the room and none of it will happen, because the plan will be too large to start.

Tom's instruction is to start by involving the next generation in one meaningful way and to resist doing everything at once. His logic is that preparation is gradual by nature and that the families who do it well involved their children over years rather than in a program. The four children being different in temperament and capability is not a complication to solve before starting. It is the reason to start with something small enough to work with all four.

The specific mechanism is that the first step has to be one the father can execute without you and without a difficult conversation. Distance is a real constraint for two of them and any plan requiring everyone in a room will slip. What travels is a question. Something he asks each of them individually, on a call, in his own words, that treats them as people with a view rather than as recipients with a status.

Next move. Use the hour on one thing. Help him choose a single question to ask each of the four in the next month, separately, and help him decide what he will do with the answers. Do not raise the family meeting. Do not raise the seven steps. Deliberately leave out the interviews, the training plan, and anything requiring travel. Book the next hour for after he has asked all four, and let what comes back set the second step.

BUILD NOTES

Not for print.

Eighteen responses, three to four paragraphs each, in session order. Every response names what is happening beneath the surface, gives Tom's logic, gets specific about the mechanism, and ends with a concrete next move.

Sourcing. Reasoning is drawn from the six video scripts, the Workbook Legend, the Workshop Transcripts, and the Coaching Book. No counsel is attributed to Tom that does not appear in those sources. Where a response extends his logic into a situation he does not address directly, the extension is in the mechanism rather than in the principle.

Three responses carry extensions worth your eye. Session 3 Scenario One treats the scheduling problem as a format problem rather than a persuasion problem, which follows from how Tom structures the Family Legacy Review but is not stated by him. Session 4 Scenario Three proposes documenting what each person understood a decision to mean when you are overruled, which is a reasonable extension rather than sourced counsel. Session 6 Scenario One reads two years of reasonable refusals as identity rather than timing, which follows from Tom's treatment of waiting too long but goes further than the source.

Confidentiality handling. Three scenarios turn on information you hold that you cannot move: Session 4 Scenario One, Session 5 Scenario Two, and Session 6 Scenario Two. All three responses resolve the same way, by declining to move the information and redirecting toward the person it concerns. That consistency is deliberate.

Length. Roughly 5,400 words. Longest single section of the appendix, which is appropriate given it is the only answer key.
