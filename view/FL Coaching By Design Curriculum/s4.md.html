Session 4 of the Foundation title

SESSION 4

Creating Alignment in the Family

Getting Everyone on the Same Page

INTRODUCTION

KEY VERSE

"Do two walk together unless they have agreed to do so?"

AMOS 3:3

Clarity is an individual achievement. A man can be completely clear about what he wants and his wife can be completely clear about what she wants and the two can be incompatible, and nothing about the clarity of either position moves the family an inch.

This is the gap that surprises advisors. You do good work in the values conversation, everyone leaves the room satisfied, and eighteen months later the family is stuck again on something that should have been settled. What happened is that five people each got clearer about their own position without any of them getting closer to each other.

The word alignment gets used loosely in this profession, usually to mean agreement, and that is the wrong target. Families do not need to want the same things. They need enough shared understanding to move in the same direction while wanting somewhat different things, which is a much lower bar and a much more durable one.

The thing that prevents alignment is almost never conflict. It is assumption, which is quieter and considerably more dangerous, because a family in conflict knows it has a problem.

Alignment does not mean everyone thinks the same. It means there is enough shared understanding to move forward together.

OPENING STORY

They Thought They Had Said It

The parents were certain. Tom noticed that first, because certainty in a first meeting is usually worth examining.

They believed they had been clear with their children about what mattered most in the family and about the direction things were heading. They were not being defensive when they said so. They had raised these children, they had lived alongside them for decades, and there had never been a serious rupture. In their reading of the situation, everyone knew where things stood.

Then Tom talked with the children.

The interpretations coming back were substantially different from each other and different again from what the parents had described. Not opposed exactly. Different in emphasis, different in what each one had assumed was settled, different in what each believed had been promised and to whom.

Every one of them cared about the family. That was never in question, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the part advisors get wrong when they hear this story. Nobody in that family was being difficult.

They simply were not aligned, and because the differences had never been said out loud, nobody knew.

What happens next is predictable. The differences do not stay dormant. They surface later, attached to a decision, at a moment when there is money on the table and a deadline attached, and by then they are not differences anymore. They are positions.

So Tom made room for the conversation. Not to force agreement, and he was explicit with the family about that. To build understanding.

Each person said how they were thinking about it. What mattered to them. How they saw the future.

What came out of that was not unity. What came out was a map. There were areas where the family was genuinely aligned, and there were areas where it plainly was not, and for the first time all of it was visible.

Tom's point about that moment is the one to keep. Once you can see it, you can work with it.

TOM CONWAY

THE FRAMEWORK

Understanding, Not Agreement

This is the shortest framework in the series and it does more work than any other. Tom uses it as the stated purpose of the Goals Conversation and as the ground rule he sets before a family talks.

  • Agreement is transactional. It settles a question and closes. It can be reached without anyone understanding anyone, and it frequently is, which is why agreements reached under pressure tend not to hold.

  • Understanding is relational. It does not settle the question. It changes the conditions under which the question gets handled, and it leads to planning and problem solving rather than to a signature.

  • The way to understanding is empathetic listening. Not sympathetic. Empathetic. You are not agreeing with the position, you are getting far enough inside it to state it back in a way the person would accept as accurate.

The practical value is that it gives you something to say when a family is bracing for a fight. You can tell them honestly that nobody is being asked to agree today, and mean it, and the temperature in the room drops immediately.

It also protects you. An advisor who is trying to produce agreement has taken on responsibility for an outcome he cannot control and does not own. An advisor who is producing understanding has taken on something he can actually deliver.

One warning attached. Understanding is not a way of avoiding the hard conversation. It is what makes the hard conversation survivable, and if the family never gets to the decision, the process has failed at the other end.

Take one family with a live disagreement. What would each person need to hear stated back accurately before they could move?

THE TOOL

The Family Legacy Goals and Gaps Analysis

This is the deliverable at the end of Stage 1, and it is the instrument that converts a set of conversations into something a family can look at together.

What it does. It sets what the family says it wants against what the current plan actually does, and shows the distance between them. The power of it is that it is not an opinion. You are not telling a family they are misaligned. You are showing them their own stated goals next to their own existing documents.

Where the gaps show up. Tom works these categories:

  • Estate planning gaps
  • Wealth transfer gaps
  • Wisdom transfer gaps
  • Clarity and communication gaps
  • Income and capital gains gaps
  • Business continuity and succession gaps
  • Business legacy gaps
  • Charitable giving gaps

Four of those are technical and every advisor around the family can find them. The wisdom transfer gap and the clarity and communication gap are the two nobody else is looking for, and they are usually the largest.

Why it produces alignment rather than argument. A family arguing about intentions is arguing about interpretation, and interpretation has no referee. A family looking at a document that does not match what they just said they wanted is looking at a fact. The conversation changes character. Nobody has to be wrong.

Running it. The analysis requires two inputs, which is why it comes third. You need the Goals Conversation across the five areas, and you need a real review of the existing plan. Tom uses the FAMILY Review to do the second one, a structured inventory covering the financial statement, assets under management, mandatory documents, insurance, liabilities and taxes, and yearly cash flow. That instrument gets its full treatment later in the series and in the companion book.

One discipline. Present the gaps without recommending the fix in the same meeting. The gap is the finding. Letting a family sit with a gap for a week is what produces the motivation that makes the fix stick.

THIS WEEK'S READINGS

Five readings, one per day, from Family Legacy Coaching by Design by Tom Conway with Brett Eastman. [Page ranges pending final layout of the companion book.]

Day One: Stage One and the Goals Conversation

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 7, opening sections

How Tom runs the Goals Conversation and what he is listening for underneath the answers.

Coaching insight. Watch for the line about the purpose being understanding rather than agreement, and notice where in the process he says it. He does not save it for the difficult moment. He establishes it before anything is at stake, which is the only time a ground rule can be established without it looking like a maneuver.

Day Two: Running the Conversation With More Than One Person

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 7, later sections

What changes when the conversation includes a spouse or the next generation rather than one decision maker.

Coaching insight. Count the number of times in the last year you have run a substantive conversation with more than the primary client in the room. For most advisors the number is small, and the reason is scheduling rather than conviction. Alignment cannot be produced in serial one-on-one meetings, however good those meetings are.

Day Three: The FAMILY Review

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 8

The structured inventory of what a family actually has, covering the financial statement, assets under management, mandatory documents, insurance, liabilities and taxes, and yearly cash flow.

Coaching insight. This is the most technical reading in the series so far and it is here for a specific reason. Alignment work without a real inventory is a conversation about feelings. The inventory is what makes the gaps demonstrable, and demonstrable is what keeps this from being your opinion against theirs.

Day Four: Goals and Gaps Analysis

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 9, opening sections

The deliverable itself, how it is assembled, and what it looks like in a client's hands.

Coaching insight. Note that the analysis compares the family's own words to the family's own documents, and that you appear in it almost not at all. That is deliberate. The less of your judgment there is in the document, the more the family can argue with the finding rather than with you, and arguing with the finding is the productive version.

Day Five: Presenting the Gaps

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 9, later sections

Delivering the analysis to a family, including which gaps to lead with.

Coaching insight. Decide in advance how you will handle the wisdom transfer gap and the clarity and communication gap, because those two are the ones a family did not know were categories. Naming them tends to produce a long silence. Prepare for the silence rather than for the objection, and do not fill it.

Alignment is unglamorous work. It produces no document anyone frames and it rarely feels like a breakthrough while it is happening. What it produces is a family that does not get ambushed by itself three years from now, and the value of that is invisible precisely because the thing it prevented never occurred.

The families who do this well are not the ones who agree. They are the ones who know exactly where they disagree, and have decided that is workable.

The full treatment of this is a six-session title of its own, called Alignment.

Next session goes to the mechanism underneath all of it, which is that alignment requires conversation, and the conversations that matter most are the ones families are worst at having.
