SESSION 6

Building a Legacy That Lasts

Preparing the Next Generation Through Coaching

INTRODUCTION

KEY VERSE

"So the next generation would know them, and they in turn would tell their children."

PSALM 78:6-7

Everything in the previous five sessions was preparation for a question that only has one honest answer per family. Is anyone ready to receive this.

Not entitled to it. Not scheduled to receive it. Ready.

The uncomfortable part is that readiness is not produced by the transfer. A great many families operate as though handing something over is itself the preparation, and the evidence runs strongly the other way. Assets arrive, responsibility arrives with them, and the person receiving both discovers that nobody ever walked them through what any of it was for.

Tom's distinction here is worth holding precisely. Preparation is not exposure. A next generation can be present at every holiday, aware of the business, generally informed about the family's position, and still be entirely unprepared, because nothing was ever asked of them and nothing was ever explained to them.

The legacy is not just being transferred. It is being carried forward.

OPENING STORY

Two Families, One Assumption

Tom sets these two beside each other because the difference between them was a decision, not a circumstance.

The first family had built something substantial. Their working assumption about the next generation was that over time the children would figure it out. There was exposure. The children knew broadly what existed. Nobody was hiding anything and nobody was being negligent in any way they would have recognized.

What there was not was involvement. Nothing was asked of the next generation and nothing was systematically explained to them.

When the time came for greater responsibility, the gap showed immediately. The children did not feel prepared, and they were correct. The transition was harder than anyone had expected, and the difficulty was not financial. It was that people were being asked to carry something they had never been given a chance to understand.

The second family did the opposite thing, slowly.

They involved the next generation gradually. They created occasions for conversation, for understanding, for actual participation. Not everything at once, and not according to any elaborate program. Over years.

When responsibility arrived in that family, it arrived to people who were ready for it, and the difference showed up as confidence.

Same category of family. Same kind of resources. One assumed. One prepared.

TOM CONWAY

THE FRAMEWORK

Preparing the Next Generation

Seven steps, in order. The order carries most of the meaning, and the first two are the ones advisors skip.

  • Permission from Generation One. You do not approach the next generation without it. Going around the first generation, however well intentioned, ends the engagement and deserves to.

  • Confidentiality. Established explicitly, with everyone, before anything is discussed. The next generation will tell you things. What you do with those things determines whether anyone tells you anything again.

  • Getting to know the next generation. As people, before they are heirs. This takes actual time and cannot be compressed into the twenty minutes before a family meeting.

  • Interviewing them. Conducted with respect, care, and active listening. This is a structured conversation, not a chat, and it is the instrument described below.

  • Advocating for them. Carrying what you have learned back to the first generation in a way that represents the next generation fairly. This is the step that earns you their trust, and it will sometimes cost you a comfortable moment with the person who pays you.

  • Initial family meeting. The first time the whole system is in one room on purpose.

  • Training them. The long work. Financial understanding, yes, and also the purpose behind what exists.

The Six Areas of Life

Alongside the seven steps, Tom works six areas where a legacy coach can encourage growth in the next generation: spiritual life, family life, physical life, financial life, vocational life, and social life.

The reason to hold all six is that a next generation prepared only financially is prepared for the smallest part of what is coming. The failures that undo families are rarely failures of financial literacy.

THE TOOL

The Next Generation Interview

This is the fourth step of the framework and the instrument that makes the rest of it possible. Tom conducts it one to one, with permission from the first generation already secured and confidentiality already established.

The nine areas he covers:

  • Financial understanding
  • Family aspirations, including their own children
  • Career aspirations
  • Spiritual life
  • Family and friend relationships
  • Physical and health issues
  • Hobbies and fun
  • Their three greatest concerns in life
  • What would bring the greatest relief if it were solved today

Read that list again and notice its shape. Financial understanding is first and it is one of nine. Seven of the nine have nothing to do with the estate. The last two are the ones that produce the material, and they only work because the first seven established that this was a conversation about a person rather than an intake for a plan.

Posture. Respect, care, and active listening. A thirty-year-old being interviewed by his father's advisor arrives assuming he is being evaluated, and often assuming the conversation will be reported back. How you handle the first five minutes determines everything after it. Say plainly what is confidential and then be rigorous about it.

What you do with it. This is where advocating comes in. You carry back what the next generation actually needs the first generation to understand, in a form they can hear, and without breaching what was said in confidence. Doing that well is the hardest skill in this entire framework and it is the one that makes you something other than the first generation's representative.

Where to start. One family. One member of the next generation. Ninety minutes. Do not bring a document.

THIS WEEK'S READINGS

Five readings, one per day, from Family Legacy Coaching by Design by Tom Conway with Brett Eastman. [Page ranges pending final layout of the companion book.]

Day One: Engaging the Next Generation

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 11, opening sections

Why the next generation has to be engaged deliberately, and what happens in families where they are not.

Coaching insight. Read the failure cases first and slowly. Every one of them involves a family that was doing something reasonable at the time. The point is not that these families were careless, it is that reasonable behavior produces this outcome by default, which is why the alternative has to be chosen on purpose.

Day Two: The Interview

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 11, middle sections

How Tom conducts the interview, what he asks, and what he does with what he hears.

Coaching insight. Notice how much of the method is about establishing that this is not an evaluation. A next generation has usually been assessed by professionals their whole adult life without being asked anything. Being genuinely asked is unusual enough that it does most of the work by itself.

Day Three: Advocating and Training

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 11, later sections

Carrying the next generation's perspective back to the first generation, and the longer work of preparing them.

Coaching insight. Advocating is where this stops being comfortable. You will sometimes have to tell a first-generation client something about his own children that he does not want to hear, from a position where you were only able to learn it because they trusted you. Decide now how you will handle that, because deciding in the moment favors the person paying the invoice.

Day Four: Ongoing Monitoring

Family Legacy Coaching by Design, Chapter 14

Stage 3, and what it means to stay with a family across years rather than closing an engagement.

Coaching insight. This is the chapter that explains the retention argument from the other direction. A family reviewed annually across a decade, with progress recorded and gaps still named, is a relationship that survives a transition because the next generation has been in the room the whole time. Note the example of a family reviewed since 2016 and what had actually changed.

A legacy that lasts is not a larger legacy. It is one that somebody was prepared to receive, and preparation is unglamorous, slow, and mostly invisible while it is happening. It looks like a twenty-nine-year-old in a meeting she does not fully understand yet, asked a question she can answer, three years before it matters.

You are unusually well placed to cause that. Not because you have wisdom the family lacks, but because you are the one professional in their lives with standing to ask and continuity to keep asking, and because you will still be there in five years when the first conversation finally pays off.

That is the whole of it. Six sessions, one shift. You keep doing the technical work, and you stop assuming it is the only thing being transferred.

Where you go next depends on where the work is heaviest for you. The full treatments are Clarity, Alignment, Communication, Meetings, and Next Generation, and each one takes a session of this guide and gives it six of its own. Completing all of them, along with the Training Manual, is what leads to the Certified Family Legacy Coach credential.

Start with the one you avoided thinking about.
