Week Three of the devotional, Days 11 through 15

DAY 11

Every Family Operates From a Set of Values

Whether They've Defined Them or Not

"For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." MATTHEW 6:21

Every family operates from a set of values, whether they've defined them or not. Those values shape decisions, relationships, and direction over time.

I want to start there rather than with the case for clarifying values, because the case gets made badly and advisors are right to be skeptical of it. The pitch usually implies that a family without a values statement is a family without values, and that is not true and every client knows it is not true.

The families you serve have values. They are operating from them today. You can read them off their behavior more reliably than off any document they would produce in a workshop.

The problem is different and more specific. If those values aren't clear, decisions can feel inconsistent or disconnected.

Notice that the failure is not moral. It is functional. An undeclared value cannot be used. It still shapes the decision, but it does so privately, inside one person, and it is unavailable to anyone else in the family who is making a decision on the same subject next month. Five people can each be operating faithfully from what they believe the family stands for and produce five incompatible outcomes, and nobody in that family has done anything wrong.

So the goal is to bring clarity to what may already be there, but hasn't been clearly expressed.

That framing is the difference between a conversation a business owner will sit through and one he will not. You are not asking him to develop values. You are asking him to say out loud what has been running his decisions for thirty years, so his children can use it.

There is a principle in Matthew 6:21. Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.

I use that verse carefully, because it is usually deployed as an accusation and that is not what it says. The verse is diagnostic rather than condemning. It states a relationship. Locate the treasure and you have located the heart, which means the evidence of what a person values is already in the open and does not require anyone's confession.

That is a considerable gift to an advisor. You do not have to wait for a client to tell you what he values. You can see a great deal of it in what he protects, what he funds without being asked, what he will interrupt a vacation for, and what he has never once raised in eleven years of meetings.

I am not suggesting you diagnose a family and hand them the result. That is the fastest way to end this conversation. What I am suggesting is that you go into the values discussion already holding a hypothesis, drawn from behavior you have watched, which means you can ask a specific question rather than an abstract one.

Abstract: what are your family's core values.

Specific: I have noticed that every time this has come up, you have protected your sister's side of the family first. Is that something you would want your children to keep doing?

The second question produces a real conversation. The first produces a list of words that no family has ever consulted afterward.

A family's values are not missing. They are unstated, which is a much easier problem and a much smaller one.

REFLECT

1. Take one client. What values can you read off his behavior that he has never articulated?

2. An undeclared value cannot be used by anyone but the person holding it. Where has that produced incompatible decisions in a family you serve?

3. Where has a values conversation gone badly because it implied the family did not have any?

4. What is the specific question you could ask, drawn from something you have actually watched, rather than the abstract one?

DAY 12

Decisions Can Feel Inconsistent or Disconnected

Each Decision Was Approached Separately

"The plans of the diligent lead to profit as surely as haste leads to poverty." PROVERBS 21:5

I worked with a family that was making a number of important decisions. Financial ones, relational ones, decisions about future planning. Capable people, all of them engaged, none of them careless.

But they hadn't clearly defined what mattered most to them. So each decision was approached separately.

Sometimes they aligned. Sometimes they didn't.

And over time, that created some confusion and tension.

I want to slow down on the phrase over time, because it is where the damage actually lives and it is the reason nobody catches this early.

No single decision in that family was wrong. If you had reviewed any one of them in isolation you would have signed off. That is exactly the problem. A family deciding case by case is not making errors, it is accumulating small divergences, and divergence does not announce itself. It compounds quietly for years and then presents as a mood rather than as a problem. People start feeling that something is off in the family without being able to point to what.

By the time it surfaces, it has no traceable origin. Nobody can identify the decision that started it, because none of them did. It was the absence of a common reference across all of them.

That is why this cannot be diagnosed by looking at decisions. You have to look at the pattern, and families almost never look at the pattern, because there is no occasion in ordinary life where anyone lays out the last decade of their choices side by side.

You can create that occasion. It is one of the more useful things an advisor does and it requires no special skill.

There is a second cost worth naming, and it is the one that concerns me more in the long run. Children raised in a case-by-case family learn to litigate. If there is no shared reference, then every decision is settled by argument, persuasion, or whoever has the most standing in the room, and that is what the next generation learns about how the family resolves things. They will use it on each other after the parents are gone, and they will use it in a room with more money in it and less authority present.

There is a principle in Proverbs 21:5. The plans of the diligent lead to profit as surely as haste leads to poverty.

Haste in that verse is not speed. A family can be slow and still hasty in the sense that matters, which is deciding without reference to anything beyond the present question. Every decision that family made was unhurried and carefully considered on its own terms. All of it was haste, because none of it was measured against anything.

Diligence, in the way the verse uses it, is not working harder on each decision. It is having something to check each decision against.

Nobody in that family made a bad call. They made forty reasonable ones in forty different directions.

REFLECT

1. Which family in your book has made a decade of individually reasonable decisions that do not add up?

2. Divergence presents as a mood rather than a problem. Where have you heard a client describe a feeling that was actually a structural issue?

3. Children in a case-by-case family learn to litigate. What have the next generations you know learned about how decisions get settled?

4. Haste is deciding without reference to anything beyond the present question. Where are you hasty in that sense?

DAY 13

They Had a Reference Point

Does This Align With What We've Said Matters Most?

"Blessed are those who find wisdom, those who gain understanding, for she is more profitable than silver." PROVERBS 3:13-14

The other family had taken time to clarify their values. Not in a complicated way, but clearly enough that everyone understood what mattered most.

So when decisions came up, they had a reference point. They would ask, does this align with what we've said matters most?

And that made decisions much clearer.

I have thought a great deal about why that family functioned so differently from the first one, and the answer is smaller than I expected when I started this work.

It was not that their values were better. It was not that they were more unified as people, or more thoughtful, or more spiritual. What they had was a sentence available to them in the room.

That is the entire mechanism. A reference point is not a document and it is not a governance structure. It is a shared statement that any member of the family can invoke without having to establish it first.

Consider what that removes from a difficult conversation. Without a reference point, a person who objects to a decision has to argue from his own preference, which means he is putting himself against another family member, and the disagreement is personal by construction. With a reference point, he can ask a question about the decision instead. Does this line up with what we said. Now the disagreement is between the proposal and the standard, and no two people in the room have to be opposed to each other.

That is not a small structural change. It is the difference between a family that can disagree and a family that avoids the subject.

I want to be honest about the limitation. A reference point does not resolve anything by itself. Families will disagree about whether a decision aligns, and they should. What it does is give them something to disagree about other than each other, and over a period of years that difference compounds in the same way divergence compounds in the other direction.

The other thing I noticed about that family is what their statement was not. It was not elegant. Nobody had workshopped it. It came out of a conversation and it used their words, which is precisely why they could invoke it in the moment without feeling like they were quoting something.

A family will not consult a statement they would not have written themselves. I have watched beautifully drafted values documents sit unused for a decade because they sounded like the consultant who facilitated them.

There is a principle in Proverbs 3. Blessed are those who find wisdom, those who gain understanding, for she is more profitable than silver.

The verb is find. Not build, not acquire, not draft. Finding implies the thing was already present and the work was locating it.

That matches what actually happened in that family, and it is the reason clarity work is less intimidating than it sounds. Nobody constructed a set of values in a conference room. They took time to say what was already operating, and then they had it.

Clarity creates a framework for decision-making. That is all it does, and it turns out to be most of what a family needs.

REFLECT

3. A family will not consult a statement they would not have written themselves. What has been drafted for a family you serve that they have never once used?

4. The verb is find rather than build. What does that change about how you would open this conversation?

DAY 14

You're Not Trying to Force a List

You're Helping Them Discover What's Already There

"Be sure you know the condition of your flocks, give careful attention to your herds." PROVERBS 27:23

You start with conversation. Simple questions can open this up. What matters most to you. What do you want your family to be known for. What has shaped how you think about life and resources.

And then you listen for patterns.

You're not trying to force a list. You're helping them discover what's already there.

I have seen the forced-list version of this many times and I want to describe it, because it is what most advisors picture when they hear values work and it is why most of them decline to do it.

The forced version hands a family a sheet with forty words on it. Integrity, generosity, excellence, faith, stewardship. Everyone circles seven. The circles are compared, the overlap becomes the family's values, and someone produces a document. It takes ninety minutes and it feels productive.

It also produces nothing a family uses, and the reason is that nobody was asked to say anything. Selecting a word from a list is not the same act as articulating a conviction, and only one of those two leaves you able to explain yourself to your children afterward.

Discovery is slower and it works differently. You ask an open question, you get a story rather than a value, and you listen for what keeps recurring across the stories. A man tells you about his father's shop and a decision he made in 1987 and why he still will not carry debt on a property, and none of those are values statements, and all three are pointing at the same thing. The value is in the pattern, and he cannot see the pattern because he is inside it.

That is your actual contribution. Not the vocabulary. The pattern.

I will also say plainly that this takes longer than advisors expect and the timeline is not negotiable. I worked with a family on a charitable question where the reframe I was proposing sat with them for about eighteen months before they came to it. Nothing I could have said in month three would have moved that up. Patience there is part of the technique rather than a failure of it, and an advisor who cannot tolerate an eighteen-month arc will keep reaching for the list.

There is a principle in Proverbs 27:23. Be sure you know the condition of your flocks, give careful attention to your herds.

That is an instruction about attention rather than about action. Know the condition. Not improve it, not restructure it. Know it.

I find that verse steadying, because it describes the posture of this whole stage of the work. You are not building anything yet. You are finding out what is actually there, in detail, before anyone proposes a change, and the temptation in our profession is to skip that in favor of the part where we are visibly useful.

A family that discovers its values will use them. A family that selects them from a sheet will file them.

Definition comes after discovery, and it takes as long as it takes.

REFLECT

1. Where have you or someone you work with run the forced-list version? What happened to the document afterward?

2. The value is in the pattern and the client cannot see it because he is inside it. What pattern have you noticed in a client that you have never named to him?

3. An eighteen-month arc. Which of your relationships could sustain that, and what would you have to change in how you account for your time?

4. Know the condition rather than improve it. Where are you skipping straight to being useful?

DAY 15

Definition Comes After Discovery

You Listen for Patterns

"The beginning of wisdom is this: Get wisdom. Though it cost all you have, get understanding." PROVERBS 4:7

Definition comes after discovery.

That is the whole sequence and I state it flatly because reversing it is the most common way this work fails. Advisors who are uncomfortable with open-ended conversation will define first and then look for evidence, and what they produce is a plan the family agrees with and does not own.

Here is what the correct order actually feels like on the ground.

You begin to hear consistency. People describe what matters in similar ways. There's a shared understanding. And decisions start to reflect those values more clearly.

Notice the order inside that sentence. Consistency in language comes first. Shared understanding second. Decisions changing is last, and it is the one everyone wants to measure. Advisors under pressure to demonstrate progress will look for the third marker in month two, find nothing, and conclude the process is not working.

The first marker is the one to watch. It shows up as an ordinary thing. A daughter uses a phrase in a meeting that her father used six weeks earlier, and she is not quoting him, she is using it because it has become how the family describes the thing. That is the whole indicator. It is unremarkable and it is the point at which I know a family has actually taken hold of something.

I want to name the discipline this requires of you, because it is real.

Definition is the part of the work that feels like value. It produces a document, it can be presented, it justifies a fee, and it can be done in an afternoon. Discovery produces nothing you can show anyone, takes months, and looks from the outside like a series of pleasant conversations.

Every professional incentive you have points toward defining early. That is why the order gets reversed, and it is not because advisors do not understand the sequence.

I have one practical safeguard for this. When you write down what a family values, write it in their words, and then go back and look for anything that is yours. A phrase you introduced, a category you supplied, a word that sounds like it came from a book. Take those out. What remains is short and unpolished and it is the only part that will survive the meeting.

There is a principle in Proverbs 4:7. The beginning of wisdom is this: get wisdom. Though it cost all you have, get understanding.

The clause I return to is though it cost all you have. That is not a description of a bargain. It is an acknowledgment that understanding is expensive and that the cost is paid before the benefit is visible.

In this work the currency is time and it is charged to the advisor. Months of conversation with nothing to show a partner, and a client who has not yet signed anything, in exchange for something that only becomes obvious later.

Clarity is part of that understanding. It helps everything else make sense.

Discover first. Define second. There is no version of this where the order works the other way.

REFLECT

1. Where have you defined first and looked for evidence afterward? What did the family do with the result?

2. Consistency in language is the first marker and changed decisions is the last. Which one have you been measuring?

3. Every professional incentive points toward defining early. Which incentive is strongest for you, and can it be changed or only resisted?

4. Though it cost all you have. What has understanding actually cost you in one relationship, and was it worth it?
