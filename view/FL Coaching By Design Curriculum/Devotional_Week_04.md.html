Week Four of the devotional, Days 16 through 20

DAY 16

Alignment Doesn't Mean Everyone Thinks the Same

Enough Shared Understanding That People Can Move Forward Together

"Do two walk together unless they have agreed to do so?" AMOS 3:3

Every person in a family has their own experiences, their own perspective, and their own way of thinking. That's natural. But if those perspectives aren't brought together, it can create misalignment, even when the intentions are good.

Alignment doesn't mean everyone thinks the same. It means there's enough shared understanding that people can move forward together.

I want to spend the whole reading on that definition, because I have watched advisors set the wrong target for years and then conclude that alignment work does not succeed.

The wrong target is agreement. It is an understandable mistake, because agreement is visible and it can be documented and it makes a meeting feel finished. It is also the wrong thing to pursue in a family, and the reason is arithmetic rather than philosophy. A family of two parents and four married children is ten adults with ten histories, and the number of subjects on which all ten will genuinely converge is small. Aim at agreement and you will spend a career failing at something that was never available.

Enough shared understanding is a different target and it is achievable. Notice the word enough. Not complete. Not perfect. Enough that the next decision does not require relitigating the last one.

Here is how I explain the difference to a family, and it usually settles the room. A son can understand exactly why his father intends to leave the bulk of the estate to charity, be able to state the reasoning accurately, and still wish it were otherwise. That family is aligned. The decision can be made, implemented, and lived with, and nobody has to pretend. What that family avoids is the far more common situation where the son does not understand the reasoning, cannot state it, and fills the gap with a story about what his father thought of him.

Understanding does not require you to want the same thing. It requires you to know what the other person wants and why, accurately enough that you are not inventing a version of them.

There is a principle in Amos 3:3. Do two walk together unless they have agreed to do so?

I know how that reads against what I have just said. The verse appears to require agreement and I have spent five paragraphs arguing that agreement is the wrong target.

Look at what the agreement in the verse is actually about. It is not agreement about the destination in the sense of preferring it. It is agreement to walk together, which is a decision about the relationship rather than about the terms. Two people who have settled that they are going somewhere as a pair will negotiate the route for years. Two people who have not settled it will argue about the route forever and the argument will never be about the route.

That is alignment. It's about moving in the same direction.

I would add one caution, because this definition can be misused. Enough shared understanding is not a lower standard that lets a family skip the hard conversation. It is a different standard and in some ways a more demanding one. Agreement can be produced by pressure in an afternoon. Understanding cannot be produced by pressure at all, and it takes longer than most families expect, because it requires each person to say something true in front of people who will remember it.

I have seen families reach agreement in one meeting and come apart within two years. I have not yet seen that happen to a family that reached real understanding, even where the understanding included a significant unresolved difference.

The families who last are not the ones who want the same things. They are the ones who know what the others want.

REFLECT

1. Which of your families have you been trying to move toward agreement when understanding would have been the achievable target?

2. Enough shared understanding. What is enough, in a family you serve, and how would you know you had reached it?

3. A son who understands his father's reasoning and still wishes it were otherwise. Would your firm read that family as aligned or as a problem?

4. Agreement can be produced by pressure and understanding cannot. Where have you produced agreement under pressure, and what happened to it?

PRACTICE

Take one family with a decision ahead of them. Sort the items.

Most families put far too much in the first column. Look at your list again and move anything that does not require a signature.

For the largest item in the second column, write what each person would need to be able to state accurately about the others.

DAY 17

Often It's Assumption

They Assume Alignment Without Ever Really Talking About It

"Speaking the truth in love, we will grow to become in every respect the mature body of him who is the head, that is, Christ." EPHESIANS 4:15

Often it's assumption. People assume others see things the same way they do. Or they assume alignment without ever really talking about it.

Assumption is the quietest failure in this work and it is the one I look for first, because a family in open conflict at least knows it has something to handle. A family running on assumption believes it is fine, and will tell you so, warmly, for as long as you keep asking.

Here is a common one, and I raise it because it is so ordinary that most advisors never examine it.

Many families assume they will divide the charitable giving responsibilities equally among their children. It seems obviously fair. But what if one child is more charitably inclined than the others? What if one of three children shares your values and the other two do not?

I have had clients say to me, my kids do not share the same charitable values that my wife and I do, so I do not trust my kids to give this money away wisely. In those cases we have sometimes named someone outside the family, a close friend or an advisor who shares the parents' charitable vision, as the successor on the donor advised fund.

Notice what had to happen before that conversation was possible. Somebody had to stop assuming.

The default arrangement was equal division among the children, which nobody had chosen. It was inherited from a general sense of fairness and it had never been tested against what anyone actually believed or wanted. Once it was examined, it turned out to be wrong for that family, and the fix was straightforward. The difficulty was never the fix. It was that nobody had looked.

I want to name why assumptions survive as long as they do, because it is not laziness.

An assumption is comfortable in proportion to how much is riding on it. The larger the consequence of being wrong, the less anyone wants to check, and checking has an implied accusation inside it. A father who examines whether his children share his charitable values is entertaining the possibility that they do not, which is not a pleasant afternoon. It is far easier to assume it and file the documents accordingly, and the documents will not object.

The cost lands later, on people who were not in the room when it was decided.

There is a principle in Ephesians 4:15. Speaking the truth in love, we will grow to become in every respect the mature body.

The pairing in that verse is what makes it usable. Speaking the truth alone is available to anyone and frequently does damage. Love alone, in the sense most families practice it, is what produces the assumption in the first place, because not raising the hard thing feels like kindness.

What the verse describes is a family that does both at once, and it attaches an outcome to it. Growth. Maturity. Not comfort, and not the absence of difficulty.

Here is what I would ask you to do with this. Take one family and identify the arrangement nobody chose. Every family has one. Equal division, the oldest running the business, the daughter handling the parents' care, the house going to whoever wants it. These arrangements arrived by default and have never been said out loud, and the family will describe them as obvious.

Then ask, gently and once, whether that is what they actually want.

Most of the time they will say yes, and you will have cost them ten minutes and given them something they now hold deliberately. Occasionally they will pause, and that pause is worth more than everything else you did that year.

Nobody in these families is being careless. They are being reasonable about something they have never examined.

REFLECT

1. What arrangement in one of your client families arrived by default and has never been said out loud?

2. An assumption is comfortable in proportion to how much is riding on it. Which of your own assumptions about a family are you least willing to test?

3. Checking has an implied accusation inside it. How would you raise the charitable succession question without sounding like a judgment on anyone's children?

4. Not raising the hard thing feels like kindness. Where has that been true in your own practice this year?

DAY 18

They All Cared About the Family

But They Weren't Actually Aligned

"The goal of this command is love, which comes from a pure heart and a good conscience and a sincere faith." 1 TIMOTHY 1:5

I worked with a family where this became very clear. The parents felt like they had been clear about what mattered most and where things were going.

The children, on the other hand, had very different interpretations.

They all cared about the family. But they weren't actually aligned. And because those differences hadn't been discussed openly, they showed up later in decisions, and created tension.

I want to hold on the phrase they all cared, because advisors skip past it and it is the most important sentence in the story.

This is not a family with a villain. Nobody was scheming, nobody was entitled, nobody had checked out. Every person in that house would have told you the family mattered more to them than the money, and every one of them would have been telling the truth.

That is precisely what made the situation dangerous. When everyone cares, everyone assumes good faith, and the assumption of good faith removes the last reason anyone would think to verify. Why would you check whether your children understand you when you know they love you and you know they know you love them?

Caring is not alignment. They are unrelated variables and a family can be high on one and empty on the other, and the family that cares deeply and is not aligned is in a worse position than the family that is openly at odds, because it has no early warning.

I have come to believe the affection actually accelerates the problem. In a family where people care a great deal, nobody wants to be the one who raises the uncomfortable question, because raising it feels like a small betrayal of the warmth. So the differences sit, unstated, and each person handles them privately by constructing an interpretation that lets them keep loving everyone.

Those private interpretations are the mechanism. The parents believed they had been clear. Each child had built a reading that accounted for what they had observed. Four people were operating from four different accounts of the same family, all sincerely, and none of them had any reason to suspect it.

The differences do not stay dormant. They surface later, attached to a decision, at a moment when there is money on the table and a deadline attached, and by then they are not differences anymore. They are positions.

There is a principle in 1 Timothy 1:5. The goal of this command is love, which comes from a pure heart and a good conscience and a sincere faith.

The word I want is goal. The verse does not treat sincerity as the finish line. Sincerity is one of the sources feeding into something else, and the something else is the objective.

That distinction is exactly what these families miss. Everyone in that house had a sincere heart and a good conscience about the family, and they treated the sincerity as though it were the outcome. It was not. The outcome is a family that actually functions together, and sincerity is an input to it rather than a substitute for it.

Here is the practical consequence for you. When a client tells you his family is close, take him at his word and then ask a second question anyway. Closeness is real and it is not evidence of alignment. The two things feel identical from the inside, which is why nobody in the family can tell you which one they have.

You can find out in an afternoon. It takes asking each of them separately what they believe is going to happen, and it will be the most uncomfortable hour you spend that quarter and the most useful.

The tension in that family was not a failure of love. It was a failure to check.

REFLECT

1. Which of your client families is high on caring and untested on alignment?

2. Affection removes the last reason anyone would verify. Where have you accepted closeness as evidence?

3. Four people operating from four sincere accounts of the same family. Whose account have you only heard one version of?

4. Sincerity is an input rather than the outcome. Where in your own life have you treated meaning well as though it were the finish line?

DAY 19

Not to Force Agreement, But to Build Understanding

Agreement Often Comes Later

"Be like-minded, having the same love, being one in spirit and of one mind." PHILIPPIANS 2:2

So we created space for conversation. Not to force agreement, but to build understanding.

Each person shared how they were thinking. What mattered to them. How they saw the future.

Agreement often comes later.

That last line is the one I want an advisor to hold, because it is doing two things at once and both matter.

The first is a promise to the family. Agreement often comes later means it is coming. You are not asking them to abandon the decision or to talk indefinitely about feelings. There is a destination and you are telling them the route.

The second is a constraint on you. Later means not today, and an advisor who says the words and then steers toward resolution in the last twenty minutes has broken the thing he just promised. Families notice that immediately and they do not raise it. They simply do not tell you the next true thing.

So I say it out loud at the start, and I say it plainly. Nothing is being decided today. We are here to understand each other, and the decisions will come after that.

I have watched the temperature in a room drop within thirty seconds of that sentence. People arrive at these conversations braced, because in their experience raising a difference means resolving it or damaging something. Removing the requirement to resolve removes most of what they were bracing against.

There is a mechanical reason this works and it is worth understanding rather than just copying.

When a decision is on the table, everything a person says becomes a move. State your view and you have taken a position, and taking a position invites a counter-position, and now there is a negotiation happening whether or not anyone wanted one. Family members who sense this coming will either overstate to establish room or say nothing at all, and both of those are rational responses to a negotiation.

Take the decision off the table and speaking is no longer a move. A person can say what they actually think without it becoming a bid. That is the only condition under which you get accurate information about what people want, and accurate information is the entire input to everything downstream.

There is a principle in Philippians 2:2. Be like-minded, having the same love, being one in spirit and of one mind.

That verse gets used to argue for uniformity and I do not read it that way. That's not about sameness. It's about shared direction and unity.

One mind in a family does not mean four people who think identically. It means four people pointed at the same thing, who have each said out loud what they see from where they stand.

I would add a caution, because this posture can be misused as easily as it can be applied. Building understanding is not a way of avoiding the hard conversation indefinitely. There are families and there are advisors who find the understanding stage comfortable precisely because nothing has to be settled in it, and a process that never arrives at a decision has failed at the other end.

Later has to actually arrive. Put a date on it in the room, before anyone leaves.

You are not delaying the decision. You are making it possible to make one that holds.

REFLECT

1. Nothing is being decided today. Could you say that in your next family meeting and mean it, including in the last twenty minutes?

2. When a decision is on the table, everything said becomes a move. Where have you watched a family member overstate to establish room?

3. One mind as shared direction rather than sameness. How would you explain that difference to a client who reads unity as everyone agreeing?

4. Some advisors find the understanding stage comfortable because nothing has to be settled. Is that a risk for you, honestly?

DAY 20

But Now They Could See It

Once You Can See It, You Can Work With It

"Do not merely listen to the word, and so deceive yourselves. Do what it says." JAMES 1:22

As those conversations happened, something became clear. There were areas of alignment, and areas where they were not aligned.

But now they could see it.

And once you can see it, you can work with it.

I once worked with a family worth over a billion dollars. They had a family office with four employees, three attorneys and two CPAs on retainer, and an annual planning meeting. When I attended one of those meetings, I realized I was the only person in the room who had ever talked to their children.

This family had 75 trusts. All the lawyers were trustees. Nobody had communicated with the kids. The family office leader admitted they had the children sign their tax returns without letting them see the numbers. A sign here approach.

I told them, you need to start talking to your kids. They need to know what you own and how to manage it when you are gone.

It took time, but they finally agreed. We brought in their company CFO, held meetings with the family, and explained everything. Twenty-five apartment complexes, real estate, golf courses, other businesses. They also had four hundred million dollars in life insurance.

It was a huge step, and it prepared the next generation for what was coming.

What I want you to notice is where the difficulty actually sat. It was not complexity. Seventy-five trusts is complicated and it is not the problem, because complexity can be explained by competent people and that family had more competent people than most. The problem was that the situation was invisible to the people it was going to land on, and invisible things cannot be worked on by anyone.

Once it was in the open, the work became ordinary. Bring in the CFO. Walk through the holdings. Explain what a trust is, who the grantor is, what a trustee does, what a beneficiary is entitled to. None of that is difficult. It had simply never been done, and it had never been done because nobody had said out loud that it needed doing.

That is the same mechanism as the family with the four different interpretations, at a different scale. Something real was operating that nobody could see, and the first act was not solving it. The first act was making it visible.

I would put it this way to an advisor. Your first deliverable in alignment work is not a recommendation. It is a picture. Here is where this family agrees. Here is where it does not. Here is what nobody has said. A family holding that picture will do a surprising amount of the work themselves, and a family without it will keep circling something they cannot name.

There is a principle in James 1:22. Do not merely listen to the word, and so deceive yourselves. Do what it says.

The verse is about the distance between seeing and acting, and it is blunt about what filling that distance with nothing does to a person. Self-deception. Not error. Deception, which implies you have been told.

That applies to a family that has now seen its own map and decides to leave it there. It applies more sharply to us. An advisor who runs the conversation, produces the picture, watches the family recognize it, and then never converts it into a decision has done something worse than not starting, because now they know and nothing followed.

Seeing it is the first half. The verse is about the second.

REFLECT

1. Which of your families is circling something they cannot name? What would the picture look like if you drew it?

2. Complexity is not the problem, invisibility is. Where have you assumed a family could not handle the detail when nobody had ever tried?

3. A sign here approach. Where does something like that exist in a family you serve, in a smaller form?

4. Seeing without acting is self-deception rather than error. What have you seen in a client family and not converted into anything?
