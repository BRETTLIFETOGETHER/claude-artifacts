Fifty-shot photography brief so a church can produce its own campaign images

FIFTY SHOTS

Photography brief for a local church campaign

40 Days of Generosity · LifeTogether

Why this exists instead of a stock library

The word "custom" in custom church photos is doing all the work. A congregation recognizes its own lobby, its own people, its own city — and recognizes stock photography instantly, at which point the campaign becomes something imported rather than something theirs.

So this is a shot list, not an image set. One photographer, two or three shooting days, roughly fifty usable frames. That is a realistic weekend for a volunteer with a decent camera and produces something no purchased library can.

A note on what I can't do. I can't generate photographs. What follows is the brief that makes fifty real ones happen.

Direction

Look. Available light. Real rooms. Nobody looking at the lens. Slightly underexposed rather than bright and even — the campaign's palette is navy and cream, and blown-out highlights fight it.

Distance. Closer than feels comfortable. Hands, faces at three-quarter, the edge of a conversation. Wide establishing shots read as real estate photography.

Never. Posed groups arranged by height. Anyone giving a thumbs-up. Money in frame — no cash, no offering plates, no checks, no card readers. That last rule is not aesthetic; a campaign that promises nobody will be asked cannot put currency in its own artwork.

Consent. Written release for every recognizable person, and a separate one for every minor signed by a parent. No exceptions, and keep the file.

THE FIFTY

Hands and small gestures (1–8)

  1. Two hands passing a dish across a table
  2. An older hand and a child's hand, resting, not posed
  3. Hands opening — palms up, on a lap
  4. A closed fist relaxing, shot as a sequence of three
  5. Hands holding a worn Bible, thumb in the margin
  6. A hand writing in a journal, pen visible, words illegible
  7. Hands stacked in prayer around a table edge
  8. A hand on someone's shoulder from behind

Faces (9–16)

  1. Someone mid-listen, not mid-speak
  2. A man in his sixties, three-quarter, thinking
  3. A woman laughing at something off-frame
  4. A teenager, unguarded, no phone
  5. Two people in conversation, both in focus
  6. Someone with their eyes closed in a service
  7. An older couple, side by side, not touching
  8. A child watching an adult

Homes and tables (17–26)

  1. A dinner table mid-meal, plates real, phone-free
  2. A small group in a living room, from the doorway
  3. Coffee cups on a side table, nobody in frame
  4. A kitchen island with people leaning on it
  5. Three jars on a windowsill — give, save, spend
  6. A child putting a coin in a jar
  7. A family calendar on a refrigerator
  8. A front door opening, host greeting
  9. Chairs pulled into an uneven circle
  10. Someone's handwriting on a card, close

The church building (27–34)

  1. Lobby before the doors open, empty
  2. Lobby full, from above if possible
  3. The room from the back row
  4. A volunteer setting up, seen from behind
  5. A group meeting in a classroom
  6. A door with a handwritten sign
  7. Kids ministry mid-activity, real chaos
  8. The parking lot at dusk

The city and the work (35–42)

  1. The church's neighborhood, wide, recognizable
  2. A local ministry the church funds, working
  3. Someone from the church serving off-campus
  4. A local partner's hands at work — a kitchen, a build, a classroom
  5. A street the congregation drives every week
  6. The place a church-funded gift went, whatever it is
  7. Someone receiving something, shot with dignity — no distress, no pity framing
  8. A volunteer and a recipient talking as equals

Generations (43–47)

  1. Three generations in one frame, unarranged
  2. A grandparent telling a story, listener in soft focus
  3. A framed old family photograph on a wall
  4. A child's drawing pinned somewhere
  5. Someone showing a photo on a phone to someone else

Texture and space (48–50)

  1. Overhead flat lay — reader, pen, coffee, reading glasses
  2. Empty chairs, late light
  3. A window with rain or morning sun, nobody in frame

Coverage requirements

Every shot in three orientations where possible: horizontal for slides, vertical for print and social, square for tiles. That turns fifty setups into a usable library rather than fifty single-use frames.

Cast across the whole congregation. If the fifty images show one age band, one income level, or one ethnicity, the campaign teaches something you did not intend and the congregation will read it correctly. Audit the take before you build anything.

Ten of the fifty must include no people at all. Rooms, tables, textures, light. Those become the backgrounds that let type breathe, and they are the shots most often forgotten on the day.

Post-production

Duotone in navy for anything with type over it. Full-colour photography behind text fights the palette every time.

Full colour, unretouched, for standalone images. No filters, no heavy grading, no skin smoothing.

Deliver a shared folder organized by section, filenames matching the shot numbers above, releases stored alongside.

A note on the working name. If the campaign ships as Crazy Generosity, this photography direction is wrong — that name calls for brighter, faster, more energetic imagery, and it also puts the word crazy in front of a congregation that includes people managing real mental health conditions. The name also carries the same problem as irrational: it concedes that the giving makes no sense. Worth settling the name before the shoot, since the photography follows it.
