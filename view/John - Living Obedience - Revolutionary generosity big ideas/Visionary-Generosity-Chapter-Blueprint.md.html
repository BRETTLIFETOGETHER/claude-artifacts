Building the chapter-by-chapter blueprint — the next level down from the Visionary Generosity outline

VISIONARY GENEROSITY

Chapter Blueprint — the level below the outline

Steve French with Dale Armstrong — The Signatry Status: developing. Working title. 16 chapters, 4 parts, 64,000 words.

HOW TO USE THIS DOCUMENT

The outline said what each chapter argues. This says how each chapter is built — where it opens, the beats in order, the sentence it turns on, the line it exists to deliver, the objection it must answer, and where it lands. A writer should be able to draft from this without asking another question.

Each chapter carries a word budget. They are uneven on purpose: the argument chapters run long, the story chapters run short, and chapter 16 is deliberately the shortest in the book.

THE READER'S ARC

Most book outlines map the argument and ignore the reader. This one moves through five emotional states, in order, and the chapter sequence exists to produce them.

Recognition (1–3). That's me, and I didn't have words for it. The reader has been faithful and has plateaued. He is met without accusation.

Discomfort (4–6). Some of what I've built was for me. The empire chapter lands here on purpose, early enough to establish that the authors will tell the truth and late enough that the reader trusts them.

Relief (7–10). This is available to me, and it's not about being a better person. The mechanics and the craft. The reader stops feeling judged and starts feeling equipped.

Aspiration (11–14). I could actually do this. Competence turns into ambition.

Resolve (15–16). We're going to do this, and here's what finished looks like. The family and the finish.

Read the sequence backward and it fails: mechanics before conviction produces a technical manual, and the empire chapter after the relief section reads as a scolding at the end of a good meal.

RUNNING ELEMENTS

French's own arc is distributed, not front-loaded. The failure appears in chapter 1, the exit regret in chapter 13, and the reason he took the job in chapter 16. Telling it all at once turns the book into a memoir; spacing it makes the reader feel accompanied.

The two-engine diagnostic recurs four times — introduced in chapter 1, applied in 4, 8, and 15. Same five questions, different stakes each time. It becomes the book's assessment product without additional writing.

Story rotation. Four types — historical patrons, mid-century industrialists, living families, and the authors. No two consecutive chapters lead with the same type.

One financial metaphor per chapter, maximum. Assigned below. Chapter 16 gets none.

Every chapter ends on a question, not an exhortation. Sixteen questions become the study guide.

PART I — THE TWO ENGINES

1. Big Hearts, Big Vision — 4,000 words

Opens with: two charts side by side. A man's net worth over twenty years, climbing. His annual giving over the same twenty years, nearly flat. He is not a hypocrite. He tithed every one of those years.

Beats 1. The plateau, described without accusation. Why: if the reader feels indicted in the first five pages he never reaches the argument. 2. French's failure — the business that nearly erased his liquid net worth, and the lesson that he had thought he was both steward and owner. Why: the authors pay first. Nothing else in the book earns credibility as cheaply. 3. The two engines named and drawn. Why: the whole book depends on the reader believing these are separate faculties. 4. The four quadrants: faithful-and-scattered, big-talk-and-deferred, neither, both. Why: a reader who can locate himself will keep reading; a reader in general will not. 5. The diagnostic, first pass. Why: converts a concept into self-knowledge on page 20.

The turn: You do not have a generosity problem. You have one engine.

The line: Vision without obedience produces talk. Obedience without vision produces faithfulness that never becomes catalytic.

Story: French (anchor). Metaphor: two engines — used literally here and never labored again. Scripture: Proverbs 29:18; 1 Chronicles 29:11–14. Objection: Isn't this just telling rich people they should give more? Answer in the chapter: no — it is telling faithful people why their giving stopped growing. Exercise: The diagnostic. Ends on: Which engine is idling in you?

2. What Do You See? — 3,800 words

Opens with: a donor at lunch, asked what he actually cares about, unable to answer after forty-five seconds — and the discovery that no one had ever asked him.

Beats 1. God's question to the prophets is what do you see, not what will you do. Why: establishes vision as a spiritual faculty rather than a strategic exercise, which is what keeps this from being a business book. 2. Sight precedes assignment, every time — Jeremiah's branch, Amos's plumb line, Zechariah's lampstand. Why: three instances make it a pattern rather than a proof text. 3. Generic sight produces generic giving. Why: names the mechanism by which enormous capacity ends up funding whoever writes the best appeal. 4. Monmouth: a cloth merchant who saw an English Bible before one existed. Why: the reader needs to see that vision is available to a businessman, not just to a prophet.

The turn: If you cannot name what you see, you will fund whoever asks best.

The line: Vision is not a strategy exercise. It is a form of sight, and God has been handing it out to merchants for a very long time.

Story: Monmouth (anchor); Tyndale in the room upstairs. Alternate: a Signatry family whose vision came from one trip. Metaphor: none — this chapter runs on Scripture and story. Resist the urge. Scripture: Jeremiah 1:11–13; Amos 7:7–8; Zechariah 4:1–6; Habakkuk 2:2–3. Objection: I'm not a visionary person. Answer: neither was the cloth merchant. Exercise: The sight inventory — three things seen in five years you could not stop thinking about. Ends on: What have you seen that you have never done anything about?

3. The Yes Underneath — 4,200 words

Opens with: Alan Barnhart at twenty-four, a legal pad, and Matthew 19 — two years before he changed anything about the business.

Beats 1. Obedience is ob-audire, to listen toward — proximity, not compliance. Why: the reader flinches at the word; the etymology defuses it in one paragraph rather than three chapters. 2. Decisions made before wealth survive wealth. Why: this is the chapter's transferable claim and it works at every income level, which the book badly needs by chapter 3. 3. The three settling decisions: ownership in writing, a lifestyle number, cap-and-float. Why: concrete practices this early establish that the book will deliver mechanics, not just conviction. 4. Public declaration as a spiritual technology — Barnhart telling new hires their labor would not fund his lifestyle, and being locked in once he'd said it a few times. Why: gives the reader something to do this week that costs nothing and changes everything.

The turn: The question was never how much. It was whether the answer had already been settled.

The line: A percentage answers the question so you never have to ask again. That is useful at thirty and anesthetic at sixty.

Story: Barnhart (anchor). Alternate: LeTourneau pledging from underneath the loss. Metaphor: the pre-authorized transaction — a decision made once that executes without re-deliberation. Scripture: 1 Samuel 15:22; 2 Corinthians 8:5. Objection: Setting a number feels legalistic. Answer: a ceiling is freedom; a floor was the rule. Exercise: Name the number. Name the review date. Tell two people. Ends on: What are you actually afraid of?

4. When Vision Runs Without Obedience — 3,600 words

Opens with: a building with a family name carved above the door, and the honest question of who it was for.

Beats 1. The failure mode of visionary giving is not stinginess; it is empire. Why: the reader has watched this happen and has never seen it named in a Christian book. Naming it buys the authors enormous trust. 2. Ambition wearing Kingdom vocabulary — the initiative that exists because the donor wanted it. Why: specific enough to sting, general enough that no one is identified. 3. Four tests: is anyone else convinced, does it cost you something you'd keep, would you fund it anonymously, who is allowed to tell you no. Why: converts a warning into a usable instrument. 4. Ananias and Sapphira, read as a vision-without-obedience story rather than a giving story. Why: the text is about the gap between the presented self and the actual one, which is precisely this chapter.

The turn: The danger for people like you is not that you will give too little. It is that you will give impressively.

The line: Vision that is never told no becomes ambition with a Scripture reference attached.

Story: composite and unnamed, deliberately. Alternate: a foundation that outlived its purpose and funds its own staff. Metaphor: governance — every owner knows an unchecked founder eventually consumes the company. Scripture: Matthew 6:1–4; Acts 5:1–11. Objection: Are you saying named giving is wrong? No. Say so, then explain what makes the difference. Exercise: Identify the person who is permitted to tell you no. If there isn't one, that is the chapter's finding. Ends on: Who tells you no?

PART II — WHERE VISION COMES FROM

5. You Can't Fund What You Haven't Seen — 3,400 words

Opens with: a trip the reader's wife made him take.

Beats 1. Distance funds presentation; proximity funds reality. Why: explains why sophisticated donors so often fund mediocre work — they are evaluating decks, not people. 2. What changes when you have met them. Why: moves the argument from information to relationship, which is where patronage actually lives. 3. Bring the children. Why: plants the generational thread eleven chapters before it flowers.

The turn: You did not need better analysis. You needed to be in the room. The line: Donors who fund from a distance fund what is well presented. Donors who have been in the room fund what is working.

Story: Trogden and the used medical equipment bound for Haiti — a gift that required proximity to imagine and nothing extraordinary to make. Alternate: Nehemiah riding out at night to look at the walls before saying a word. Metaphor: diligence. Nobody here would buy a company from a deck alone. Scripture: Nehemiah 2:11–18; Luke 10:33. Objection: I don't have time to visit everything I fund. Then fund less, better. Exercise: Schedule one visit before the next gift. Ends on: When did you last stand where your money goes?

6. The Size of the Assignment — 3,800 words

Opens with: two families of nearly identical means, one giving at four figures and one at seven, and the single variable that explains it.

Beats 1. People give in proportion to what they believe is at stake, not to what they have. Why: this is the most financially consequential sentence in the book for any ministry leader who reads it. 2. Enlarging a donor's vision is therefore the most valuable thing a leader can do — and almost nobody attempts it. Why: turns the chapter into the one that ministry leaders photocopy. 3. How to size an assignment honestly: name the problem, name what finished looks like, name what it costs, inflate none of the three. Why: the guardrail that keeps chapter 6 from teaching donors to be manipulated. 4. Warren's willingness to reorganize a congregation's ambition around global scale. Why: the only church-side example in Part II, and the reader needs one.

The turn: Your giving is not capped by your capacity. It is capped by the largest thing anyone has invited you into. The line: A family worth fifty million with a vision the size of a building campaign will give like a family worth five.

Story: Warren, from published material only. Alternate: the Signatry vision statement, introduced here and paid off in chapter 16. Metaphor: total addressable market — the number that determines how much capital a business can absorb. Scripture: Isaiah 54:2; Ephesians 3:20. Objection: This sounds like teaching ministries to ask bigger. Distinguish enlarging vision from inflating need. The difference is honesty about cost. Exercise: Write down the largest thing you have ever been personally invited to fund. Notice how small it is. Ends on: What is the biggest thing anyone has ever asked you to be part of?

7. God's Rate of Return — 4,000 words (drafted)

Opens with: a sixty-three-year-old, eleven months from selling a company he started in a spare bedroom, asking how much he should give.

Beats 1. How much should I give is a subtraction question. Why: one sentence that reframes the reader's entire posture, and the reason this chapter is the sample. 2. Scripture's own vocabulary for giving is return, not loss. Why: pre-empts the objection that return language is an accommodation. 3. The sower: the seed is identical, the soil varies, and the multiple is a function of the ground. Why: the book's original exegetical contribution, and it converts the question from how much to where. 4. The guardrail — the Macedonians in poverty, LeTourneau's loss, Buford's son. Why: placed mid-chapter, not appended. A return chapter that admits loss is trustworthy; one that doesn't is prosperity teaching. 5. The three multipliers within the reader's control. Why: lands the chapter in arithmetic rather than aspiration.

The turn: He was not being asked to take a loss. He was being asked to reallocate. The line: Same seed, same sincerity, same amount — and a return that runs from zero to a hundred based on the ground it lands in.

Story: Barnhart's give-half-reinvest-half engine. Alternate: LeTourneau's increments to ninety percent. Metaphor: reinvestment rate. Scripture: Matthew 13:1–23; 2 Corinthians 8:1–5; 1 Timothy 6:17–19. Objection: This is prosperity teaching in a suit. Answered in beat 4, at length. Exercise: Trace one gift from five years ago. Most readers cannot. That is the finding. Ends on: Is your instinct subtraction or allocation?

8. Vision Is Given, Not Generated — 3,400 words

Opens with: Lady Huntingdon, who could not preach, in a drawing room full of aristocrats who would never stand in a field.

Beats 1. You do not brainstorm a calling. Why: releases the reader from the exhausting search for passion, which is the most common private failure among wealthy givers. 2. Vision arrives through position, history, wound, or proximity. Why: four concrete channels beat one abstract exhortation. 3. Fund where you already have access, expertise, or grief. Why: turns limitation into assignment. 4. The diagnostic, second pass — now against the sight inventory from chapter 2.

The turn: The thing you have been trying to discover has been sitting in your own biography. The line: She could not preach. So she filled a room with people who would never have come to a field, and handed them Whitefield.

Story: Huntingdon (anchor). Alternate: Heald, whose global work grew out of a missionary childhood and a real estate career. Metaphor: unfair advantage — every investor knows the returns come from where you already have an edge. Scripture: Esther 4:14; 1 Corinthians 12:4–7. Objection: This feels like settling for what's convenient. Convenient and given are not the same thing; one costs nothing and the other usually costs everything. Exercise: The access-expertise-grief inventory. Ends on: What has God already put you next to?

PART III — THE PATRON'S CRAFT

9. Back the Person, Not the Project — 3,600 words

Opens with: John Newton before he was John Newton — an unknown curate with no pulpit and a drawer of unpublished verse.

Beats 1. Projects are fundable and forgettable; people compound. Why: the single most useful reallocation instruction in the book. 2. What patronage includes besides money — placement, counsel, rest, access, protection. Why: makes patronage available to readers whose capacity is relational rather than financial. 3. Stay through the bad year. Why: distinguishes a patron from a grantmaker, which is the chapter's whole point. 4. Fewer people, longer, with fewer strings.

The turn: Thornton did not fund Newton's work. He funded Newton. The line: Grant cycles cannot do this. Patrons can.

Story: Thornton and Newton — the placement, the counsel, the push to publish the hymns. Alternate: Buford funding leaders rather than programs. Metaphor: the cap table — you back founders, not features. Scripture: Philippians 1:3–7; 3 John 5–8. Objection: Isn't unrestricted funding irresponsible? Answer: it is what you do after trust is earned, not before. Exercise: Name three people, not three organizations. Ends on: Whose best work is still ahead of them?

10. Fund Before It's Provable — 3,400 words

Opens with: 1526, and the question of whether to fund a translation that is illegal.

Beats 1. By the time the work is proven, your money is no longer the decisive money. Why: reframes risk as the patron's actual product. 2. Patrons supply risk capital; institutions supply confirmation capital. Why: gives the reader a defined role rather than a vague call to courage. 3. What it can cost — Monmouth's year in the Tower. Why: the book must not sell risk cheaply. 4. A defined risk allocation with a stated tolerance for loss.

The turn: The gift that mattered was made when nobody could prove it would work. The line: He paid for the Bible you own, and he paid for it in a cell.

Story: Monmouth again, at greater depth than chapter 2. Alternate: an early Signatry family who funded something unproven. Metaphor: seed round. Everyone here knows where the returns come from and what the loss rate is. Scripture: Ecclesiastes 11:1–6; Hebrews 11:8. Objection: I have a fiduciary duty to be careful. With your family's provision, yes. With the portion already given away, that duty is different. Exercise: Set a risk allocation for next year. Name the percentage and the loss you will accept. Ends on: What would you fund if you did not need to be proven right?

11. Fund the Unglamorous Middle — 3,200 words

Opens with: year seven of a translation project. No launch, no ribbon, no photographs. Just work.

Beats 1. Most philanthropy is allocated by fundraising skill. Why: names an uncomfortable truth the reader has suspected and never heard stated. 2. Launches and buildings attract money; sustaining years do not. Why: explains why good work dies at exactly the moment it is about to succeed. 3. Patience is the patron's structural advantage over institutional grantmaking. Why: turns a critique into a competitive edge the reader can act on. 4. Ask what breaks if funding stops in year four — and fund that.

The turn: Nobody is competing with you for year seven. The line: The best people working on a problem are rarely the ones with the best development department.

Story: the Barnhart giving group, researching recipients and looking for effectiveness rather than polish. Alternate: a ministry that nearly closed in year six. Metaphor: the J-curve — every operator knows the trough before the return. Scripture: Galatians 6:9; 1 Corinthians 3:6–8. Objection: How do I know it's working if there's nothing to show? Fair. Answer with relationship and proximity, not metrics theater. Exercise: Ask three organizations you fund what would break in year four. Ends on: What are you funding that nobody is photographing?

12. Stay Behind the Curtain — 3,000 words

Opens with: three verses in Luke 8, and the women who funded the ministry of Jesus out of their own means.

Beats 1. Recognition is the tax that quietly consumes the vision. Why: connects back to chapter 4 without repeating it — there the problem was ambition, here it is judgment. 2. Anonymity is operational protection, not false humility. Why: reframes a spiritual discipline as a practical one, which this reader will accept faster. 3. Tell the decision, not the number. Why: resolves the genuine tension between hiddenness and the contagion that spreads generosity. 4. The names history kept and the names it didn't.

The turn: The moment a gift requires acknowledgement, the assignment quietly changes from the work to you. The line: We are still singing what Thornton paid for. Almost nobody has heard of Thornton.

Story: the women of Luke 8 (anchor). Alternate: Huntingdon and Thornton, whose names most Christians have never heard. Metaphor: none. This chapter should be plain. Scripture: Luke 8:1–3; Matthew 6:3–4. Objection: Public giving encourages others. True — which is why the rule is the decision goes public and the amount stays private. Exercise: Make one gift this year that no one will attribute to you. Ends on: Whose name is on the thing you funded?

PART IV — BUILDING A FAMILY THAT CAN SAY YES

13. Capacity: Give the Asset, Not the Proceeds — 4,200 words

Opens with: a man's arithmetic. To net the gift and cover the tax, he needs to sell two rental properties.

Beats 1. Vision outruns cash, always. Why: connects Part IV to Parts II and III so the mechanics read as obedience rather than tax strategy. 2. Nine-tenths of wealth sits outside the checking account. Why: the number that reframes the reader's sense of his own capacity. 3. The sequence is the gift — give-then-sell versus sell-then-give, with real arithmetic. Why: teach it once, mechanically. This is the page advisors will photocopy. 4. French's own regret. Why: the authors pay again, and at the point of maximum usefulness. 5. Where the window closes — often before a letter of intent.

The turn: He did not become more generous that afternoon. He became better informed. The line: You cannot obey a large prompt with a small checkbook.

Story: Haverkamp (anchor); French's exit (confirm before use). Alternate: Trogden's private stock. Metaphor: the leak in the pipe — the water leaving the reservoir and the water reaching the field are different volumes. Scripture: Deuteronomy 26:1–11; 2 Corinthians 8:12. Objection: This is tax planning with a Bible verse on it. Answer directly: the tax outcome is real and secondary. The point is the size of the yes it makes possible. Exercise: The asset inventory. List the illiquid. Circle the appreciated. Ends on: What do you own that you have never once considered giving?

14. Speed: A Yes That Takes Days, Not Years — 3,000 words

Opens with: a timeline. Conviction in March. Advisors in May. Board in August. Documents in November. The moment passed in June.

Beats 1. Most prompts die in the machinery. Why: locates the failure in structure rather than in the heart, which is both true and merciful. 2. Friction is not neutral — it silently converts generosity into accumulation. Why: the strongest argument in the book that design is a spiritual matter. 3. Build the infrastructure before you need it. Why: actionable this quarter. 4. Pre-agreed authority, so one spouse or one signature can move.

The turn: You did not decline. You were simply too slow to accept. The line: A family that needs eighteen months to say yes will miss most of what it is asked to do.

Story: a same-week gift made possible by structure built years earlier. Alternate: the gift that died in committee. Metaphor: settlement time — having capital and having it available are different things. Scripture: Proverbs 3:27–28; 2 Corinthians 9:5. Objection: Speed sounds like a lack of discernment. Distinguish preparation from impulse — the discernment happened in chapter 3. Exercise: Time your last significant gift, conviction to transfer. Ends on: How fast can your household say yes?

15. The Family That Sees Together — 3,800 words

Opens with: a nineteen-year-old defending a grant recommendation to her father and losing the vote.

Beats 1. Children inherit what they helped decide. Why: the sentence that makes the chapter, and the reason instruction fails where participation works. 2. The mechanisms: the annual meeting, the shared fund, real authority over real dollars. Why: specific enough to copy without a consultant. 3. The child who isn't interested — usually a child who has never been given a decision. Why: every family has one, and its absence from other books is why they feel unreal. 4. The diagnostic, final pass — taken as a family this time.

The turn: An estate plan transmits assets. It has never once transmitted a vision. The line: A family with one generous parent and four silent heirs does not produce a legacy. It produces litigation.

Story: a family practicing generosity across four generations since 1965. Alternate: Green's trust, and the Green who applies for a job. Metaphor: succession planning — the thing every owner knows he has underinvested in. Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4–9; Psalm 78:4–7; Joshua 4:20–24. Objection: This will create conflict. It will surface conflict that already exists. Better now than at the reading of the will. Exercise: Put the first family meeting on the calendar and give it one real decision. Ends on: Could your children state what your family is for?

16. The Last Check — 2,400 words

Opens with: a vision statement read aloud, and the silence after it.

Beats 1. Perpetual need versus a finishable assignment. Why: names the scarcity that is the book's entire opportunity. 2. What an ending does to a balance sheet. Why: pays off chapter 6 — the size of the assignment, now at maximum. 3. Working backward from the end to this year's number. Why: the book must end in arithmetic or the reader closes it moved and unchanged. 4. Why French took the job. Why: the last piece of the authors' story, held until now.

The turn: A family that adopts an ending stops asking how much to give this year and starts asking when the work could be finished. The line: Almost everyone will offer you a need. Almost no one will offer you a finish.

Story: the vision itself — the last check for the last missionary to the last unreached people group. Alternate: none. Do not dilute this chapter. Metaphor: none, by design. The book stops borrowing from finance in its final pages. Scripture: Matthew 24:14; Revelation 7:9; 2 Timothy 4:7. Objection: Isn't a finish line naive? Engage it with theological humility about timing — mature Christians disagree here, and the chapter should say so rather than pretend otherwise. Exercise: Write the family's end-state sentence. One sentence, no qualifiers. Ends on: the closing prayer, and one question: what will be true when your family's work is done?

PRODUCTION NOTES

Draft order: 1, 3, 7, 13, 16 — conviction, practice, argument, mechanism, ending. If those five hold, the rest is execution. Chapter 7 is drafted.

Word budget: 57,000 across the chapters, plus roughly 7,000 in front matter, appendices, and the study guide. Chapters 4, 12, and 16 are deliberately short; a chapter that indicts, a chapter about hiddenness, and a chapter about finishing should all feel restrained.

The transitions matter more than usual because the argument moves between conviction and mechanics repeatedly. Each part should open with a one-page bridge — 300 words, unnumbered — that names what just happened and what is about to.

Still to close. Thirteen of the sixteen anchors are American, and eleven are business owners. Source three stories at ordinary income levels and two from outside the United States, and place the first ordinary-income story in chapter 2 — otherwise the book proves a narrower thesis than the cover claims.
