Building the 100-question discovery bank for legacy families

ONE HUNDRED QUESTIONS

A discovery bank for legacy families

Companion to the Legacy Family Edition · LifeTogether Status: developing, August 2026

How to use this

Do not ask all one hundred. This is a bank, not a script. A good session uses eight to twelve questions and leaves most of the room's talking to the family.

The order of the categories is the order of a relationship. Story before ownership. Ownership before enough. Enough before fear. Nobody answers question 31 honestly for someone who hasn't earned it with questions 1 through 10.

Some of these are private. Marked ⚑ below — for a couple alone, or for a facilitator one-on-one, never in a room with children, in-laws, or advisors present. Asking one of those in a group does real damage and cannot be walked back.

The discipline that makes this work: never ask a question you are not prepared to sit in silence after, and never follow a hard answer with a solution. The value is in what surfaces, not in what you do about it that afternoon.

ONE · ORIGIN AND STORY

Start here, always. People will tell you about money only after they have told you about themselves.

  1. Where did the first dollar of this actually come from?
  2. Who worked hardest for what you now hold — and is that person still alive?
  3. What did your parents believe about money, and how did you learn it? Was it ever said out loud?
  4. What was the leanest season, and what did you do then that your children have never heard?
  5. What did you give up to build this?
  6. Was there a moment you knew it had worked? What did you feel?
  7. Who helped you that you have never adequately thanked?
  8. What part of the story do you sanitize when you tell it?
  9. If your grandchildren could know only one chapter of this story, which one?
  10. What would the person you were at twenty-five think of the person you are now?

TWO · OWNERSHIP AND IDENTITY

  1. If God owns it, what document says so?
  2. Where does your behavior contradict what you say you believe about ownership?
  3. Write your job description as a steward, the way you would write a role you were hiring for. What does it say?
  4. When did you stop thinking of yourself as someone building and start thinking of yourself as someone holding?
  5. What would you still be if the money were gone tomorrow?
  6. How much of your identity is load-bearing on the business or the balance sheet?
  7. What do people assume about you because of what you have, and which assumption bothers you most?
  8. Do you introduce yourself by what you do or by what you own? Which would you rather?
  9. ⚑ What would your closest friend say you actually trust?
  10. If your net worth were public tomorrow, what would change about your life?

THREE · ENOUGH

  1. What is the annual number at which your household has enough?
  2. ⚑ If you cannot write it, what specifically makes it hard?
  3. Who decided what enough looks like for you?
  4. What have you bought in the last five years that you were certain would satisfy you?
  5. What is the next number you are aiming at — and what happens when you reach it?
  6. What has increased in the last decade, and did your giving increase proportionally?
  7. Where is the line, for you specifically, between wise provision and stockpiling?
  8. If you drew a line, what would you do with everything above it?
  9. Who in your life is permitted to tell you that you have enough?
  10. If your income doubled, what would change and what would not?

FOUR · FEAR

The category that breaks most rooms open, and the one that requires the most care.

  1. ⚑ What is the specific scenario you rehearse at three in the morning?
  2. What are you protecting, and from what?
  3. When has God provided in a way you did not expect and have half forgotten?
  4. What would you do differently if you were certain you would be cared for?
  5. What is the largest gift you have seriously considered and not made? What stopped it?
  6. Where is your grip tightest?
  7. ⚑ Do you fear losing the money more, or losing what it says about you?
  8. ⚑ What are you afraid would happen to your children if they knew the full number?
  9. Whose financial collapse have you watched, and what did you conclude from it?
  10. What would it take for you to feel safe — and is that number reachable?

FIVE · VISION AND SIGHT

  1. What have you seen in the last five years that you could not stop thinking about?
  2. What has God already given you access to, expertise in, or grief over?
  3. Where does your giving currently go that you have never actually visited?
  4. What problem would you want to see finished in your lifetime?
  5. If money were not the constraint, what would you attempt?
  6. Who is doing work you believe in, and when did you last ask them what they need?
  7. What breaks your heart that does not break most people's?
  8. What is the largest thing anyone has ever invited you to be part of? Was it big enough?
  9. What would you fund if you did not need to be proven right?
  10. What would be true in the world if your family's work were finished?

SIX · CAPACITY

  1. What do you own that you have never once considered giving?
  2. What percentage of what you hold is liquid?
  3. What has appreciated most, and what is your basis in it?
  4. What could be given rather than sold?
  5. What do you have that is not money — access, reputation, network, expertise, a building, a fleet?
  6. Who on your advisory team has actually completed a non-cash gift before, rather than read about one?
  7. Does the person managing your money know what you believe about it?
  8. What is sitting idle that someone else could use tomorrow?
  9. What would you have to sell to make the gift you have been contemplating — and is selling necessary?
  10. If you gave away a quarter of what you hold, what would actually change about your life?

SEVEN · TIMING AND THE EXIT

  1. How many years are you from a possible liquidity event?
  2. Is anything currently under discussion, letter of intent, or contract?
  3. What do you want the first eighteen months after a sale to look like?
  4. What will you do with your time?
  5. Who will tell you no during that period, and have you asked them to?
  6. ⚑ What portion of the business do you intend to give — and have the two of you said the number out loud to each other?
  7. If the sale does not happen, what of this plan should you do anyway?
  8. How long did your last significant gift take, from conviction to settlement?
  9. What slowed it down?
  10. What has to exist so that a yes takes days rather than months?

EIGHT · CHILDREN AND INHERITANCE

Nearly every question here is private first. Bring them into the family room only after the couple has answered them alone.

  1. ⚑ What amount is helpful for each child, rather than what amount is possible?
  2. ⚑ Have you said those numbers out loud — to them?
  3. ⚑ Which child do you treat differently about money, and why?
  4. What did your children learn about money in this house that you never said?
  5. What do you want your money to make possible for them, and what do you want it to prevent?
  6. What work would you want each of them doing if the money did not exist?
  7. Who in the family has never been given a real financial decision?
  8. ⚑ What are you afraid an inheritance will do to them?
  9. What would you want them to be able to say about how you handled this?
  10. If you gave more away, what would they lose — and would they mind?

NINE · GOVERNANCE AND DECISIONS

  1. What is your family actually for? Could each person state it in a sentence?
  2. Who decides, and who merely gets consulted?
  3. When did the family last argue about money, and how did it end?
  4. What conversation has this family been avoiding for more than three years?
  5. Who is missing from these decisions who should be in them?
  6. What is your policy on in-laws — and is it stated, or inferred?
  7. ⚑ What would happen to family unity if one of you died this year?
  8. What objects, dates, or practices make your grandchildren ask questions?
  9. How does the next generation learn what you decided and why?
  10. Who is the most generous member of this family, and has anyone ever said so out loud?

TEN · LEGACY, JOY AND THE FINISH

  1. What has generosity actually done in you?
  2. When have you been most joyful in giving, and what made that time different?
  3. What gift do you regret — and what did it teach you?
  4. What are you funding that nobody photographs?
  5. Whose best work is still ahead of them, and are you close enough to know?
  6. What would you want read at your funeral about what you did with what you were given?
  7. What will be true when your family's work is done?
  8. What do you want your grandchildren to inherit that is not money?
  9. What are you going to do differently, and by when?
  10. If this were the last year you had capacity, what would you do first?

FACILITATOR NOTES

The five that most often open a room. Numbers 4, 21, 31, 41, and 84. If you only have an hour, build it out of these and let the family take it where it goes.

The three that most often close one. Numbers 52, 53, and 62 — the balance-sheet questions. Ask them too early and the family concludes you are working an angle, and everything after that is polite. These belong in a private advisory conversation, not in a formation session.

Never ask in a mixed room. Anything marked ⚑, and especially 71 through 73. A parent naming different numbers for different children, in front of those children, without preparation, will do damage that outlives the meeting.

Sequence within a session. One story question to open. Two or three from the middle categories. One from legacy to close. Resist the urge to cover ground — a family that answered three questions honestly has done better than one that got through twelve.

When something heavy surfaces. It will, and it usually will not be about money. Receive it, thank them, do not solve it, and follow up privately in the week. Money conversations reach grief, estrangement, and shame faster than facilitators expect.

Watch the spouse who is not talking. In most rooms one spouse carries the financial narrative and the other has a different one. Ask them directly, gently, and give them room. Some of the most important answers in this bank come from the quieter person.

Never ask a question you would not answer yourself. Families can tell instantly whether you have done this work. If you have not set your own finish line, do not ask about theirs.
