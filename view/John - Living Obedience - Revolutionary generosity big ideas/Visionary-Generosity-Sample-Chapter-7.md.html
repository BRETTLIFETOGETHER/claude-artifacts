VISIONARY GENEROSITY

Sample Chapter — Chapter Seven

God's Rate of Return

Draft for review. Written in Steve French's first person per the single-narrator structure. All convictions and framing here are drawn from his documented public teaching. Three passages marked [SF — confirm or replace] need his own account before this goes anywhere; I've written them as placeholders in his documented voice rather than leaving them blank, so the chapter reads whole. Scripture is NIV.

A man sat across from me a few years ago who was eleven months out from selling a company he had started in a spare bedroom. He was sixty-three. He had done the thing almost nobody does — built something real, kept it, and lived to sell it on his own terms. His attorney was excellent. His investment banker was excellent. He had a folder in front of him with more analysis in it than most people see in a lifetime.

And he asked me the wrong question.

He asked how much he should give.

I want to be careful here, because it is a sincere question and it comes from a good place. He was not being stingy. He was, in his own way, trying to be faithful. He had been in church his whole life. He tithed. He had been told since he was a boy that ten percent belonged to God, and he had done that faithfully through lean years when it cost him something, and he was now trying to figure out what the honest number was on the largest transaction of his life.

But "how much should I give" is a subtraction question. It assumes the pile is his, and that generosity is the act of removing something from it. Every answer to that question is a smaller number than the one before it, because subtraction always hurts.

So I asked him a different one. I asked what he thought the money was going to do.

He looked at me like I had changed the subject.

The word we use wrong

I spent a long career buying and selling companies. I have been in rooms where a two-times return was considered a good outcome and rooms where three times was cause for celebration. I know what it feels like to model a deal, to argue over a point of margin, to lie awake over an assumption in a spreadsheet. That is the language I think in, and I suspect it is the language many of you think in too.

Which is why I have come to believe that the church has been using the wrong word with people like us for about a hundred years.

The word is sacrifice. It is a true word and a biblical one, and I am not going to argue it away. But when you say "sacrifice" to a man who has spent forty years allocating capital, he hears loss. He hears that you are asking him to accept a negative return in exchange for feeling better about himself. And because he is a decent person who loves God, he will do it — a little. He will accept a small loss annually and call it obedience.

Here is what nobody told him. Jesus did not primarily talk about giving in the language of loss. He talked about it in the language of return.

Look at where he actually goes when the subject is money. Treasure that doesn't corrode. Seed that produces at thirty, sixty, and a hundred times what was sown. A manager commended, of all things, for shrewdness. A field worth selling everything to buy. Over and over, the frame is not what you will lose but what you will get, and where, and for how long.

This is not a marketing softening of the gospel. It is the gospel's own vocabulary. And it means that the man across the table from me was not being asked to take a loss. He was being asked to reallocate — out of an instrument with a mediocre long-term return and into one with a return no market on earth can approach.

I have said in public before that God is the best investor there has ever been, and I mean it plainly. Where a strong human return is two or three times, his is a hundred, a thousand. That is not my number. It is the number in the parable.

Read the parable again, slowly

The parable of the sower is usually preached as an evangelism text, and it is one. But sit with the return schedule for a minute, because it is doing something specific.

The seed is identical in all four cases. What varies is the soil. Path, rocks, thorns, good ground — and only the last one produces, at a hundred, sixty, or thirty times what was sown.

Notice what that means for a giver. Jesus is not saying that generosity produces a hundredfold return in the abstract. He is saying the multiple depends entirely on where you sow. The same seed, the same amount, the same sincerity — and the return runs from zero to a hundred based on the ground it lands in.

I have watched this play out for twenty-five years and I can tell you it is not a metaphor. I have seen a hundred thousand dollars go into a project that produced nothing anyone can find today, and I have seen a hundred thousand dollars go into one man in a hard place and watched it become a church, then eleven churches, then a training center that has now sent people into three countries. Same money. Same year. Same donor.

The difference was the soil.

So the question is not how much. The question is where, and into what, and does it reproduce.

What I am not saying

I need to stop and put a fence here, because this argument goes bad quickly and I have watched it go bad in people I love.

I am not telling you that if you give, God will make you wealthy. That teaching has done real damage, it has hurt poor people worst of all, and it is not in the text.

Read the Macedonian churches in 2 Corinthians 8. Paul describes a group in severe trial and extreme poverty who gave beyond their ability, and who begged for the privilege of doing it. That is the highest commendation of generosity anywhere in the New Testament. And Paul never reports that they got rich. He reports that they gave themselves first to the Lord, and the money followed the surrender.

R.G. LeTourneau, who built earthmoving machines and eventually reversed his tithe — giving ninety percent and living on ten — did not get there on a rising curve. He got there through a period in which he skipped what he had pledged, watched an anticipated hundred-thousand-dollar profit turn into a hundred-thousand-dollar loss, and then pledged again from underneath the debt. He gave in the direction of a return he could not see, while his balance sheet was arguing the other way.

And Bob Buford, who organized the last half of his life around the hundredfold and spent it funding leaders rather than programs, lost his only son. Whatever the hundredfold means, it did not mean insulation.

So hold the return and the loss together, or you will preach something cruel. The return is real. It is not denominated in dollars into your account, it is not on your timetable, and a great deal of it is not visible from here. Anyone who promises you otherwise is selling something.

What I am saying is narrower and, I think, more useful: within the part of this you actually control, most Christians are leaving enormous multiples on the table for reasons that have nothing to do with their hearts.

The arithmetic that is ours

Here is where exponential stops being a sermon and becomes a spreadsheet.

Three multipliers are entirely within your control. None of them requires you to love God more than you already do. All three are things almost nobody has explained to you.

The first is what you give. Roughly nine-tenths of the wealth in this country sits outside of cash — in businesses, land, appreciated stock, equipment, crops, intellectual property. And roughly all of the giving comes out of the checking account. That mismatch is the single largest unclaimed opportunity in the American church, and it is a mechanical problem, not a spiritual one.

I sat with a family who wanted to fund a project for a college ministry. They owned rental property. The husband did the math the way any careful person would: to net the gift amount after selling, plus cover the tax on the sale, he would need to sell two properties. Then he learned he could give one property to a charitable fund before it sold, and the ministry received more than the two-property plan would have produced. He did not become more generous that afternoon. He became better informed.

By unlocking assets rather than writing checks, families can frequently give something on the order of ten times what they otherwise would have. Ten times. Same conviction, same household, same year.

The second is when you give. Capital deployed at fifty-five works for thirty more years than capital deployed at eighty-five. A gift at death is a transaction. A gift during life is a partnership — you get to see the outcome, correct your mistakes, learn what you are actually good at funding, and let your children watch you do it. An estate plan teaches your family nothing. A father who gave away the building and explained why teaches them everything.

The third is what you fund. This is the soil. Some good things are consumed and some good things reproduce, and both are worth funding, but only one of them multiplies. A meal is eaten. A well is drilled and serves for years. A translator trained produces a Bible that produces churches that produce translators. When the Barnhart family began giving away half their company's profits every year, they did not hand the decision to a committee of the wealthy. They gave it to a group of employees and spouses who research recipients, build real relationships with them, and go looking for the best people working on a problem rather than the organizations with the strongest fundraising departments.

That is a soil decision, made by people close enough to the ground to know the difference.

The engine

I want you to see what happens when all three run at once, because the Barnharts are the clearest case in America and the numbers are public.

Two brothers took over a family crane business operating out of two bedrooms of the house they grew up in. Before it grew, they settled two things. The first was ownership — Alan has said it as directly as it can be said, that "God owns this business. My job is to be a steward." The second was a cap on their own lifestyle, set at roughly the level of a ministry worker, decided when there was almost nothing to cap.

Then they built an engine. Roughly half of the company's annual earnings go out to the Kingdom every year. The other half goes back into the business. Give half, reinvest half — which means the giving grows the company that grows the giving. In their first year they gave away fifty thousand dollars, which was more than Alan's salary. Decades later that number had become a million dollars a month, and the company had grown from ten employees to over a thousand.

And in 2007 they gave the company itself away — ninety-nine percent into a charitable trust, one percent retained for voting. They still run it. They will never receive its accrued value. They kept none of it.

Now look at what actually compounded there. Not their net worth. Their capacity. Every year the engine could do more than the year before, because they had structured their lives so that growth had nowhere to go except out.

That is exponential giving. It is not a bigger heart than yours. It is a decision about ownership, a cap on consumption, a mechanism for converting assets, and a discipline about soil — four things, all of them available to you, most of them available this quarter.

Back to the table

I did not answer the man's question that day. I want to be honest about that, because I think it is the most useful thing I did for him.

Instead I asked him what he had seen in his life that he could not stop thinking about. It took him a while. He talked around it, the way people do. And then he told me about a place he had visited on a trip his wife had made him take, and about a man he had met there, and he got about four sentences into it before his voice went.

We talked for two more hours. By the end of it he was not asking what percentage was appropriate. He was asking whether it was possible to give a portion of the company before the letter of intent was signed, and how fast it could be done, and whether his kids could be part of deciding where it went.

He had stopped subtracting. He had started allocating.

That is the whole shift, and it does not happen because someone shames a person into it. It happens when somebody enlarges what a person can see, and then shows them the mechanism to act on it while the window is still open.

The work

Before you go to the next chapter, do three things. Not later. This week.

Inventory what you own. Not your net worth — the actual assets. List every illiquid thing: business interests, real estate, appreciated securities, equipment, land, royalties. Circle the ones that have grown in value. That circled list is your real giving capacity, and until today it has probably never been part of the conversation.

Name one soil. One problem, one place, one person whose work you have seen with your own eyes. Not a portfolio. One. Write down what finished would look like there, and what it would cost.

Ask the timing question. If you are within five years of any liquidity event — a sale, an inheritance, a windfall, a maturing position — call your advisor this week and ask what could be given before rather than after. If your advisor cannot answer that question, that is information too.

For reflection

  1. When you think about giving, is your instinct subtraction or allocation? Be honest — the tell is whether the number feels like a cost or an assignment.

  2. Where has your giving been sown into soil you have never actually seen? What would it take to go and look?

  3. What is the largest thing you own that you have never once considered giving — and what is the real reason it has never crossed your mind?

A prayer

Father, you own it all — not just what is in the account, but the business, the ground, the years, the health, the mind that built any of it. Forgive me for calculating what I would part with as though the pile were mine to divide. Show me the soil. Give me the nerve to plant where you point instead of where it is safe, and the patience to wait for a harvest I may not live to count. Make my hands quick and my grip loose. Amen.
