40 DAYS OF GENEROSITY

The Daily Reader

Days 1–14 · Weeks One and Two LifeTogether

Format. Each day: a story, the biblical foundation, the transferable principle, an exercise you can do today, a next-step challenge, and three questions for reflection. Five to seven minutes.

A note on the stories. The daily readings run on Scripture and documented history — material that is public, verifiable, and needs no permission. Contemporary testimony belongs in your group and on your weekend, where your own church supplies it. That is deliberate: the people in the pew should hear from the ancients and from each other, not from strangers in a book.

WEEK ONE — WHO OWNS IT?

DAY 1 · Everything You Gave Us Was Already Yours

He had just raised the largest offering in the nation's history, and the first thing he did was give the credit away.

The story. David had spent his life wanting to build a temple and had been told he would not be the one to do it. So he did the next thing — he gathered the materials for his son to use. Gold, silver, bronze, iron, timber, precious stones, and then his own personal treasure on top of it. The leaders followed, and the people gave willingly, and the whole nation rejoiced at what had been assembled.

Then David prayed. And in front of the entire assembly, at the peak of the most generous moment of his life, he said something that took the ground out from under everyone standing there: who am I, and who are my people, that we should be able to give as generously as this? Everything comes from you, and we have given you only what comes from your hand.

He did not thank the givers. He told them they had never owned it.

The foundation. 1 Chronicles 29:11–14. Read it slowly, out loud if you can.

The principle. Generosity does not begin when you decide to release something. It begins when you realize it was never yours to hold.

Today's exercise. Write one sentence and sign it: God owns __, and my job is ____. Fill in the first blank with something specific — an account, a house, a business, a paycheck.

The next step. Put that sentence where you will see it every day for the next thirty-nine.

Three questions 1. If you had to describe who owns what is in your accounts, what would you honestly say? 2. Where does your behavior contradict what you just wrote? 3. What would change tomorrow if you managed one thing as a steward instead of an owner?

DAY 2 · The Man Who Kept the Receipt

He owned everything and had earned none of it, and it took a disaster to show him.

The story. Job lost his livestock, his servants, his houses, and his children inside a single day. What he said next has been quoted for three thousand years, and it is easy to hear it as resignation when it is actually a statement about ownership: naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked I will depart.

Much later, when God finally answers Job out of the storm, he asks a question that settles the whole argument: who has a claim against me that I must pay? Everything under heaven belongs to me.

Job never got an explanation. He got an owner.

The foundation. Job 1:21 and Job 41:11.

The principle. You arrived with nothing and you will leave with nothing. Everything in between is on loan, and the loan has a purpose.

Today's exercise. List three things you own that you did not create — a talent, an opportunity, a relationship, a body, a mind. Beside each one, write the name of the person who gave it to you.

The next step. Thank one of those people this week, out loud.

Three questions 1. What have you worked hardest for, and how much of it was actually in your control? 2. What would you have the most difficulty releasing? 3. What does it change to think of that thing as entrusted rather than earned?

DAY 3 · The Cloth Merchant

History remembers the translator. It has almost entirely forgotten the man who paid for the paper.

The story. In the 1520s a London cloth merchant named Humphrey Monmouth met a young scholar with an illegal ambition — to put the Bible into ordinary English so that a plowboy could read it. Translation without the Church's approval was a crime. Monmouth gave William Tyndale money. He gave him a room in his own house to work in. And when the New Testaments were printed in Germany, Monmouth used his merchant ships to smuggle them back into England.

He was arrested and spent a year in the Tower of London on more than twenty charges.

Every English Bible in print traces back to Tyndale. Tyndale traces back to a businessman with a warehouse and a fleet.

The foundation. 1 Corinthians 4:7 — what do you have that you did not receive?

The principle. The thing you already own — a room, a network, a skill, a fleet — may be exactly the instrument God intends to use. You are not required to become someone else first.

Today's exercise. Write down three things you have that are not money. A room. A vehicle. A contact. An afternoon. A professional skill.

The next step. Offer one of them to someone this week without being asked.

Three questions 1. What do you have access to that most people around you do not? 2. Whose work would move forward if you opened one door? 3. What have you been assuming is irrelevant to God's purposes?

DAY 4 · The Servant Who Buried It

Two servants were praised for taking a risk. One was condemned for playing it safe.

The story. A man leaving on a journey handed his property to three servants — five bags of gold, two, and one. The first two put the money to work and doubled it. The third dug a hole and buried his, and when the master returned he explained himself carefully: I was afraid, so I hid it.

Notice that he did not lose anything. He returned exactly what he had been given. And he is the one who is called wicked and lazy.

The parable is not about investment returns. It is about a servant who mistook preservation for faithfulness.

The foundation. Matthew 25:14–30.

The principle. You will not be asked what you received. You will be asked what it did.

Today's exercise. Name one thing you are currently preserving rather than deploying — money, a skill, a house, a season of life.

The next step. Take one action this week that puts it to work.

Three questions 1. Where has caution quietly become the thing you call wisdom? 2. What are you afraid would happen if you deployed it? 3. What did the third servant believe about his master, and do you believe it too?

DAY 5 · Two Small Coins

The largest gift in the temple that day came from the person with the least.

The story. Jesus sat down opposite the place where the offerings were put and watched the crowd. Rich people threw in large amounts. Then a poor widow came and put in two very small copper coins, worth almost nothing.

He called his disciples over specifically to point her out. The others gave out of their wealth, he said. She gave out of her poverty — everything she had to live on.

He did not tell her to be careful. He did not send someone after her with her coins. He held her up as the standard and moved on.

The foundation. Mark 12:41–44.

The principle. God's accounting measures what remains, not what left. Which means this whole subject was never reserved for people with margin.

Today's exercise. Give something today that you will actually notice. Not comfortable. Noticeable.

The next step. Do not tell anyone you did it.

Three questions 1. Have you ever assumed this conversation was for wealthier people than you? 2. What do you have that feels too small to matter? 3. What did the widow believe about tomorrow?

DAY 6 · The Rich Fool

He was not a criminal. He was a successful farmer with a storage problem.

The story. A man's land produced a bumper crop and he had nowhere to put it. So he made a plan any advisor would approve: tear down the barns, build bigger ones, store the surplus, and then relax — you have plenty of grain laid up for many years. Take life easy; eat, drink and be merry.

That night God called him a fool. Not for the harvest. Not for the barns. For a plan in which no one else appeared, and God did not appear, and death did not appear.

Jesus tells the story in response to a man asking him to settle an inheritance dispute.

The foundation. Luke 12:13–21.

The principle. The danger of a good year is not the money. It is that a plan built entirely around yourself will look completely reasonable right up until the moment it doesn't.

Today's exercise. Look at your own plan for the next ten years. Write down who else appears in it.

The next step. Add one name.

Three questions 1. What are you storing, and what is it for? 2. Whose life would be different if your surplus had a destination? 3. What would you do differently if you knew this year were the last one?

DAY 7 · Rest — What You Wrote

No new story today. Go back and look at what you have written this week.

The foundation. Psalm 24:1.

The principle. Six days of reading changes very little. One sentence you actually signed changes a great deal.

Today's exercise. Read your ownership sentence out loud. Change it if it needs changing. Then say it to one other person.

The next step. Ask that person to check in with you about it in a month.

Three questions 1. What surprised you this week? 2. What did you resist? 3. What is the one thing from this week you do not want to forget?

WEEK TWO — ENOUGH

DAY 8 · The Prayer Nobody Prays

A man in Proverbs asks God for two things before he dies, and the second one is stranger than the first.

The story. Give me neither poverty nor riches, Agur prays, but give me only my daily bread. Then he explains himself, and the explanation is the remarkable part. If I have too much, I may disown you and say who is the Lord? If I have too little, I may steal and dishonor your name.

He is not being humble. He is being careful about his own soul. He has looked honestly at what abundance does to a person and asked God to protect him from it.

Almost nobody prays the second half of that prayer sincerely.

The foundation. Proverbs 30:7–9.

The principle. Wealth is not neutral. It has an effect on the person holding it, and a wise person plans for that effect the way he plans for a downturn.

Today's exercise. Pray Agur's prayer out loud, both halves, and notice which half is harder to mean.

The next step. Write down what "enough" would look like for your household — in one sentence, not a number yet.

Three questions 1. Which half of that prayer do you actually want answered? 2. What has more money done to people you have watched up close? 3. What are you assuming money will protect you from?

DAY 9 · The Bread That Wouldn't Keep

God built a lesson about enough into the food itself.

The story. In the desert, bread appeared on the ground every morning. Moses told them to gather what they needed for the day — no more. Some ignored him and kept extra overnight. By morning it was full of maggots and smelled.

Every single day for forty years, they had to decide again whether God would show up tomorrow. And every day he did.

The only exception was the sixth day, when they gathered double and it kept perfectly, because the seventh day was rest.

The foundation. Exodus 16:16–20.

The principle. Storing was not a sin of greed. It was a failure of trust, and God made it physically impossible to succeed at.

Today's exercise. Identify one thing you are hoarding out of anxiety rather than out of stewardship. There is a difference and you know which is which.

The next step. Release a portion of it this week.

Three questions 1. What does your saving pattern say about what you believe regarding tomorrow? 2. Where is the line for you between wise provision and stockpiling? 3. When has God provided in a way you did not expect and have half forgotten?

DAY 10 · The Man Who Reversed It

He started at thirty percent, in debt, and ended at ninety.

The story. R.G. LeTourneau had a seventh-grade education and eventually held nearly three hundred patents for earth-moving machinery. But the decision that shaped his life came at thirty, when he was deep in debt and had skipped a pledge he had made to his church. That year his anticipated hundred-thousand-dollar profit turned into a hundred-thousand-dollar loss.

Chastened, and with no money for payroll, he pledged again — the amount he had skipped, plus the same amount for the coming year.

Over the following decades his giving climbed until he was living on ten percent and giving away ninety. He liked to say that he shoveled money out and God shoveled it back, and God had the bigger shovel.

He did not begin at ninety percent. Nobody does.

The foundation. 2 Corinthians 9:6–8.

The principle. The extreme cases arrived by increments. What looks like a leap was almost always a staircase.

Today's exercise. Write down your current giving percentage. Then write next year's, one step higher.

The next step. Set a date to raise it again, and put it on the calendar.

Three questions 1. What has your giving percentage done over the last ten years? 2. What has your income done over the same period? 3. What would it take to make the first number move with the second?

DAY 11 · The Finish Line

Two brothers set a limit on themselves in the first year, when there was almost nothing to limit.

The story. Alan Barnhart was twenty-four and about to take over a small family crane business with his brother. He had been reading through the Bible's warnings about wealth — hundreds of passages over roughly two years — and had come to a conclusion that frightened him: he did not want business success to equal spiritual failure.

So before the company grew, he and his brother capped their own incomes at roughly the level of a ministry worker. They told new hires that the fruits of their labor would not increase the owners' lifestyle. Half of annual profits would go out; half would go back into the business.

Their first year they gave away fifty thousand dollars — more than Alan's own salary. Decades later that figure had grown to about a million a month, and in 2007 they gave the company itself away.

The foundation. 1 Timothy 6:6–10.

The principle. A ceiling is what sets you free. A floor only tells you the minimum.

Today's exercise. Write the annual figure at which your household has enough. If you cannot write it, write what makes it hard — that answer is worth more than a number you don't believe.

The next step. Tell one person the number, and name the date you will revisit it.

Three questions 1. What is the honest reason a number is difficult to write? 2. Who decided what "enough" looks like for you? 3. What would you do with everything above the line?

DAY 12 · The Question Reversed

A woman who gave away half of what she earned asks a better question than the one we usually ask.

The story. April and Craig Chapman began their careers as software engineers. Two early windfalls launched their giving, and over the years they gave away half of what they earned.

Somewhere in that process the question changed for her. Most Christians ask how much should I give? — a question that starts from ownership and works down. She began asking a different one: how much do we keep?

It is the same math and a completely different conversation. One asks what to subtract. The other asks what to retain, and everything else has a destination.

The foundation. Luke 3:10–11 — the man with two shirts.

The principle. Change the question and the whole calculation reverses. Giving stops being what leaves and becomes what remains.

Today's exercise. Do next month's budget backward. Start with what you will give, then what you will save, then what is left to live on.

The next step. Run one full month that way.

Three questions 1. Which question have you actually been asking? 2. What does your budget's order of operations reveal? 3. What would have to be true for you to start with giving?

DAY 13 · The Prophet at the Door

He asked a starving woman for her last meal, and it was not cruelty.

The story. There was a famine, and a widow in Zarephath was gathering sticks to cook a final meal for herself and her son before they died. A stranger arrived and asked her for water, and then for a piece of bread.

She told him the truth: she had a handful of flour and a little oil, and this was the end of it.

He asked her to make him something first.

She did. And the flour did not run out and the oil did not run dry, through the whole famine, for her household and for him.

It is the most unreasonable request in Scripture, made to the least likely person, at the worst possible moment.

The foundation. 1 Kings 17:7–16.

The principle. What looks like recklessness from outside is usually trust from inside. The widow was not being careless with her son's life. She was betting on the character of the God who sent the man.

Today's exercise. Name your fear specifically. Not "the future" — the exact scenario you rehearse.

The next step. Tell that fear to one trustworthy person out loud.

Three questions 1. What are you actually afraid of? 2. What would you do differently if you were certain you would be cared for? 3. Where is your grip tightest, and what is it protecting?

DAY 14 · Rest — The Number

No new story. Go back to the number.

The foundation. Proverbs 30:8–9.

The principle. Almost nobody writes it the first time. The second week is when it usually happens.

Today's exercise. Write the number, or write the obstacle. Then look at what you wrote in week one about ownership and see whether the two agree.

The next step. If you have a spouse, have this conversation with them before week three begins.

Three questions 1. What has been hardest about these two weeks? 2. What have you avoided? 3. What are you hoping the next four weeks do in you?

PRODUCTION NOTES

Why the stories are ancient. Every story in days 1–14, and every one assigned for 15–40, is either Scripture or documented public history. No permissions, no story bank, no fabrication. That solves the largest open build item in one design decision.

Where contemporary testimony lives. In the group sessions and the weekend messages, supplied locally. A congregation hearing from its own people is worth more than a congregation reading about strangers, and it makes the campaign theirs.

Two stories in days 1–14 need clearance before print. Barnhart on day 11 and Chapman on day 12 are widely published but living — permission through NCF and Generous Giving respectively. Day 30, Stanley Tam, is historical and needs only attribution.

Length. Days run 300–400 words. Rest days run about 120. Total for the full forty: roughly 13,000 words.
