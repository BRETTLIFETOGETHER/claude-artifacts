TWO ENGINES — SESSION FIVE

The Cloud of Witnesses

An optional fifth session, or a standalone 90 minutes The Signatry with LifeTogether

Purpose. The first four sessions teach the argument. This one puts faces on it. Participants meet six people — three from history, three living — and work out which engine each one was running, which is the fastest way to internalize a framework without being lectured about it again.

Format. 90 minutes. Works as the closing session of the four-session workshop, as a standalone evening, or as a giving-circle discussion that recurs monthly with different witnesses.

The governing rule still applies. Nobody asks for anything.

Participant Guide

Opening question: Name one person whose generosity you have watched up close. What did it cost them?

The six witnesses

Humphrey Monmouth — a London cloth merchant who saw an English Bible before one existed. He gave William Tyndale money and a room in his own house, then used his merchant ships to move the printed New Testaments back into England. He spent a year in the Tower of London for it. We have the Bible in English. Almost nobody knows his name.

Selina, Countess of Huntingdon — she could not preach and had no theological contribution to make. What she had was a house and a guest list. The aristocracy would never have stood in a field to hear George Whitefield, but they would accept an invitation to her drawing room. So she filled it, again and again, and handed him the room.

John Thornton — backed John Newton when Newton was a customs official with a criminal past, no education anyone respected, and a drawer of unpublished verse. Thornton placed him in a pulpit, gave him counsel and rest, and pushed him to publish the hymns. One of them was "Amazing Grace."

R.G. LeTourneau — a seventh-grade education, nearly 300 inventions in earthmoving machinery, and a reversed tithe: ten percent kept, ninety percent given. He did not begin there. He began at thirty, deeply in debt, having skipped a pledge and watched an anticipated six-figure profit become a six-figure loss — and pledged again from underneath it.

Alan and Katherine Barnhart — Alan was twenty-four when he read Matthew 19 and spent two years working through hundreds of passages before changing anything. He and his brother capped their incomes in the first year of the business, when there was almost nothing to cap. Give half, reinvest half. Fifty thousand in year one, more than Alan's salary. Decades later, a million a month. In 2007 they gave the company away.

April and Craig Chapman — two software engineers, two early windfalls, and a decision to give away half of what they earned. Her framing inverts the usual question: not how much to give, but how much to keep.

The exercise — score the witnesses

For each one, mark 1 to 5 on both engines. Vision is what they could see and how big it was. Obedience is their willingness to act before the outcome was visible.

What the pattern shows

Almost none of them ran both engines evenly at the start. Most began strong on one and were carried into the other by circumstance, a person, or a decision that turned out to cost more than expected.

Notice also what each one gave that was not money. A room. A guest list. A shipping network. A pulpit. A recruitment conversation. Every witness in this session contributed something that would not appear on a giving statement.

Take home. Choose one witness whose situation is closest to yours and write one sentence about what you would have to change to be described that way.

Reflection 1. Which witness did you find yourself resisting, and what does that tell you? 2. What do you have that is not money — access, a room, a network, a reputation, an introduction? 3. Whose best work is still ahead of them, and are you close enough to know?

Session Five — Teaching Script

[0:00–0:10] Opening question, full circle. Someone whose generosity they watched up close, and what it cost. Do not editorialize. This session runs on other people's stories and it should start with one of theirs.

[0:10–0:40] The six witnesses. Roughly five minutes each. Tell them; do not explain them. The room will draw the lesson faster than you can deliver it, and the temptation to apply each one is what flattens this session.

Facilitator note: Order matters. Monmouth, Huntingdon, Thornton first — three people who gave something other than money, which quietly disarms the participant who is already calculating what he can afford. Then LeTourneau and Barnhart, who are about scale. Chapman last, because her question lands hardest after the room has been impressed by large numbers.

[0:40–0:55] Score them. Individually and silently. Then take one witness — Huntingdon usually generates the best argument — and ask the room to defend competing scores. Let them disagree. The disagreement is where the framework gets learned.

[0:55–1:05] Score yourself. Silent. No sharing. Ask one question aloud: did anything move since Session One?

[1:05–1:20] What they gave that wasn't money. Work the list from the room rather than presenting it. Room, guest list, ships, pulpit, recruitment pitch, relationship. Then: Which of those do you have?

Facilitator note: This is the section that makes the workshop usable for a giving circle or a church group where capacity varies widely. Do not skip it to save time.

[1:20–1:30] Close. Take-home, reflection questions read aloud, brief prayer. No summary, and no ask.

Adapting for three audiences

Advisors. Add a seventh witness — a professional who raised the generosity question with a client and changed a family's trajectory. Shift the closing question from what do you have to what could you ask. The advisor's contribution is a question, not a gift.

Pastors. Lead with Huntingdon and Thornton rather than Barnhart. A congregation's high-capacity families need to see patronage that is relational before they see patronage that is financial, or the session reads as a preamble to a campaign.

Giving circles. Run it as a recurring format — one witness per month, scored and discussed, with a rotating member responsible for bringing a seventh witness of their own. This is the version with the longest life.

CANVA BUILD SPEC

Since I can't generate Canva files or links directly, this is the paste-in spec. Every string below is final copy — a designer should not need to write a word.

System

Palette. Navy #12253F (dominant) · Cream #FAF7F0 (ground) · Gold #B08B3F (accent, sparing) · Charcoal #2B2B2B (body) · Rule #D8D0C2

Type. Headlines in a serif with authority — Playfair Display or Lora. Body in Inter or Source Sans. Eyebrow labels in all caps, tracked +3, gold, 10pt.

Motif. Two small dots, lower left, on every page — one filled gold, one outlined gold. That is the two-engine mark. Do not add a color bar or accent stripe.

Rules. Generous margins. One idea per surface. No stock photography of hands cupping seedlings, no globes, no sunrises. No emoji.
