Building the six-message weekend series outline for preachers

40 DAYS OF GENEROSITY

The Weekend Series — Six Messages

Teaching outlines for the preaching pastor · LifeTogether Status: developing, August 2026

Before you preach any of it

Say the covenant in week one and mean it. Somewhere in the first ten minutes: Over the next six weeks nobody is going to ask you for anything. There is no pledge card at the end of this. There is no capital campaign behind it. If that changes how you listen, good — that's the point.

If your church is running a budget appeal in the same season, say that too, and say where the line is. A congregation will forgive you for asking. It will not forgive you for asking while claiming you weren't.

Preach the arc, not the topic. These six move in a specific order — ownership, contentment, fear, sight, family, joy. Reorder them and the series stops working, because each week rests on the one before. Fear cannot be preached to a room that hasn't settled ownership.

Your own number. Before week two, write your finish line. A congregation can tell inside ninety seconds whether the person preaching has done this, and week two is the message where they'll be listening for it.

WEEK ONE · WHO OWNS IT?

Big idea. Generosity does not begin when you decide to release something. It begins when you realize it was never yours.

Text. 1 Chronicles 29:11–14 · Psalm 24:1 · Deuteronomy 8:17–18

The arc

Open on the gap. Almost every Christian in this room would say God owns everything. Almost every Christian in this room manages money like an owner. Name that without accusation — the gap is not hypocrisy, it's the absence of a decision anybody ever asked us to make.

The text. David has spent his life wanting to build the temple and has been told he won't be the one. So he gathers materials for his son instead — gold, silver, timber, and then his own personal treasure on top. The nation follows. It's the largest offering in Israel's history.

Then he prays, and in front of everyone he says the thing that takes the ground out: who am I, and who are my people, that we should be able to give as generously as this? Everything comes from you, and we have given you only what comes from your hand.

He doesn't thank the givers. He tells them they never owned it.

The turn. There are only two ways to think about a gift. Subtraction — this is mine and some is leaving. Or allocation — this belongs to someone else and I'm deciding where it goes. Every answer to a subtraction question is a smaller number than the last, because subtraction hurts. Nobody in history has given joyfully while doing subtraction.

Land it. The question isn't how much should I give. It's what does the owner want done with this?

Illustration options. David is the text and carries the message. Alternates: Job, who lost everything in a day and said he arrived with nothing and would leave the same way; a local person who can describe the moment ownership became real to them.

Close. Hand out the sentence card: God owns __, and my job is ____. Ask them to sign it and put it somewhere they'll see it for thirty-nine more days. Then state the covenant.

Next week. "If God owns it, the next question is obvious and almost nobody asks it — how much are we supposed to keep?"

WEEK TWO · ENOUGH

Big idea. The tithe is a floor. Nobody ever handed you a ceiling, and the ceiling is what sets you free.

Text. Proverbs 30:7–9 · 1 Timothy 6:6–10 · Exodus 16:16–20

The arc

Open on the number. Ask them what number they thought would be enough when they were twenty. Then ask whether they reached it, and what happened when they did. Expect laughter, then something quieter.

The text. Agur asks God for two things before he dies. Give me neither poverty nor riches. And then he explains himself — if I have too much I may disown you; if I have too little I may steal. He isn't being humble. He's looked honestly at what abundance does to a person and asked to be protected from it.

Then the manna. Gather what you need for today, no more. The ones who stored extra woke up to maggots. Forty years of deciding every single morning whether God would show up tomorrow — and every morning he did.

The turn. A floor tells you the minimum and leaves every other question open. A ceiling answers all of them at once. Once a household names the number at which it has enough, everything above the line has an obvious destination and the annual argument ends.

Land it. The reason nobody writes the number is not greed. It's that writing it means trusting something.

Illustration options. Alan Barnhart capping his income in the first year of the business, when there was almost nothing to cap — fifty thousand given away in year one, more than his own salary; a million a month decades later. Alternates: LeTourneau's climb by increments; a local family who set a limit.

The trap in this message. This is the sermon most likely to be heard as an appeal. Get ahead of it: I'm not asking you to give more. I'm asking you to decide what's enough, and I have no idea what you'll do with the answer.

Close. Write the number, or write the obstacle. Tell one person.

Next week. "Most of us can't write that number, and it isn't because we love money. Next week, what's actually underneath it."

WEEK THREE · OPEN HANDS

Big idea. The enemy is fear, not greed. Greed you repent of. Fear you get walked out of.

Text. 1 Kings 17:7–16 · Matthew 6:25–34 · Hebrews 13:5

The arc

Open by naming it. Almost nobody in this room withholds because they love money. They withhold because they're afraid — of running out, of a downturn, of a child's future, of needing help and having none. Say that plainly and watch the room exhale.

The text. There's a famine. A widow in Zarephath is gathering sticks to cook a last meal for herself and her son. A stranger asks her for water, and then for bread. She tells him the truth — a handful of flour, a little oil, and that's the end of it.

He asks her to make him something first.

Read it aloud in full. It's short, and it lands better read than summarized.

The turn. From the outside that looks like recklessness. From the inside it's trust. She wasn't careless with her son's life; she was betting on the character of the God who sent the man.

Then Hebrews, and notice what the promise actually is: never will I leave you. Not you will always have enough. The comfort is a person, not a balance.

Land it. Name the fear specifically. Not "the future." The actual scenario you rehearse at three in the morning.

Illustration options. George Müller, who ran orphanages without ever making an appeal and kept records precisely so the claim could be tested. Alternates: LeTourneau pledging from underneath a six-figure loss; a local person who gave when it was frightening.

Pastoral note. This is the most pastoral message of the six and the one most likely to surface something that isn't about money. Have your care team ready and say from the platform that someone is available afterward.

Close. No exercise card this week. Pray, and give them room.

Next week. "Once the grip loosens, a different question shows up. Not how much — where."

WEEK FOUR · WHAT DO YOU SEE?

Big idea. Sight comes before assignment. A person who can't name anything specific will give generically, to whoever asks best.

Text. Jeremiah 1:11–13 · Amos 7:7–8 · Nehemiah 2:11–18 · Luke 10:30–37

The arc

Open on the question. When God commissions the prophets, he doesn't ask what they'll do. He asks what they see. Jeremiah is shown a branch. Amos is shown a plumb line. The assignment follows the sight, every time.

The text. Nehemiah hears about the walls and weeps, but he doesn't announce a plan. He rides out at night and looks at them himself, alone, before he says a word to anybody. Then the Samaritan — the priest and the Levite weren't cruel, they were on their way somewhere. What made the third man different was that he was willing to be interrupted.

The turn. You fund what you can picture. That's not shallowness, it's how attention works. Which means the most spiritually significant thing many of us could do this year is go somewhere and look.

Land it. Vision usually arrives through what you already have access to, already know how to do, or have already grieved. You aren't required to invent a calling.

Illustration options. Humphrey Monmouth, a London cloth merchant who saw an English Bible before one existed — gave Tyndale money, a room in his own house, and his merchant ships to smuggle the printed copies back into England, and spent a year in the Tower for it. Alternate: Lady Huntingdon, who couldn't preach and had a house and a guest list instead.

Close. Three things you've seen in five years you couldn't stop thinking about. Then schedule one visit before the series ends.

Next week. "Nobody was argued into this. Every generous person I know caught it from somebody. Next week, from whom — and who's catching it from you."

WEEK FIVE · TOGETHER

Big idea. Children inherit what they helped decide. Instruction transmits almost nothing.

Text. Joshua 4:20–24 · Deuteronomy 6:4–9 · Acts 4:32–35

The arc

Open on who taught you. Ask the room to think of one person whose generosity they watched up close. Almost nobody will name a sermon.

The text. Israel crosses the Jordan and Joshua has them stack twelve stones where everyone can see. The stated reason is startlingly practical — so that when children ask what these mean, someone will have to tell the story.

The turn. Most families have no stones. No object, no date, no repeated telling. Just assets that show up at the end with no explanation attached, and a next generation left to guess what any of it meant.

Land it. A family with one generous parent and four silent heirs doesn't produce a legacy. It produces litigation.

Illustration options. David Green moving Hobby Lobby's ownership into a trust the family can't reach — a Green who wants income from the business applies for a job. Alternates: a four-generation family in your own church; the Barnhart giving group, where dozens of employees and spouses decide together.

Widen it explicitly. Say from the platform that a household is whoever you share life with. People without children or family nearby will otherwise sit through this message convinced it wasn't for them.

Close. Build a stone this week. And have one conversation about a gift you'll decide together.

Next week. "Six weeks ago we started with who owns it. Next week, what it's all actually for."

WEEK SIX · THE RETURN

Big idea. The giver is the primary beneficiary — and there's an ending worth aiming at.

Text. Acts 20:35 · 2 Corinthians 8:1–5 · 2 Corinthians 9:6–8 · Matthew 24:14 · Revelation 7:9

The arc

Open on what changed. Ask what's different after six weeks. Take three or four answers from the room if your setting allows it.

The text. Jesus says it's more blessed to give than to receive, and that our hearts follow our treasure rather than leading it — which means giving isn't the report card of a changed heart, it's the mechanism of one.

The guardrail, and do not skip it. God is generous, the return is real, and none of that is a promise that giving makes you wealthy. Paul praises the Macedonians for giving beyond their ability out of severe poverty, and never once reports that they got rich. He reports that they gave themselves to the Lord first and the money followed. Say this plainly. A final message that promises return without cost undoes five good weeks.

The turn. Almost every organization in your life offers you perpetual need. Almost nobody offers you a finish. But there is one — a great multitude from every nation, tribe, people and language. The work is finishable, and somebody will write the last check.

Land it. What would be true when your family's work is done?

Illustration options. Todd Harper's two decades of watching what generosity does to the giver, held alongside Bob Buford — who organized his life around the hundredfold and buried his only son. Both, together. That pairing is what makes the joy believable.

Close. The end-state sentence. One sentence, no qualifiers.

And then nothing. No offering appeal. No card. No next steps slide with a giving link. Somebody in that room has been waiting six weeks to see whether you meant it. Pray, and dismiss them.

SERIES MECHANICS

Length. Standard for your church. These outlines assume 30–40 minutes.

What connects the tracks. Each message closes with the same exercise the daily reader assigns that week and the group session works through. A person in all three tracks hears it three times in three registers. A person in only one still gets a whole message.

Guest teachers. Weeks 3 and 5 are the ones a campus pastor or a lay leader can carry. Weeks 1, 2, and 6 should come from the senior pastor — ownership, the finish line, and the close all depend on the congregation believing the person saying it has done it.

Testimonies. One per week, three minutes, no dollar amounts, briefed personally. Vary them across the income range of the congregation. If all six come from your wealthiest households, the series quietly teaches that generosity belongs to them.

The one thing that ruins it. An ask in week five or six, however gentle. Including a soft one. Including a well-meant offer to help.
