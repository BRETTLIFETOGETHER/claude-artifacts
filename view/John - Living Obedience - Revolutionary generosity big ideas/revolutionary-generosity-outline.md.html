REVOLUTIONARY GENEROSITY

How Families Give While They Live, Give More Than Cash, and Leave More Than Money

Steve French and Dale Armstrong — The Signatry

Status: concept / exploratory. No publisher, no contract, no author agreement in place. Chapter titles below are proposed; the five-part architecture is the previously approved structure. Every story cited is documented in public sources and requires written permission before publication.

Target length: 60,000–68,000 words | 20 chapters across 5 parts | ~3,000 words per chapter

THE PREMISE

Most Christian books about money argue for a percentage. This one argues for a transfer.

The thesis is that a family becomes revolutionary in its generosity at three specific breaks with normal Christian giving: it gives during life rather than at death, it gives assets rather than cash, and it forms the next generation into givers rather than heirs. Each break is a decision, each decision has a documented track record, and together they move a household from writing checks to deploying what it holds. The book's promise to the reader is not a bigger tithe. It is a different relationship to everything they own — and a family that still likes each other in forty years.

The reader: a Christian household with assets — a business owner five years from an exit, a family two years past one, a couple in their fifties who have more than they need and no plan for it, and the next generation watching to see whether any of it means anything.

Why these two authors: French is a business owner who bought and sold companies before he ever ran a foundation, and who names a failure rather than a success as the moment God settled the ownership question with him. Armstrong has spent a career on the other side of the table, in the rooms where generosity either gets asked about or doesn't. Conviction and mechanics, in one book, from people who have done both.

The editorial rule that protects the book: the manuscript never sells a donor advised fund. No Signatry pitch in the body, no case study that functions as a brochure, all vehicle mechanics confined to the appendices. Borrowed from Generous Giving's discipline of never asking. A book from a DAF sponsor that never asks is the only version of this book that gets endorsed by the rest of the movement.

Introduction — "The Word We Have to Earn." Opens by admitting that revolutionary is a word The Signatry has used for years and that most books don't deserve. Defines it against three specific breaks, promises the evidence, and tells the reader that the last chapter is about an ending rather than a growth curve.

PART I — WHO OWNS THIS?

1. The Steward and the Owner

Premise: Nearly everyone affirms that God owns it all and manages the money as if they own it, and the gap between the two is where this book lives.

  • Anchor story: French's own — adopted, humble start, more than fifteen companies acquired, and a failure he ranks among the defining events of his life. His summary of what God was teaching him: he had thought he was both the steward and the owner.
  • Ideas carried: ownership as an actual title transfer, not a metaphor; the categories of giver (tither → percentage giver → finish-line giver → asset giver → owner-transferor) so a reader can locate himself honestly on page 20.
  • Scripture spine: Psalm 24:1; 1 Chronicles 29:11–14; Deuteronomy 8:17–18; 1 Corinthians 4:7.
  • Practice installed: a signed, dated one-page ownership statement, reviewed annually.
  • Closing question: If God owns it, what document says so?

2. The Camel and the Needle

Premise: Scripture's warnings to the wealthy are not decorative, and the people who took them literally are the ones with the best stories.

  • Anchor story: Alan Barnhart at 24, reading Matthew 19 and then working through hundreds of passages over roughly two years before changing anything about the business.
  • Ideas carried: the two-year study as the actual method; fear rather than greed as the real enemy — almost nobody withholds because they love money, they withhold because they are afraid.
  • Scripture spine: Matthew 19:16–26; Luke 12:13–21; 1 Timothy 6:9–10; Mark 4:19.
  • Practice installed: a reading plan, not a decision — the chapter asks the reader to study before he commits.
  • Closing question: What are you actually afraid of?

3. How Much Is Enough

Premise: The tithe is a floor argument. The finish line is a ceiling argument, and ceilings are what set people free.

  • Anchor story: the Barnharts capping their income at the level of a ministry worker in year one, when there was almost nothing to cap — and the reflection years later that they gave up some things but none of the good ones.
  • Ideas carried: the keep question rather than the give question, an inversion April Chapman has pressed for years; separating the lifestyle line from the giving line so every future increase becomes a giving decision automatically.
  • Scripture spine: Proverbs 30:8–9; 1 Timothy 6:6–8; Philippians 4:11–13; Exodus 16 (manna that could not be stored).
  • Practice installed: name the number, name the review date, tell two people.
  • Closing question: What is your number, and who knows it?

4. Decide Before the Money Comes

Premise: Convictions formed before wealth survive wealth. Convictions formed after it get renegotiated.

  • Anchor story: R.G. LeTourneau, thirty years old and deeply in debt, deciding to treat God as his business partner — the ninety percent came later and by increments.
  • Ideas carried: public declaration as a spiritual technology (Barnhart told new hires the fruits of their labor would not fund his lifestyle, and having said it, he was committed); the escalating ladder; give while you live, because an estate plan teaches nothing and a father who gave away the building and explained why teaches everything.
  • Scripture spine: Matthew 6:19–21; Matthew 6:24; Luke 12:33–34.
  • Practice installed: a written annual escalation — this year's percentage, next year's, and the top rung.
  • Closing question: Who have you told?

PART II — WHAT YOU ACTUALLY OWN

5. The Checkbook Is the Smallest Room in the House

Premise: Roughly nine-tenths of wealth sits outside cash, and almost all giving comes out of the checking account.

  • Anchor story: a family that assumed generosity meant a bigger check and discovered the gift was already on the balance sheet.
  • Ideas carried: the bottleneck is competence, not character — people are more willing than they are equipped; the gap between intent and gift is filled with valuations, appraisals, board approvals, and a lawyer who has never done this before.
  • Scripture spine: Deuteronomy 26:1–11 (firstfruits — the offering is the asset, not the currency); Proverbs 3:9–10.
  • Practice installed: an asset inventory — list everything owned, circle what is illiquid, note what appreciated.
  • Closing question: What do you own that you have never once considered giving?

6. Give the Asset, Never the Proceeds

Premise: Same heart, same asset, radically different Kingdom outcome — the whole difference is the sequence.

  • Anchor story: Brent Haverkamp, who calculated that funding a college ministry project meant selling two rental properties to cover the gift plus the tax, then gave one property to the fund before selling it instead. And David and Casey Trogden, whose giving began with used medical equipment from the business going to Haiti and grew into gifts of privately held stock.
  • Ideas carried: your business can be the missionary — owners keep waiting to have money to give when they already have inventory, capacity, equipment, expertise, and access.
  • Scripture spine: 2 Corinthians 8:12; Luke 16:1–13 (using worldly wealth to make friends for eternity).
  • Practice installed: before any sale, ask one question — can the asset be given before it is sold?
  • Closing question: What is under contract right now?

7. Before the Letter of Intent

Premise: Liquidity is the moment of maximum spiritual danger and maximum Kingdom opportunity, and the window closes earlier than owners think.

  • Anchor story: French's own exit, and the pattern he and Armstrong see repeatedly — the owner who learns about this two weeks too late, from an advisor who assumed he was not interested.
  • Ideas carried: after a sale, a lifetime of purpose collapses into guarding a pile, and the builder becomes a security guard; the eighteen months around an exit determine the next thirty years.
  • Scripture spine: Luke 12:16–21; Ecclesiastes 5:10–15.
  • Practice installed: the pre-exit checklist — eight questions to answer before a letter of intent (Appendix C).
  • Closing question: How many years are you from a possible sale — and does anyone advising you know what you believe about money?

8. Structure Is Ministry

Premise: Feelings decay. Documents don't. Structure is what makes conviction outlive the person who had it.

  • Anchor story: David Green moving Hobby Lobby's voting stock into a mission-oriented trust the family cannot reach — a Green who wants to earn a living from the business applies for a job. Alongside it: Stanley Tam, who made God the owner of his plastics company in 1936 and became an employee of the business he founded. And the Barnharts, who placed 99 percent of the company into a trust at the National Christian Foundation in 2007.
  • Ideas carried: stewardship can be passed; ownership can only be inherited — the distinction that solves the fairness problem, the fragmentation problem, and the fourth-generation problem at once; friction is not neutral, it quietly redirects generosity into accumulation.
  • Scripture spine: Luke 12:42–48; Matthew 25:14–30.
  • Practice installed: review the documents against the convictions from chapter 1 — do they match?
  • Closing question: If you died tonight, what would your paperwork say you believed?

PART III — THE FAMILY IS THE UNIT

9. God Works Through Families

Premise: Philanthropy speaks in individuals. Scripture and inheritance both operate on households.

  • Anchor story: French's recurring conviction that God has a vision and mission for every family, and that it is not a one-generational purpose.
  • Ideas carried: the family, not the donor, is the unit of generosity; one generous parent and four silent heirs produces litigation, not legacy.
  • Scripture spine: Deuteronomy 6:4–9; Psalm 78:4–7; Joshua 4 (the memorial stones).
  • Practice installed: write the family's purpose statement — one page, read aloud at the next gathering.
  • Closing question: Could your children state what your family is for?

10. Equal Isn't Always Fair

Premise: Dividing an estate evenly is often the lazy choice rather than the loving one.

  • Anchor story: Green's judgment that handing the company to his heirs would be unfair to him and potentially ruinous to them. Set alongside the line of thinking Ron Blue has advanced for decades on inheritance as a parenting decision with a long tail.
  • Ideas carried: wealth without a job is a spiritual risk to your children, not a gift; an inheritance is a job assignment, and most estate plans assign nothing.
  • Scripture spine: Proverbs 13:22; Proverbs 20:21; Luke 15:11–32.
  • Practice installed: decide what amount is helpful rather than what amount is possible — and say it out loud while everyone is alive to discuss it.
  • Closing question: What is the most loving number for each child, and is it the same number?

11. The Family Giving Table

Premise: Generosity that stays in the parents' hands forms nobody.

  • Anchor story: Barnhart Crane's employee group — about 55 employees and spouses who research recipients, build real relationships with them, and allocate roughly half the company's annual profits. One generous owner converted into dozens of formed givers.
  • Ideas carried: give the decision away, not just the money; champion a cause rather than servicing a list, because concentration produces expertise and expertise produces joy.
  • Scripture spine: Acts 6:1–7; 2 Corinthians 8:16–24 (giving administered by more than one set of hands).
  • Practice installed: the annual family meeting with real dollars the next generation allocates and defends.
  • Closing question: When did your children last give away money that mattered to them?

12. Four Generations

Premise: Anyone can be generous once. The claim of this book is generational, so the evidence has to be.

  • Anchor story: a family practicing generosity since 1965 across four generations, carrying a family foundation while deliberately teaching the value to children and grandchildren.
  • Ideas carried: the great wealth transfer is a discipleship event currently being handled by lawyers; what passes down is what gets rehearsed, and most families rehearse nothing.
  • Scripture spine: 2 Timothy 1:5; Psalm 145:4; Deuteronomy 4:9.
  • Practice installed: record the family's story — the origin, the hard years, the decisions and why they were made.
  • Closing question: Who in your family can tell the story without you in the room?

PART IV — THE COMPANY YOU KEEP

13. The Ask-Free Room

Premise: Trust is built where nobody is selling, and that constraint is what produces the candor.

  • Anchor story: the Journey of Generosity — a pressure-free retreat where no one solicits and no one is told where to give — and the fact that Generous Giving has been privately funded since 2000 precisely so it never has to ask.
  • Ideas carried: wealthy Christians spend their lives being handled; loneliness rather than stinginess is the more common condition.
  • Scripture spine: Matthew 6:1–4; Ecclesiastes 4:9–12.
  • Practice installed: one ask-free retreat a year.
  • Closing question: When was the last time you talked about money with someone who wanted nothing from you?

14. The Table Beats the Transaction

Premise: Generous families do their best work in the company of other generous families.

  • Anchor story: the convening model — peer communities of high-capacity givers, of which The Gathering is the most established example, where the conversation raises both the quality of the giving and the courage of the giver.
  • Ideas carried: solo philanthropy tends toward the sentimental or the defensive; a table of peers asks better questions and funds harder problems.
  • Scripture spine: Proverbs 27:17; Hebrews 10:24–25.
  • Practice installed: find or build a table of six to twelve — and meet on a calendar, not on impulse.
  • Closing question: Who sharpens your giving?

15. The Advisor Is the Gate

Premise: Most charitable intent in America dies quietly in a professional's office because nobody asked.

  • Anchor story: Armstrong's territory — the conversations that happen and the ones that don't, and what it costs a family when the question is never raised.
  • Ideas carried: the advisor who raises generosity well doesn't lose assets, he gains trust, referrals, and the next two generations of the family; this is the fastest distribution channel in the entire movement.
  • Scripture spine: Proverbs 11:14; Proverbs 15:22.
  • Practice installed: ask your advisor the question directly; if he can't answer it, find one who can.
  • Closing question: Does the person managing your money know what you believe about it?

16. The Church That Isn't Asking

Premise: Most churches only talk about money when they need some, which trains congregations to hear every mention of giving as an ask.

  • Anchor story: the pastor who preaches generosity in a year with no campaign, and what changes in the room.
  • Ideas carried: generosity is a discipleship strategy, not a fundraising strategy; giving usually rises when a church makes the switch, but the chapter has to insist that is a side effect or it becomes the thing it is arguing against.
  • Scripture spine: 2 Corinthians 9:6–15; Malachi 3:8–12 (handled with care, not as leverage).
  • Practice installed: for pastors — one generosity series in a non-campaign year.
  • Closing question: What does your church teach about money when it needs nothing?

PART V — WHAT IT'S ALL FOR

17. Fund the Problem, Not the Pitch

Premise: Most philanthropy is allocated by fundraising skill, which is a poor proxy for effectiveness.

  • Anchor story: the Barnhart giving group deliberately searching for the best people working on a problem rather than the organizations with the strongest development departments.
  • Ideas carried: from charity to problem-solving — problem-solving requires concentration, patience, collaboration, and a willingness to fund the unglamorous middle of a decade-long effort; measure how fast the money leaves rather than how much has accumulated.
  • Scripture spine: Isaiah 58:6–12; James 2:14–17.
  • Practice installed: publish your household's outflow rate to your own family once a year.
  • Closing question: What would winning look like, and who is closest to it?

18. Generosity Travels

Premise: This is not American affluence dressed in Scripture.

  • Anchor story: Daryl Heald's work — a commercial real estate broker who joined the Maclellan Foundation in 1997, helped launch Generous Giving, Giving Wisely, and Generosity Path, and whose retreats have reached roughly 100,000 people across 90 countries in 40 languages.
  • Ideas carried: generosity travels better than affluence; the giver in a poor country is teaching the giver in a rich one, and the next decade of growth is more likely to come from the global church than from another wave of American exits.
  • Scripture spine: 2 Corinthians 8:1–5 (the Macedonians gave beyond their ability, out of poverty — the passage that keeps this book from being only for the rich); Mark 12:41–44.
  • Practice installed: fund something you will never visit, chosen by someone who lives there.
  • Closing question: Whose generosity puts yours in perspective?

19. The Return Is Joy

Premise: The giver is the primary beneficiary, and the joy is real rather than compensatory.

  • Anchor story: two decades of Todd Harper's observations from inside those rooms, and April Chapman's conviction that generosity is among the best evangelism tools available.
  • Ideas carried: your heart follows your treasure rather than the reverse — giving is the mechanism of change, not the report card; the applause problem is its own trap, which is why anonymity protects the giver.
  • Scripture spine: Acts 20:35; 2 Corinthians 9:7; Matthew 6:19–21; Proverbs 11:24–25.
  • Practice installed: give one gift this year that nobody will ever know you gave.
  • Closing question: Who is being changed by your giving — and is it you?

20. The Last Check

Premise: Aim at an end, not at growth.

  • Anchor story: The Signatry's vision statement, which imagines a finish line rather than a growth curve — the final check written for the final missionary sent to the final unreached people group, so that the last person hears the gospel.
  • Ideas carried: a family that adopts an end-state vision starts asking when the work could be finished rather than how much it should give this year, and that question reorganizes a balance sheet faster than any sermon.
  • Scripture spine: Matthew 24:14; Revelation 7:9; 1 Timothy 6:17–19.
  • Practice installed: write the family's end-state sentence — what will be true when the work is done?
  • Closing prayer.

BACK MATTER

Appendix A — Ten Practices With a Track Record. The practical spine, pulled out of the narrative so a reader can act without re-reading: settle ownership in writing; set a financial finish line; cap the lifestyle and let the giving float; give the asset, never the proceeds; give before the letter of intent; escalate the percentage annually; give as a family with real money and real votes; concentrate on a cause; sit in an ask-free room; measure how fast the money leaves.

Appendix B — The Financial Finish Line Worksheet. One page. The number, the review date, the two witnesses.

Appendix C — Before the Letter of Intent. The eight questions for any owner within five years of a possible exit. Highest-value page in the book and the one advisors will hand to clients.

Appendix D — Asset Giving Mechanics. Business interests, real estate, appreciated securities, grain and livestock, intellectual property. Written so a family attorney can use it. Every vehicle discussion lives here and nowhere else.

Appendix E — Scripture Index. All references by theme: ownership, warning, enough, heart and treasure, sowing and supply, family and generations, eternal reward. NIV throughout.

Study Guide. Twenty sessions, one per chapter — three discussion questions and one action step each. This is what makes the book adoptable by churches, advisor cohorts, and family offices.

PRODUCTION NOTES

Write in this order, not chapter order: 1, 3, 6, 7, 20. Chapter 1 sets the authors' credibility, chapter 3 carries the most transferable practice, chapters 6 and 7 are the ones the advisor channel will move, and chapter 20 establishes whether the ending is earned. If those five work, the rest is execution.

The gap to close before drafting: eight of the twelve anchor testimonies are business owners and eleven are American. As it stands the book proves a narrower thesis than the cover claims — that revolutionary generosity is what happens to people who sell companies. Source four stories at ordinary income levels and two outside the United States, and place one of them no later than chapter 2.
