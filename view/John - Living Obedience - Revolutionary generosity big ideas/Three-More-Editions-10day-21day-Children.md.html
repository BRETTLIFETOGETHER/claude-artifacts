THREE MORE EDITIONS

The 10-Day Challenge · The 21-Day Journey · The Children's Edition

Drawn from the twelve-module library · LifeTogether Status: developing, August 2026

EDITION ONE — THE 10-DAY CHALLENGE

Ten days. Ten actions. One habit.

What it is for. Not formation — motion. The 40-day campaign changes what a person believes; this changes what they did this week. It is the shareable edition, the one someone forwards to a friend, and the natural on-ramp to everything else.

Delivery. Text message or app, one push per morning. Under 100 words. No group, no book, no leader.

The arc. Possession, person, hidden, time, order, cost, together, sight, stretch, commitment. Each day escalates slightly on the one before.

DAY 1 · Give something you can hold. Not money — an object you actually use. Money is abstract and easy; a thing you have to carry out to the car is not. Something in your house today belongs in someone else's.

DAY 2 · Give to someone who cannot repay you. Jesus said to invite the people who can't return the favor. Most of our generosity quietly circulates among people who might one day be useful to us. Break that today.

DAY 3 · Give without anyone knowing. No credit, no mention, no post. Matthew 6 makes hiddenness a discipline, not false modesty — because the moment a gift needs an audience, the audience starts shaping the gift.

DAY 4 · Give two hours. Time is the resource nobody has, which makes it the most honest thing you own. Give two hours to a person or a work that has no claim on your calendar.

DAY 5 · Give first. Whatever you were going to give this month, give it today — before the bills, before the transfer, before anything else clears. Firstfruits means the first, not the remainder.

DAY 6 · Give something you would rather keep. You have identified it already. It came to mind on Day 1 and you chose something easier. Give that one.

DAY 7 · Decide a gift with someone else. A spouse, a friend, a child, a roommate. Let them have a real vote. Generosity practiced alone forms one person; practiced together it forms two.

DAY 8 · Give to something you have seen with your own eyes. Not a cause. A place you have stood, or a person you have met. If nothing qualifies, that is today's finding — go and look this week.

DAY 9 · Give past comfortable. One gift that changes something about your month. Not reckless. Noticeable. You will learn more about what you believe in this single act than in the previous eight combined.

DAY 10 · Decide what happens next. Write one sentence: For the next year I will ______. An amount, a percentage, a practice, a person. The challenge ends today. The habit starts tomorrow, or it doesn't.

What it feeds. Day 10's sentence is the on-ramp into the 21-day journey or a group. Every church running the challenge should have the next thing ready to hand out on day 9.

EDITION TWO — THE 21-DAY JOURNEY

Three weeks. Three questions.

What it is for. Habit length. Twenty-one days is long enough to install a practice and short enough that a person finishes. This is the January edition, the post-Easter edition, and the personal-reset edition — for individuals or a group that cannot give six weeks.

Daily format. A short story, one Scripture, one practice, one question. Roughly 250 words. Leaner than the 40-day reader on purpose: the campaign reader has three questions and a next step because a whole church is moving together. A person alone needs one question they will actually answer.

What compression costs. The 21-day drops the warning module and the community module as standalone days, folding both into other readings. That is the right sacrifice — a solo reader can absorb the warning through the rich fool on day 10 and doesn't need three days on community they have no group to practice with.

EDITION THREE — THE CHILDREN'S EDITION

Generosity is caught at the kitchen table.

The governing principle. Children do not learn generosity from lessons. They learn it from watching a decision get made and from being handed one of their own. Every hour of teaching is worth less than one afternoon where a child allocated real money and had to explain why.

Two components. A family table version parents lead at home, and a kids ministry version a church runs on a weekend. Both run four weeks, in parallel with the adult campaign.

THE SPINE — THREE JARS

Give, Save, Spend. Physical jars, real money, every week, for the length of the campaign and ideally forever.

It is old, it is unglamorous, and nothing else in children's financial discipleship has its track record. The reason is mechanical: a child who divides money three ways every week has made 200 allocation decisions by age ten, and every one of them was theirs.

The rule that makes it work: the Give jar's destination is the child's choice, not the parent's. A five-year-old who gives to the animal shelter has practiced generosity. A five-year-old who was told where to give has practiced compliance.

THE FAMILY TABLE VERSION

Four conversations, fifteen minutes each, at dinner. Parents lead. No preparation beyond reading the card.

WEEK 1 · Who made all this? Read together: Psalm 24:1 and Genesis 1. Talk about: Everything we have came from somewhere. Who made the trees? Who gave Dad his job? What in this room did we make ourselves, and what did we receive? Do: Set up the three jars. Explain each one. Give each child their first allocation. Ask a child: What is your favorite thing that you did not buy?

WEEK 2 · How much is enough? Read together: Exodus 16 — the bread that wouldn't keep. Talk about: God gave exactly enough for one day, and the people who tried to store extra found it spoiled. Why do you think God did it that way? Do: Each person names one thing they wanted badly and got. Do they still play with it? Ask a child: If you could only keep three toys, which three?

WEEK 3 · The boy with the lunch. Read together: John 6:1–13. Talk about: A boy gave five loaves and two fish to feed five thousand people. It wasn't enough. He gave it anyway. Do: Each child chooses where their Give jar goes. Parents ask questions but do not overrule. Ask a child: What do you have that seems too small to help?

WEEK 4 · Let's do it. Read together: Acts 20:35. Talk about: What did we decide, and why? Do: Empty the Give jars and make the gift together. If possible, go in person. Take a photograph. Ask a child: How did that feel?

The stone. Frame the photograph and put it somewhere visible. That is the object that makes a younger sibling ask what it is — which is the whole point of Joshua 4, and the reason most families transmit nothing.

THE KIDS MINISTRY VERSION

Four sessions, forty-five minutes, elementary age. Each follows the same shape: a game, a story, a verse, and a take-home card that pulls the parents in.

SESSION 1 · It's Not Ours Game: Borrowed. Each child is handed an object belonging to a leader and given one job — take care of it for the hour. At the end they give it back. Then ask: was it ever yours? Story: David gathering everything for the temple and telling everyone it was God's already. Verse: Psalm 24:1. Take home: A jar label sheet and a note to parents explaining the three jars.

SESSION 2 · Enough Game: The bread that spoiled. Give every child a small treat and tell them they can eat it now or save it for next week. Talk about what happened to the manna. Story: The rich fool and his bigger barns, told simply — a man who had plenty and only thought about himself. Verse: 1 Timothy 6:6. Take home: A card with one question for the dinner table: what is enough for our family?

SESSION 3 · The Boy Who Shared Lunch Game: Five loaves. A small amount of something shared across the whole room, so they experience the arithmetic not working and it working anyway. Story: John 6, from the boy's point of view. Verse: Luke 6:38. Take home: A Give jar decision card — the child chooses, the parent listens.

SESSION 4 · Let's Go Game: None. This session goes somewhere or brings someone in. Story: A real person from your church, three minutes, told to children. Verse: Acts 20:35. Do: The class makes one gift together, decided by vote. Take home: The photograph.

THE TEEN EDITION — a note, not a curriculum

Teenagers should not get the children's edition. They should get a slightly adapted version of the adult one, and the reason matters: a fifteen-year-old with a first paycheck is making real allocation decisions and knows it.

What changes for teens. The finish line becomes a conversation about what they think they will need. Comparison becomes the fear module, because their comparison happens on a screen and it is relentless. Ownership stays exactly as written.

The two stories that land. Alan Barnhart was twenty-four with a legal pad and hundreds of Bible passages when he made the decision that shaped forty years. R.G. LeTourneau was thirty and deeply in debt. Neither was a wealthy man deciding what to do with a fortune. Both were young men deciding who they were going to be — which is exactly where a seventeen-year-old is standing.

The practice. Give them a real allocation from the family's giving, require research, and let them defend it to the adults. Then do what they recommend.
