VISIONARY GENEROSITY

Sample Chapter — Chapter Nine

Back the Person, Not the Project

Draft for review. Written in Steve French's first person, per the single-narrator structure. Third sample chapter, chosen because it carries Part III's voice — narrative rather than argumentative — and because it is the chapter that draws most directly on the historical patrons. Two passages marked [SF — confirm or replace] need his own account. Scripture is NIV.

In 1764 a man named John Newton was thirty-nine years old and had almost nothing to show for it.

He had been a sailor, then a slave trader, then a tide surveyor in Liverpool checking cargo on the docks. He had been converted years earlier and had spent most of the decade since trying to get ordained and being turned down. He had no formal education anyone respected. He wrote verse in the evenings that nobody had published. If you had been asked to identify the person in England most likely to shape the next century of the English-speaking church, you would not have chosen the customs official with the criminal past.

A merchant named John Thornton chose him.

Thornton had money — a great deal of it — and what he did with it in Newton's case is the thing I want you to notice, because almost nobody does it. He did not fund a Newton project. There was no project. He arranged for Newton to be placed in a church where he would be heard, gave him counsel and rest and an income, and pushed him to publish the hymns he had been writing privately.

One of those hymns was "Amazing Grace."

We are still singing it. Almost nobody has heard of Thornton.

The instinct that costs the most

Here is the pattern I see in nearly every family I sit with, and I had it myself for years.

We fund projects. A building, a campaign, a translation initiative, a well, a scholarship cohort. Projects are easy to evaluate, easy to explain to a spouse, easy to report to a board, and easy to conclude. They have a beginning, a budget, and an end, and the giver knows exactly what he bought.

They are also, in my experience, the lowest-multiplying use of a serious gift.

Because a project finishes. And then you are back where you started, evaluating the next proposal, and the thing you funded exists but has not made more of anything. Whereas a person — the right person, backed early and held onto — compounds. He gets better. He trains others. He fails, learns something you could not have taught him, and applies it to work you never anticipated funding. Twenty years later there are things in the world that trace back to your decision and were never in any proposal you read.

Bob Buford understood this better than anyone I have known about. He spent the back half of his life funding leaders rather than programs, and the reason Leadership Network mattered was not that it ran good events. It was that a single generous man decided the highest-return asset available to him was other people's capacity, and then organized decades around that conviction.

That is not a strategy you can put in a grant cycle. It requires a patron.

What patronage actually includes

I want to widen this, because if patronage were only about money it would exclude most of the people who could do it.

Look at what Thornton actually gave Newton. Money, yes. But also placement — he used his influence to get a man into a pulpit where he would be heard. Also counsel, over years. Also rest; Thornton's home was somewhere Newton could go. Also insistence, which may have mattered most, because left to himself Newton would probably never have published the hymns.

Look at Humphrey Monmouth and William Tyndale a couple of centuries earlier. Monmouth gave Tyndale money. He also gave him a room in his own house to work in. And then — this is the part I find remarkable — he used his merchant shipping network to smuggle the printed New Testaments back into England, which is not philanthropy, it is logistics. He gave the thing he actually had.

Look at Lady Huntingdon. She had no money problem to solve for George Whitefield and no theological contribution to make. What she had was a house and a guest list. The aristocracy of England would never have stood in a field with common people to hear an evangelist, and they would absolutely accept an invitation to her drawing room. So she filled it, repeatedly, and handed him the room.

Money, placement, access, logistics, counsel, rest, insistence, protection. Every one of those is a patron's contribution, and most of them cost something other than cash.

The bad year

Here is where patronage separates from grantmaking, and it is not a small distinction.

Institutions fund on results, which means they fund into strength. A ministry that has a good year gets renewed. A ministry that has a bad year gets a polite letter and a suggestion to reapply. This is not malice; it is what a fiduciary responsibility to a board produces, and I would probably design it the same way if I were sitting in that chair.

But almost everything worth funding has a bad year. Sometimes two. The founder gets sick, the country closes, the co-leader leaves, the strategy that worked stops working. And the people who were funding results are gone at exactly the moment when staying would have mattered most.

A patron stays. That is essentially the whole job description.

I would go further and say the bad year is where the relationship gets built. Anyone will take your call after a good quarter. The person you funded through a hard one will tell you the truth for the rest of his life — which is the single most valuable thing a giver can possess, and the one almost no giver has, because everyone around us has a reason to manage us.

The Barnhart family's giving group does something that gets at this from a different angle. Rather than reviewing proposals, employees and spouses build actual relationships with the people they fund, over years. They are not evaluating strength. They are close enough to see it when it is not there.

Fewer, longer, looser

Three practices come out of this, and they run against most of what we have been taught about diligence.

Fewer. A family funding thirty organizations is doing something closer to shopping than to patronage. There is no possibility of relationship at thirty. Somewhere between three and eight is where a person can actually know the people, and the concentration itself is what produces the judgment that makes the next decision better.

Longer. Commit for a decade where you can, and say so out loud. The knowledge that you are not being re-evaluated every twelve months changes what a leader is willing to attempt. Short funding produces safe work, every time, and then we blame the leaders for playing it safe.

Looser. Restricted giving is what you do before trust exists. Once it exists, restriction is mostly a way of managing your own anxiety at the expense of someone who knows the work better than you do. That has a limit — I am not arguing for handing money to people you have not vetted — but the number of families who are still restricting gifts to people they have known for fifteen years is very high, and the leaders notice.

Someone will object that unrestricted giving is irresponsible, and I want to take that seriously rather than wave it off. It would be irresponsible as a starting posture. As an earned one, it is simply what trust looks like when it has been converted into practice. You did the diligence at the beginning. The question is whether you are ever going to act like it concluded.

What Paul did

There is a small phrase in Philippians that I did not notice for twenty years of reading it. Paul thanks God for the Philippians' partnership in the gospel from the first day until now, and the word he uses for partnership is the one that gets translated elsewhere as fellowship — the ordinary word for a shared life, and in the commercial usage of the time, something close to a business partnership.

He is not thanking them for a donation. He is describing a decade-long joint venture in which they were principals rather than sponsors.

And in the little letter of 3 John there is an instruction almost nobody preaches: send these people on their way in a manner worthy of God, because they went out for the sake of the Name, and we ought to show hospitality to such people so that we may work together for the truth. Not support them. Work together with them.

That is the posture. Not a donor and a recipient. Two people with different assets, both of them principals, doing one thing.

The work

Name three people, not three organizations. Write down the individuals — not the ministries they lead — whose best work you believe is still ahead of them. If you cannot name three, that is the finding, and the answer is proximity rather than research.

Pick one and lengthen the commitment. Tell them the number and the horizon. Watch what it does to what they are willing to plan.

Inventory what you have besides money. Relationships, a board seat, a building, a network, a skill, a reputation, an introduction that would take you ten minutes and change someone's decade. Most patrons underestimate this list badly.

Ask one of them what they would attempt if funding were not the constraint. Then be quiet and listen to the whole answer.

For reflection

  1. Whose best work is still ahead of them, and are you close enough to know?

  2. What have you restricted out of trust, and what have you restricted out of anxiety?

  3. If someone you fund had a genuinely bad year this year, would you find out — and what would you do?

A prayer

Father, you have given me things that are not money and I have been slow to count them. Show me the person rather than the proposal. Give me the patience to stay through a year that produces nothing I can report, and the humility to be a partner rather than a sponsor. Let there be someone twenty years from now whose work you used, who could not have done it without what you gave me — and let almost nobody know. Amen.
