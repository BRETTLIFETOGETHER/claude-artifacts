Building the visionary/obedient generosity outline for the French book, with why and how per chapter

VISIONARY GENEROSITY

How Big Vision and a Settled Yes Fund the Work of God

Steve French with Dale Armstrong — The Signatry

Status: concept / exploratory. Working title. No publisher, no author agreement. Every story cited below is documented in public sources; all require written permission before publication.

Alternate titles: What Do You See? · Behind the Curtain · The Patron's Vision

Target length: 60,000–68,000 words | 16 chapters across 4 parts

THE THESIS

Two engines drive generosity, and almost every book on the subject runs on only one.

Vision is the pull — what a person can see, and how big it is. Obedience is the yes — the willingness to act when told, before the outcome is visible. Vision without obedience produces talk: grand plans, deferred giving, buildings with a family name on them, a foundation that admires its own balance sheet. Obedience without vision produces faithfulness that never becomes catalytic: a lifetime of good, small, reactive gifts from someone who could have funded a movement and never saw one worth funding.

Both engines together produce the people this book is about. They saw something before it existed, and they said yes to a specific assignment inside it before anyone could prove it would work. History remembers the person on the platform. This book is about the one who paid for the platform.

That tension is what makes this a book rather than a theme. It also solves a problem the word obedient creates on its own — obedience sounds like compliance until you pair it with something magnificent, at which point it sounds like the only reasonable response.

The reader: business owners, families with assets, and the advisors who serve them — people with capacity who have never been handed a vision large enough to require it.

THE STORY ARCHITECTURE

Three sources of story, braided rather than sequenced.

Historical patrons. Humphrey Monmouth, who funded and sheltered Tyndale and did a year in the Tower of London for it. Lady Huntingdon, who gave Whitefield a room full of aristocrats when the churches were closed to him. John Thornton, who placed John Newton in an influential pulpit and pushed him to publish the hymns. John Rinehart's Gospel Patrons introduced these to a generation of givers; this book uses them differently, as evidence for the two-engine argument rather than as inspiration alone.

Living patrons. Alan and Katherine Barnhart. David and Barbara Green. Rick Warren. The Signatry families — Haverkamp, Trogden, Kouplen, Davis. R.G. LeTourneau and Stanley Tam as the mid-century bridge.

The authors. French's own failure, his own missed giving opportunity at a business sale, and what both taught him about who owns what.

Biblical patrons. Luke 8:1–3 — women funding Jesus's ministry out of their own means, named once and then largely forgotten, which is the pattern the whole book is describing. Barnabas selling the field in Acts 4. Lydia, Phoebe, Philemon, Gaius.

PART I — THE TWO ENGINES

1. Big Hearts, Big Vision

Premise: Generous people plateau not because their hearts shrink but because their vision does.

Why: Most stewardship teaching aims at the heart and assumes vision will follow. It doesn't. A person can have a fully surrendered heart and still give to whatever crosses the desk, because nobody ever showed them something worth their whole capacity. The two engines have to be named separately before they can be run together.

How: Open with the diagnostic — a reader assesses which engine is missing. High heart, low vision produces scattered faithful giving. High vision, low heart produces announced intentions and delayed gifts. The chapter ends with the reader locating himself.

Story: French's own account — adopted, humble start, more than fifteen companies acquired, and the failure he ranks above his successes because it settled the ownership question permanently.

Scripture: Proverbs 29:18; 1 Chronicles 29:11–14.

2. What Do You See?

Premise: God's recurring question to the prophets is not what will you do but what do you see.

Why: Vision is a spiritual capacity before it's a strategic exercise. Jeremiah, Amos, and Zechariah are all asked what they see, and the assignment follows the sight. A family that cannot see anything specific will give generically, which is why so much wealth ends up funding whatever asks loudest.

How: The practice of looking — going where the work is, staying long enough to see past the presentation, and writing down what you actually saw rather than what you were told.

Story: Humphrey Monmouth, a cloth merchant who saw an English Bible before one existed, gave Tyndale a room in his own house, and used his merchant ships to smuggle the printed New Testaments back into England.

Scripture: Jeremiah 1:11–13; Amos 7:8; Habakkuk 2:2–3.

3. The Yes Underneath

Premise: Vision is worthless without a settled willingness underneath it, decided before the specific ask arrives.

Why: Every large gift in this book was preceded by a decision made earlier, in quieter circumstances, about who owned what. The people who deliberate each opportunity on its merits give far less than the people who settled the question once. Obedience here means the posture of someone close enough to be told — ob-audire, to listen toward — not compliance with a rule.

How: The pre-decided yes. Settle ownership in writing, name the lifestyle number, and reduce the live questions to how much and when.

Story: Alan Barnhart at twenty-four, reading Matthew 19 and then working through hundreds of passages over two years before changing anything, then capping his income in the first year of the business when there was almost nothing to cap.

Scripture: 1 Samuel 15:22; 2 Corinthians 8:5.

4. When Vision Runs Without Obedience

Premise: The failure mode of visionary giving is not stinginess. It's empire.

Why: Vision untethered from obedience becomes ambition wearing a Kingdom vocabulary — the wing with the family name, the initiative that exists because the donor wanted it rather than because God asked for it, the foundation that outlives its purpose and funds its own staff. This chapter has to be written honestly or the rest of the book is dangerous.

How: The tests that keep vision accountable — is anyone besides you convinced, does it cost you something you'd rather keep, would you fund it if your name never appeared, and who tells you no.

Story: Told carefully and without naming individuals; the pattern is common enough that no reader will need a name.

Scripture: Matthew 6:1–4; Acts 5:1–11.

PART II — WHERE VISION COMES FROM

5. You Can't Fund What You Haven't Seen

Premise: Vision is a function of proximity, not intelligence.

Why: Donors who fund from a distance fund what is well presented. Donors who have been in the room fund what is actually working. The difference is not sophistication — it's that one of them has met the people.

How: Go. Schedule the visit before the gift, not after. Bring the children. Stay past the tour.

Story: David Trogden, whose giving began when he learned that used medical equipment from his business could serve a community in Haiti — a gift that required proximity to imagine and nothing extraordinary to make.

Scripture: Nehemiah 2:11–18; Luke 10:33.

6. The Size of the Assignment

Premise: Small vision produces small giving regardless of capacity.

Why: People give in proportion to what they believe is at stake, not in proportion to what they have. A family worth fifty million with a vision the size of a building campaign will give like a family worth five. Enlarging the vision is therefore the most financially consequential thing a leader can do for a donor — and the least commonly attempted.

How: How to size an assignment honestly: name the problem, name what finished looks like, name what it costs, and refuse to inflate any of the three.

Story: Rick Warren's public willingness to reorganize an entire church's ambition around global scale, and the giving that followed. Handled from his published material only.

Scripture: Isaiah 54:2; Ephesians 3:20.

7. God's Rate of Return

Premise: Reframing giving as investment is not a gimmick; it's how the people in this book actually think.

Why: For an audience that built wealth by allocating capital, sacrifice language lands as loss and return language lands as reason. French's framing is direct — where a strong human return is two or three times, he puts God's at a hundred or a thousand. The theological caution matters here: this is not a promise of financial return, and the chapter has to say so plainly or it drifts into prosperity teaching.

How: Evaluate gifts the way you'd evaluate capital — what does this produce, what compounds, what is consumed. Then hold the results loosely, because the ledger isn't visible from here.

Story: LeTourneau, who liked to say God's shovel was bigger than his, and who reached a reversed tithe by increments rather than in one leap.

Scripture: Matthew 13:8, 23; Luke 6:38; Malachi 3:10, read with care.

8. Vision Is Given, Not Generated

Premise: You don't brainstorm a calling. You receive one, usually through the life you were already living.

Why: Donors exhaust themselves trying to manufacture passion. In practice, vision arrives through position, history, wound, or proximity — the thing you already know, the place you already are, the pain you already carry. That's a relief rather than a limitation.

How: The inventory — what has God already given you access to, expertise in, or grief over? Fund there first.

Story: Lady Huntingdon, whose vision came directly out of her position: she couldn't preach, but she could fill a drawing room with the aristocracy who would never have stood in a field to hear Whitefield.

Scripture: Esther 4:14; 1 Corinthians 12:4–7.

PART III — THE PATRON'S CRAFT

9. Back the Person, Not the Project

Premise: The best gifts in history were bets on people, made early and held long.

Why: Projects are fundable and forgettable; people compound. Every historical patron in this book backed an individual whose best work was still ahead of him, and stayed through the failures. Grant cycles can't do this. Patrons can.

How: Choose fewer people, fund them longer, give unrestricted where trust is earned, and make the relationship real enough that they'd tell you the truth about a bad year.

Story: John Thornton, who didn't merely fund John Newton — he placed him in an influential church, gave him counsel and rest, and pressed him to publish the hymns that included Amazing Grace.

Scripture: Philippians 1:3–7; 3 John 5–8.

10. Fund Before It's Provable

Premise: By the time a work is demonstrably successful, your money is no longer the decisive money.

Why: Patrons supply risk capital. The gift that mattered was made when the outcome was uncertain, the work was unpopular, and the founder had no track record. This is uncomfortable for people trained to require evidence, and it's precisely where their capacity is most useful.

How: Set aside a defined portion of annual giving as risk capital, with a stated tolerance for loss and no expectation of a report.

Story: Monmouth funding Tyndale when unauthorized translation was illegal — and paying for it with a year in the Tower of London, a cost Tyndale himself never bore in that form.

Scripture: Ecclesiastes 11:1–6; Hebrews 11:8.

11. Fund the Unglamorous Middle

Premise: Most philanthropy is allocated by fundraising skill, which is a poor proxy for effectiveness.

Why: Launches and buildings attract money; the seventh year of a translation project does not. The patron's advantage is patience — the willingness to fund the part of the work nobody photographs.

How: Ask what would break if funding stopped in year four, and fund that. Measure how fast money leaves rather than how much accumulates.

Story: The Barnhart giving group — dozens of employees and spouses who research recipients, build relationships with them, and deliberately look for the best people addressing a problem rather than the organizations with the strongest development departments.

Scripture: Galatians 6:9; 1 Corinthians 3:6–8.

12. Stay Behind the Curtain

Premise: The patron's power depends on not needing the platform.

Why: Recognition is the tax that quietly consumes the vision. The moment a gift requires acknowledgment, the assignment shifts from the work to the giver, and the giver's judgment degrades. Anonymity is not false humility; it's operational protection.

How: Give one significant gift a year that nobody will ever attribute to you. Decline the naming opportunity once, on purpose, and notice what it costs.

Story: The women of Luke 8, who supported Jesus's ministry out of their own means and appear in three verses; and Thornton and Huntingdon, whose names most Christians have never heard while singing what they funded.

Scripture: Luke 8:1–3; Matthew 6:3–4.

PART IV — BUILDING A FAMILY THAT CAN SAY YES

13. Capacity: Give the Asset, Not the Proceeds

Premise: You cannot obey a large prompt with a small checkbook.

Why: Vision routinely outruns cash. Most families hold nine-tenths of their wealth outside the checking account and give almost entirely from inside it, which caps the size of every yes they'll ever say. The mechanism is not a detail — it's what makes obedience possible at scale.

How: Inventory the illiquid assets. Gift the business interest, the property, the appreciated stock before a sale is under contract. Learn the sequence once and it applies for life.

Story: Brent Haverkamp, who calculated that funding a ministry project meant selling two rental properties to cover the gift plus the tax, and instead gave one property to the fund before selling it. Alongside French's own regret — the question about why nobody told him.

Scripture: Deuteronomy 26:1–11; 2 Corinthians 8:12.

14. Speed: A Yes That Takes Days, Not Years

Premise: Most prompts die in the machinery.

Why: A family that needs eighteen months, three advisors, and a board vote to make a gift will miss most of what it's asked to do. Friction is not neutral; it silently converts generosity into accumulation. Structure is what lets a conviction become a transfer while the moment is still live.

How: Build the giving infrastructure before you need it — the fund, the margin, the pre-agreed authority, the advisor who already knows what you believe.

Story: The mechanics behind a same-week gift, told through a family who had prepared for a moment they couldn't have predicted.

Scripture: Proverbs 3:27–28; 2 Corinthians 9:5.

15. The Family That Sees Together

Premise: Vision transmits through participation, not instruction.

Why: Children inherit what they helped decide. A family whose next generation has never researched a cause, argued about a grant, or met a recipient will inherit assets without the vision that made them meaningful — which is how third-generation wealth becomes litigation. French's conviction is that God has always intended to work through families, and that a family's purpose is not a single-generation matter.

How: The annual family meeting with real dollars the next generation allocates and defends. Write the family's vision statement. Take the children on the visit.

Story: David Green moving Hobby Lobby's voting stock into a mission-oriented trust the family cannot reach — a Green who wants income from the business applies for a job. And a family practicing generosity across four generations since 1965.

Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4–9; Psalm 78:4–7; Joshua 4:20–24.

16. The Last Check

Premise: The largest vision available is a finished one.

Why: Almost every organization offers donors perpetual need. Almost none offers a finish. A vision with an end reorganizes a balance sheet in a way that no appeal for sustaining support ever will, because it converts a donor into someone with an assignment and a deadline.

How: Write the family's end-state sentence — what will be true when the work is done — and then work backward to this year's number.

Story: The Signatry's vision of a finish rather than a growth curve: the last check written for the last missionary sent to the last unreached people group.

Scripture: Matthew 24:14; Revelation 7:9; 2 Timothy 4:7.

Closing prayer.

BACK MATTER

Appendix A — The Vision Inventory. Twelve questions to surface what a family has already been given access to, expertise in, or grief over.

Appendix B — The Pre-Decided Yes. One page: ownership statement, lifestyle number, review date, two witnesses.

Appendix C — Before the Letter of Intent. Eight questions for any owner within five years of a possible sale.

Appendix D — Asset Giving Mechanics. Business interests, real estate, appreciated securities, grain, intellectual property. All vehicle discussion lives here and nowhere else in the book.

Study Guide. Sixteen sessions — three discussion questions and one action step per chapter. Built for family offices, advisor cohorts, and church classes alike.

PRODUCTION NOTES

Draft in this order: 1, 3, 6, 13, 16. Chapter 1 establishes the two-engine argument, chapter 3 carries the most transferable practice, chapter 6 is where a leader learns to enlarge a donor's vision, chapter 13 is the mechanism, and chapter 16 determines whether the ending is earned.

Differentiation from Gospel Patrons. Rinehart owns the historical-patron lane and owns it well. This book is not a rival history — it uses three of the same figures as evidence in an argument he doesn't make, then does two things he doesn't: it pairs the historical patrons with living families whose numbers can be examined, and it supplies the mechanism that makes patronage possible for someone whose wealth is illiquid. If the manuscript reads as Gospel Patrons with more chapters, it fails. State the difference in the introduction.

Permissions. Barnhart material through NCF and Generous Giving. Green material through Zondervan and Bill High. Signatry families internally. Any Rick Warren content restricted to his published writing and public talks, with permission sought before print — no reconstructed quotes, no characterization of private conversations.

Open decisions: 1. Whether the book is French's alone with Armstrong contributing, or a genuine two-voice book. Single narrator is almost always stronger. 2. Whether chapter 13 names French's own missed opportunity at his exit. It's the most credible page available and the most personally exposing. 3. Whether Rinehart writes the foreword. He's the natural bridge, it defuses the comparison, and it signals the book is additive rather than competitive. 4. The gap to close before drafting: most anchor stories are American business owners. Source at least three at ordinary income levels and two outside the United States, and place one no later than chapter 2.
