WEEK ONE

F: The Financial Statement

Day 1

They Don't Know What They Have

They don't know who owns it

"Be sure you know the condition of your flocks, give careful attention to your herds." Proverbs 27:23

A lot of times, families don't know what they have. They don't know what its value is. They don't know who owns it.

I have been saying that sentence for close to thirty years and it still surprises people, because the families I am describing are not disorganized. They are successful. They have accounts and advisors and reports arriving on a schedule. Some of them have a business worth more than everything else on the page combined. What they do not have is one page that says what they own, what they owe, and whose name it sits in.

The third one is the one nobody expects. Ownership makes a big difference. It matters whether an asset is owned by the husband, owned by the wife, owned jointly, held in a trust, or held inside an entity, because the way an asset moves depends entirely on who owns it. A family can be completely certain about what they intend and completely wrong about what will happen, and the reason is that intention and titling are two different things. Only one of them is legally operative, and it is not the one they have been thinking about.

So what I am after in a financial statement is not complicated. What do they have, and how much is it worth, and who owns it. That is the whole first pass.

Here is the distinction I want you to hold onto, because it is the one that separates this work from account review. Information is not understanding. A family can have a great deal of information and no understanding at all. They receive statements. The statements are accurate. Nobody has done anything wrong. And if you asked the wife to explain the family's position, she could not do it, and if you asked the husband to explain how the business would move if he died on Thursday, he could not do that either.

You have to have a lot of information, and you need to understand your situation. Those are two separate jobs. Most of the people around a family's table have done the first one. Almost nobody has done the second.

And if you do not understand what you have been entrusted with, it is difficult to steward it the way you want to steward it.

I want to give you one picture of what this looks like when it finally gets assembled, because it is not usually the picture people expect.

A lot of times it is the wife who does not have a clue. She knows he goes to work every day. She knows he has a business. She has no idea what it is worth. Then we put it all on the table, and she looks at it, and she says, my goodness, I cannot believe we have all this stuff. Then she asks where she can go shopping.

I tell that lightly and I do not mean it lightly. That woman has been living inside a financial life she could not see the shape of. That is not a spreadsheet problem. That is a marriage carrying an imbalance nobody named. When the picture goes on the table, one person stops holding all the cards, and the family can start engaging together, on the investments, on the future, on their giving, on what they want to do for their children. That is where alignment actually starts.

Solomon is writing to men whose wealth walked around on four legs and could be counted in an afternoon, and even then he does not say be sure you own flocks. He says know their condition. Give careful attention. The instruction is not about accumulation, it is about attention, and it assumes that a man can own a great deal and pay attention to none of it.

Your clients own things that do not walk into a pen at night. They own interests inside entities they have not looked at in nine years. Knowing the condition of that takes deliberate work, and deliberate work is exactly what nobody has been paid to do.

You are going to be the one who does it.

Not because it is sophisticated. It is the least sophisticated thing in this entire module. You are going to ask a family for a list of what they own and what they owe, and you are going to put a column next to it that says who owns each line, and most of the value you deliver in the first ninety days will come out of that one column.

Start with what they have. You cannot align anything to a picture nobody has seen.

Reflect

  1. Pick your largest client. Without opening a file, can you name every significant asset they own? What is the first thing you had to guess at?

  2. Of the assets you just listed, how many could you title correctly right now as husband, wife, joint, trust, or entity?

  3. Where in your book is there a family with excellent reporting and no shared understanding? What has that cost them so far?

  4. When was the last time a client told you something about their own balance sheet that surprised you? What does it mean that it surprised you?
