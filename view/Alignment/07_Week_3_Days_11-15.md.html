WEEK THREE

M: Mandatory Documents

Day 11

It Will Not Happen

If it's not written down and codified

"Write down the revelation and make it plain on tablets, so that a herald may run with it." Habakkuk 2:2

[Key verse recommendation. No scripture appears in the Session 3 video and none in the companion books attaches to Mandatory Documents. This one matches the teaching and needs Tom's approval.]

People don't realize how important the documents are, because what you say you want to have happen, if it's not written down and codified, it will not happen.

I do not soften that and it does not need softening. The state of whatever state you are in has a document ready for you, and I will guarantee you are not going to like it.

That is not a scare tactic. It is a description of what the law does in the absence of instruction. Somebody is going to decide, and if it is not the person who owned the assets, it will be a statute written for the average case by people who never met this family.

I have a story about this that I do not enjoy telling.

My son is a lawyer. It took me three years of badgering him before he made his will. He had three kids at the time.

A lawyer. Three children. Three years of his father, who does this for a living, telling him to sit down and do the thing that takes an afternoon.

I tell that on my own family because it kills the explanation everybody reaches for first, which is that people do not do this because they do not understand it. My son understood it completely. He had the technical knowledge, he had the access, he had the motivation sitting in three car seats. He still did not do it for three years.

So it is not ignorance. It is something else, and I think the something else is that this task has no deadline attached to it. Every other important thing in a professional's life arrives with a date. This one arrives with a feeling that you should get to it, and a feeling loses to a calendar every single time.

Which means your job with a client is not primarily to persuade. It is to supply the date.

Now, what actually gets written down.

From an estate planning standpoint you have wills, and you might have trusts. You need a power of attorney, somebody who is going to make your decisions if you are not able to make them. You need healthcare directives, because if you are in the hospital somebody has to decide, and that needs to be settled in advance rather than in a corridor.

Those are the basic estate documents. Then people may have irrevocable life insurance trusts. And if you have a business there is another whole school of documents, shareholder agreements and operating agreements and buy-sell agreements.

The reason to look at all of them is so that we understand where you are right now today. And then what we are ultimately going to get to is what you need to do to get your documents to align with your clarity.

That is the sequence and it does not reverse. Clarity first. Then make sure the documents are aligned. Then communicate it to the next generation.

Habakkuk is told to write it down and make it plain, and then given the reason, which is so that a herald may run with it. The writing is not for the man who received the vision. He already knows it. The writing is so that somebody else can carry it at speed, later, without him.

That is exactly what an estate document is for. It is not for the person who signed it. It is so that people who are grieving and hurried and not thinking clearly can pick it up and run.

Make it plain enough that they can.

  3. Where could you supply a date rather than an argument this week?

  4. What would be undecided in your largest client's family if he were in a hospital on Friday, unable to speak?

Day 12

That Might Be 50% Correct

The three myths of estate planning

"Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring." Proverbs 27:1

I tell people there are three myths of estate planning.

The first myth is, I am never going to die.

Nobody says that out loud. Everybody plans as though it were true, which is the same thing with better manners. A man who genuinely believed he had a limited number of years would not leave a document untouched for a decade.

The second myth is, I have got the highest priced attorneys in town, so certainly my estate is in good shape.

I say, that might be 50% correct. You might have the highest priced attorneys in town.

That is the only joke in this session and it is not really a joke. Price is evidence of price. It is not evidence of currency, and it is not evidence that anybody has looked at the document since the day it was signed, and it is certainly not evidence that the document describes a family that still exists.

Here is what that looks like in practice, and I have seen it more than once.

If it is an old will, you named your uncle to be your executor. Well, he died three years ago. And you did not name anybody else.

That is not a defect in the drafting. On the day it was written, the uncle was an excellent choice and the attorney did good work. The document did not fail. It simply kept saying the same thing while the world around it moved, and nobody went back.

So we just need to make sure they are up to date, and that the people who are going to do these responsibilities for your estate are aware they are going to get to do it, and that they are willing to do it for you. It takes a lot of work to be the executor of an estate.

Read that again. Aware. Willing. A name written into a document by somebody who never asked the person is not a plan. It is a hope with a signature on it.

The third myth is, it is my kids' money.

That one gets its own day tomorrow, because it is not a technical error like the other two. It is a different account of who owns anything.

What I want you to take from the first two is the sequence I actually use, which is short.

You start by reviewing what exists. What documents are in place, and when were they created. Then you ask, do they still reflect the current situation. Then you ask the question that does the real work: if something were to happen, how would this play out, would it be clear, and would it be aligned with what you said you wanted?

That third question moves the conversation off the past and onto the future, and clients engage with it completely differently. Reviewing the past sounds like auditing the attorney. Testing the future sounds like protecting the family.

Solomon is not telling anyone to be morbid. He is telling them not to boast, and the boast in question is a quiet one. It is the assumption of available time.

A document written ten years ago is a bet that nothing important would change. Most of your clients have already lost that bet and have not gone back to check the ticket.

Go check one this week.

  4. When you review documents, are you auditing the past or testing the future? Which does the client hear?

Day 13

God Didn't Give It to Your Kids

Make sure they're prepared

"Yours, Lord, is the greatness and the power and the glory and the majesty and the splendor, for everything in heaven and earth is yours. Wealth and honor come from you; you are the ruler of all things." 1 Chronicles 29:11-12

The third myth of estate planning is that it is my kids' money.

I say, God didn't give it to your kids. He gave it to you.

That sentence stops rooms, and I have never once had to explain it. Everybody understands it immediately, including people who do not share my faith, because it is not really a theological argument at the moment it lands. It is a question about who is responsible.

If it is the children's money, then the parent is a temporary custodian of an inheritance already spoken for, and his job is to protect it and hand it over with as little friction as possible. Nothing about that job requires him to think about who his children are.

If it was given to him, then he is answerable for what happens to it, including what it does to the people who receive it. That is a much larger job and it cannot be completed by a lawyer.

And so if you are going to pass it on to your kids, you need to make sure they are prepared to be able to handle it.

Notice that the conclusion is not that they should receive less. Sometimes it is, and I have clients who reduced what they were leaving after they saw the whole picture, and they were right to. But the instruction here is not about the amount. It is about preparation, and preparation is work somebody has to do in advance, with actual people, over years.

Here is the part I want you to see clearly, because it is where advisors get this wrong.

Preparation is not a distribution schedule. Age thirty, age thirty-five, age forty is not preparation. It is a delay mechanism, and a delay mechanism assumes that a person who is not ready at thirty will have become ready at forty through the passage of time. Time does not do that. People do that, or nobody does.

So when a parent tells me the trust handles it, I ask what the trust is handling. It is handling the timing. It is not handling the child.

Now, the reason this belongs in a session about mandatory documents rather than in some softer part of the curriculum.

A document is where a belief about ownership becomes operative. The man who believes it is his kids' money writes one kind of instrument. The man who believes he was entrusted with it writes another, and the second one usually has instructions in it, and language about why, and provisions that reflect knowledge of specific people rather than a formula.

You can frequently tell which belief a client holds by reading his documents, without him ever having said a word about it.

David is at the end of his life, handing over an enormous project to a son, and he does not say any of this belongs to Solomon. He says everything in heaven and earth belongs to God, and then he says wealth and honor come from you. He is standing in front of the largest transfer of his life and locating the ownership somewhere else entirely.

That is not humility for show. It is the thing that makes the transfer survivable, because a man who knows he did not ultimately own it is capable of asking whether the person receiving it is ready.

Ask a client this week whose money he thinks it is.

Then listen to how fast he answers.

Reflect

  1. Which of your clients treats the estate as his children's money already? How does that show up in his decisions?

  2. What does his belief about ownership cause him to skip?

  3. Where have you accepted a distribution schedule as though it were preparation?

  4. If you read your top ten clients' documents cold, could you tell which belief each one holds?

Day 14

Who's Running the Business When You're Not There?

Somebody has to be in charge

"Go to the ant, you sluggard; consider its ways and be wise! It has no commander, no overseer or ruler, yet it stores its provisions in summer." Proverbs 6:6-8

People talk about needing a succession plan if you have a business. Well, you need a continuity plan.

People say, what does that mean.

Let us say you get in a car wreck and you are in the hospital for thirty days getting repaired. Who is running the business when you are not there? If you do not have a plan for that, who knows. Everybody is running around. Nobody knows who is in charge.

Somebody has to be in charge.

I had a client who had not figured this out for himself, and then he got in a car accident and was in the hospital for a month, and there was not anybody in charge.

The company did not stop needing decisions while he was recovering. A new contract was coming in. The leadership team he had built, capable people who knew the business, spent that month circling. The vice president finally asked out loud who had the authority to make a particular decision, and it was not really clear. So they waited, and they guessed, and things went undone, because nobody wanted to be the person who signed something the owner would have handled differently.

His wife had authority on paper. She did not know what was going on, which meant the authority never converted into a decision.

He recovered. The month cost him anyway.

Here is the distinction, stated as plainly as I can make it, because these two words get used interchangeably by people who should know better.

Succession is what eventually happens to the business. Continuity is who has authority during the thirty days you are unavailable.

A lot of people have an idea on the succession part but not the continuity part. And succession is the one everybody plans for, because it is the interesting problem. It involves valuation and structure and the future, and it can be worked on pleasantly over several years.

Continuity is the boring problem. It is a list of names and a document that says who signs what. Nobody enjoys building it and nobody feels sophisticated afterward.

It is also the one that actually happens. Most business owners will be temporarily unavailable at some point, from an accident or an illness or a surgery, and comparatively few of them will die suddenly at the peak of an unresolved succession question.

The likely event is the unplanned one.

And when there is a family business it is usually the biggest asset in a family's net worth, so if they do not know what they are going to do with it, it can lose tremendous value very quickly, and it cannot provide for the family going forward. There are a lot of employees who are dependent on this business carrying on.

Solomon points at an insect with no commander, no overseer, no ruler, and says look at what it manages to do anyway. The ant stores in summer for a season it cannot see and has nobody instructing it.

Your clients have the opposite problem. They have commanders and overseers and a leadership team, and no provision at all for a season they can see coming.

Ask one of them this week what happens during the thirty days.

Most of them have never once been asked.

Day 15

They Want Cash

The government doesn't want your business

"The wise store up choice food and olive oil, but fools gulp theirs down." Proverbs 21:20

If you own a business, there is a lot of value in the business. The government wants a part of that value when you are gone.

So it is not just about whether the taxes are due. It is about how you are going to get paid. A lot of times people with businesses have a lot of value, but they have very little cash.

And the government doesn't want your business, they want cash.

That is the whole problem in one sentence and it is worth memorizing, because it explains an outcome that otherwise looks like bad luck.

If it has not been planned for, it can force decisions that the family would not otherwise make, including selling the business. And if you are in a fire sale situation, the value you are going to get is nowhere near what it truly was.

Let me follow that through, because families do not picture the sequence. They picture a tax bill, which sounds unpleasant and survivable.

Here is what actually happens. The bill arrives with a due date. The family holds one asset large enough to produce that kind of cash, and it is the business. So the business goes on the market with a deadline attached, and the deadline is visible to every buyer who walks in the door.

The family then loses three things rather than one. They lose the business. They receive materially less than it was worth, because everyone knew they had to sell. And they lose the income the business was producing for them, which was funding their life.

And it does not stop at the family. There are employees dependent on that company continuing, and they had no part in any of this and no warning.

Now, the method, because it is not what most people reach for.

I start by looking at what the potential estate tax liability actually is. Then I ask how they are going to pay for it. There are a lot of ways to pay for it. One of them is insurance.

Sizing first, then solving. That order protects you and it protects the client, because the moment you lead with a solution you have become a person selling something, and there are plenty of those in this business already.

It also gives the client a number instead of a worry, and a number is actionable. A worry just sits there and grows.

Solomon contrasts storing up with gulping down, and the fool in that proverb is not malicious. He is simply consuming what is in front of him without provision for what is coming. He has no plan to be hungry later.

The business owner facing an unfunded estate tax bill has done the same thing in a more sophisticated costume. He built enormous value and made no provision for the one certain claim against it.

Size the liability for one client this week.

Bring him a number, not a concern.

Reflect

  2. Have you ever calculated the potential estate tax liability for your largest business owner, or have you referred to it generally?

  3. Where have you led with a solution before you had a number?

  4. Who else depends on that business continuing, and does the owner have any idea how many?
