Day 26

We Are Buying Her a Lexus

Mom doesn't need a Lexus

Watch the Day 26 video before you read.

"Whoever loves money never has enough; whoever loves wealth is never satisfied with their income." Ecclesiastes 5:10

Is it budget? Is it cash flow that they really need to deal with?

It really depends upon how much you have. Because some of my clients that are worth millions of dollars, they never had a budget. Never thought about having a budget. They don't need a budget, because they've got plenty of money to spend.

But for the average person, sometimes they're called mass affluent people, budget is important. Not so much that we know where every dollar is going. But we know, do we spend a hundred thousand a year? Do we spend eighty thousand? Do we spend four hundred thousand?

It's a big deal, because they need to have enough resources saved so that income off of those resources is enough for them to be able to continue their lifestyle.

Notice what he is not asking for. Not a budget in the sense of tracking every dollar. A number. One number, roughly right, describing what this household actually consumes in a year.

Without that number, nobody can say whether the resources set aside will sustain the life this family expects to keep living. Every retirement projection, every distribution plan, every conversation about how much is enough depends on it, and a great many advisors have never asked for it because it feels intrusive.

A lot of people don't realize, too, when they get older, there's a lot of medical expenses that show up, and they're not planning for it. You just go see a whole lot more doctors, because you've got doctors for this and doctors for that.

Now the story, and it is not really about cars.

One of my attorney friends said to me, my client passed away, his wife was left, and so she needed a new car. And he was her executor. So he said, I went out to buy her a car, and she was used to driving a Lexus, so I went to buy her another Lexus.

And her son came to me and said, mom doesn't need a Lexus. She can get along with a Ford.

My friend said, your mother is used to driving a Lexus. We are buying her a Lexus.

Because this kid is thinking, hey, whatever's left over when mom is gone is mine.

[Attribution note. Tom is relaying an exchange told to him by an attorney. Marked as Tom quoting someone else.]

That son was not being frugal on his mother's behalf. He was doing arithmetic. Every dollar spent on her was a dollar that would not reach him, and the first place he chose to economize was his widowed mother's daily life.

He had already begun spending an inheritance that had not arrived.

Nothing in that estate plan was defective. The documents worked. The money went where it was supposed to go. What the plan never accounted for was a son who would look at his mother and see a diminishing balance, and nobody found that out in time because nobody had ever sat down with him and asked him anything.

This is the week where the technical material meets actual people, and the Lexus is the hinge. For five weeks you have been assembling structures. Here you find out what kind of person is standing at the other end of them.

Solomon says whoever loves money is never satisfied with their income, and the striking thing about that son is that he had no income problem. He had a mother with a car.

Dissatisfaction of that kind does not arrive with a large sum. It is already present, and the sum reveals it.

Find out what one family actually spends. Then find out who is watching them spend it.

Day 27

There's No Such Thing as an Independent Financial Decision

What could you have done with it had you not spent it

Watch the Day 27 video before you read.

"The ground of a certain rich man yielded an abundant harvest. He thought to himself, what shall I do? I will tear down my barns and build bigger ones. But God said to him, you fool. This is how it will be with whoever stores up things for themselves but is not rich toward God." Luke 12:16-21

Why would a family that has resources need to be sensitive to the topic of cash flow?

One of the things that we say when we talk to people about cash flow is there's no such thing as an independent financial decision.

So when you decide to go and buy a Starbucks for five bucks, you could have done something else. You could have given that five bucks to a poor person.

So it's not a matter of, well, can I afford it. It's a matter of what could you have done with it had you not spent it doing that.

I use five dollars because the number is too small to argue about. Nobody is going to mount a defense of a coffee. Once the principle is granted at five dollars it is granted at five hundred thousand, and five hundred thousand is where the conversation actually needs to go.

Notice also the substitution being made. Can I afford it is a question about capacity and the answer is almost always yes for the families you serve, which is why the question is useless to them. What could I have done with it is a question about alternatives, and it has a different answer every time.

That reframe is the entire teaching, and it can be delivered as an accusation very easily. Do not ask a client to justify a purchase. Ask what else it could have done. One puts him on trial. The other hands him options he was not holding.

Now the parable, which people consistently mishear.

When you look at the parable in the Bible about the guy that was building bigger barns, there's one thing it says about that guy. It says that man was going to store it up for himself and was not rich toward God. That's why he got nailed, because he wasn't rich toward God.

So there's no problem with creating bigger barns if you've got a reason for why you're creating those bigger barns. If you're giving that extra money to the work of God, go make as much money as you can.

Read the parable again and you will find no condemnation of the harvest, the barns, the building, or the wealth. The man is not criticized for having a good year. The indictment sits entirely in two words, for himself, and in the phrase not rich toward God.

That matters enormously for the people you serve, because most of the successful Christian business owners I know carry a quiet suspicion that there is something faintly disreputable about their own accumulation, and nobody has ever told them where the actual line is.

The line is not the size of the barn. It is whether there is a reason for it.

A man building a larger barn in order to give more is doing something entirely different from a man building a larger barn because that is what one does with a good harvest. From the outside the two barns are identical.

The rich fool's failure is also not greed in the way we usually picture greed. He asks himself what shall I do, answers himself, and never asks anybody else. That is the whole tragedy in four verses. There was nobody in the room.

Being in that room is your job.

Ask a client this week what a decision cost him in the other categories. He has almost certainly never been asked.

Day 28

Cash Flow Is Really Where Values Become Visible

The difference between your values and your valuables

Watch the Day 28 video before you read.

"For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." Matthew 6:21

[Scripture recommendation. Tom teaches this verse elsewhere in the module but does not quote it in this clip. Needs his approval as the key verse here.]

You're helping them see how their money is actually being used over time, not just what they have, but how it flows. Where it goes, what it supports, and whether it aligns with what they say matters most.

This has a lot to do with actual inheritance to your children. Because if you're going to leave your kid a million dollars, if he can get five percent off of that, that's fifty thousand dollars a year you're adding to his income. If you're leaving him much more than that, then it's a lot more.

That conversion is worth doing out loud with every parent you serve, because it changes the question.

Parents think about an inheritance as a lump sum, a single event, a number on a page. The child experiences it as an income, arriving every year, on top of whatever he already earns, for the rest of his life. Those are different things and they have different effects on a person.

Run the conversion and the conversation stops being how much should we leave and becomes what will this do to the way this particular person works, decides, and gets up in the morning.

Because cash flow is really where values become visible.

Because it's one thing to say, I want to be a generous person. But if you're spending all your money, you're not going to be able to be generous.

It's like somebody said, you talk about the difference between your values and your valuables. So on one hand, you look at your valuables, all that God has given in terms of things and cash flow and such, but you've got these values over here that are kind of saying what you want to do, and they're contrary to this.

That's where it kind of gets more down to earth, or it starts to come to life.

Because up until this point, a lot of what we've talked about can stay at a higher level. Structure, planning, long-term decisions. But this is daily life. This is day to day. These are habits. This is patterns.

And there's no financial expenditure decision that doesn't also have ramifications or play against a set of values that matter to you. And that's actually what's happening when no one is really thinking about it.

That last clause is the one to sit with. When no one is really thinking about it.

The gap between a family's values and their valuables does not open through a decision. Nobody sits down and elects to spend against their own stated priorities. It opens through a thousand unexamined transactions, each individually defensible, none of them measured against anything.

Which is why the remedy is measurement rather than conviction. These families are not short on conviction. They are short on any mechanism that shows them what their conviction actually produced last year.

And one of the simplest but most important questions underneath all of this is, are you living within your means? Because if that's not clear, everything else starts to feel unstable.

Jesus does not say a good heart will eventually produce generosity. He says the treasure goes first and the heart follows it. That is the opposite of how most people read the verse, and the difference matters enormously, because the common reading lets a man wait indefinitely for a feeling to arrive.

Your spending pattern is not a report on your heart. It is a cause of it.

Put a year of one family's money into categories and show them the shape of it. You will not have to say anything else.

  3. What in this work has stayed at a higher level and never reached daily life?

  4. If treasure leads and the heart follows, what has your own spending been leading your heart toward?

Day 29

Are They Living Within Their Means?

The first question I usually ask the next generation

Watch the Day 29 video before you read.

"Dishonest money dwindles away, but whoever gathers money little by little makes it grow." Proverbs 13:11

The first question I usually ask the next generation. I ask them if they're living within their means.

They're not getting any money necessarily from mom or dad right now. So do they have a budget? Are they living within their means?

And in many cases, if they are living within their means, then another question I ask them is, how is your giving? Are you giving to God's work right now?

Two questions. I have asked them hundreds of times and I have never needed a third.

The reason I ask before any money has moved is that the answer is real at that stage. A young man running his own household on his own income is producing evidence. The same man ten years into receiving distributions is producing something else, and it is much harder to read.

And because, in the cash flow area, we talk about there's only five things you can do with your money. You can use it for your lifestyle, you can give it away, you can pay taxes with it, you can pay off debts if you have debts, which would include a mortgage. And then lastly, you can save it for the future.

So there's only five things you can do with your money.

That is a closed set, and the closure is what gives it force. Every dollar lands in exactly one of those five. There is no sixth category and no dollar sitting outside the system, which means a dollar added to one is a dollar removed from another, always, whether or not anybody notices.

And so I want to know how these kids are doing with what they have right now.

Because that also will help mom and dad to know that if they're deeply in debt, because they're spending more than they're bringing in every year, that's going to let mom and dad know that, well, I just need to be careful in terms of how I'm going to be able to help these people.

Careful is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and it does not mean give less. It means the form of the help has to change. Money handed to a household that already runs a deficit does not fix the deficit. It funds it for a while, and then the deficit resumes at a higher level.

Especially, you have trusts going on. They're working in the business, maybe getting a little bit, but the same systems and strategies they use with the limited resource they have probably extend to the other.

So having them learn proper financial management and basic principles of budgeting. They may not have built the family's business, but they are receiving the benefit in a form that can be dangerous in its full expression.

That last phrase is worth keeping. Dangerous in its full expression. Not dangerous. Dangerous at full strength, to a person who has only ever handled it diluted.

Solomon contrasts money that dwindles with money gathered little by little, and the second describes a process rather than an event. Little by little is not a strategy. It is a character, formed over time in ordinary amounts, and it either exists in a person before the large sum arrives or it does not.

Which is exactly why I ask a thirty-two-year-old about his own budget. I am not auditing him. I am looking for whether the gathering is happening at the scale he currently operates at, because scale does not change what a person is. It only makes it more visible.

Go meet somebody's children. You cannot prepare a person you have never met.

  4. Where would money handed to a household simply fund a deficit rather than fix it?

Day 30

What Did You Do With What I Gave to You?

God is not going to ask you

Watch the Day 30 video before you read.

"After a long time the master of those servants returned and settled accounts with them. Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things." Matthew 25:19-21

You need to understand that you're responsible for the stewardship of your assets.

God is not going to ask you, what did your clients do with their money. He's going to ask you, what did you do with what I gave to you.

And so when you get that perspective, then you're not judgmental on anyone.

That sentence does two jobs and the second one is larger than the first.

The first is that it frees you from having opinions about how other people spend. You will sit across from a man who spends more in a year than you will earn in five, and there is no arithmetic available that makes that comfortable. What makes it possible is the recognition that his accounting is not yours to keep.

The second is that it puts a bill in your own name.

One thing Ron Blue says is that you love your children equally, therefore you treat them uniquely. Which is very biblical, because if you look at the parable of the talents, he gave five to one, two to another, one to the third.

God doesn't give equally to people.

When you look at the guy that went and hired people to work in his field. One guy, paid him a denarius for the day. The other guy came at five o'clock, he paid him a denarius. And the guy complained. And he said, well, it's my money. I do with it whatever I jolly well please.

That's the New England way to say it.

Because quite honestly, if you really believe that God owns everything, then what you have is what God has allowed you to have. It's either a lot or it's not a lot. But God is the one who makes that kind of decision on your behalf.

That is what ends comparison, and comparison is the occupational disease of this profession. You will spend a career standing next to balance sheets larger than yours. The only thing that makes that survivable is the recognition that the distribution was not an accident and was not yours to arrange.

Now, the accounting itself, because the parable is specific about how it works.

The master returns and settles accounts. He does not compare the servants to each other. He does not ask the two-talent servant why he was not a five-talent servant. Each man is examined against what he was actually given, and the man with five and the man with two receive word for word the same commendation.

The only one who fails is the one who did nothing, and his defense is the instructive part. He was not reckless and he did not steal. He preserved the asset perfectly and returned it intact, and that turns out to be the failure rather than the mitigation.

There is a version of that in this business that I see constantly. An advisor who does competent, careful, unremarkable work for thirty years, harms nobody, and never once asks a family the question that would have changed something. Nothing lost. Nothing risked. The talent went back in the ground.

So cash flow, it's really important, because it helps to understand where people are going to be. And you just want to know if they're living within their means. And that's not always obvious.

Ron Blue also told me one time, he said, if someone looks like they can give you a million dollars, they probably can't.

Thirty days ago you started with a page listing what a family owns and owes. You end here, with a question about what you did with what you were given.

You will not be asked about the size of your book. You will not be asked how you compared to the man down the hall. You will be asked what you did with what you had, and the answer will consist mostly of specific conversations you either had or did not have with specific families.

Several of those conversations are available to you this week.

  3. Where have you preserved something rather than used it?

  4. Whom do you compare yourself to, and what would it take to stop?
