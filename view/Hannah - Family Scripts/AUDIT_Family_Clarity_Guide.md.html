Honest audit of the Clarity family guide from the reader's point of view

FAMILY CLARITY GUIDE — AUDIT

Would a family actually go through this, and would it help them.

Written against the guide as it now stands, after the case studies were replaced.

THE HEADLINE PROBLEM

185 questions across six sessions. About 31 per session.

An advisor can absorb that. Advisors consume professional development for a living, they read densely, and they are being paid to get better at this.

A family sitting in a living room on a Tuesday will answer eight or nine.

That is not a small gap. It means the guide is currently designed for a reader who does not exist, and the family will handle it the way people always handle an overloaded worksheet. They will do the easy questions and skip the hard ones.

Which is backwards, because the hard ones are the product.

The guide also never says which questions matter most. Reflect explicitly says "there are more here than you need, take the ones that catch," which is honest but is also the guide admitting it overshot. Nothing else in the session says that, so the reader is left assuming the other twenty-five are all required.

What I would do. Mark one question in each session as the one that matters. Not a summary, a marked question. If a family answers only that one, the session worked. Everything else becomes optional by contrast rather than by omission.

WHAT THE GUIDE ASSUMES

It assumes more than it should, and it assumes it silently.

Assumption How often Problem
You are married "your spouse" 37 times, "your marriage" 8 Widowed, divorced, single, or married to someone who will not participate
You have children "your children" 21 No children, estranged children, one child rather than several
You have assets to transfer throughout Session 4 A family with modest resources is told the session is for them once, in the last line
You share a faith "God" 13 times, six Scripture passages, Going Deeper in every session An adult child or in-law who does not believe is in the room and unaddressed
Your family is reachable Together and Family Conversation both assume someone will answer Estrangement is never named anywhere in six sessions

The blended family problem is the largest of these. Remarriage, stepchildren, children from two marriages, and an inheritance question that is genuinely harder than the one the guide describes. That family is common, they are the ones who most need this, and the guide currently reads as though they do not exist.

And "Before You Bring the Family In" is built on a marriage. For a widow, a divorced parent, or someone whose spouse will not engage, that section has nothing in it. It says "if there is no spouse, decide who the first person is," which is one sentence carrying a lot of weight.

WHAT A FAMILY ACTUALLY WANTS

Worth separating from what the curriculum wants for them.

What they want

To know their family will not come apart over this. That is the whole motivation and the guide never says it plainly until Session 4.

To have one specific conversation, and to have it go well. Most people picking this up have a particular conversation in mind. The guide never asks them to name it.

To find out they are not behind. This is bigger than it looks. Families arrive at this material assuming everyone else has done it. Telling them plainly that most families never do this is a relief, and the guide only says it twice.

To not be judged. Especially about money, and especially by their own children.

What they do not want

Homework. Anything that feels like being graded. To reveal numbers. To be told there is a right way to do this that they have missed. And to be handed something their spouse will find pious or their children will find preachy.

THE QUESTIONS THEY WANT TO ANSWER

This is where the guide is furthest off.

The guide asks curriculum questions. "Which of the five areas is clearest?" is a good question inside a framework and it is not a question anybody walks in with.

The questions families actually arrive with:

  • Will my kids be okay?
  • Did I do enough?
  • Is it too late for us?
  • What happens the day I am gone?
  • Why can we not talk about this?
  • How do I even bring it up?
  • Am I going to hurt somebody by being honest?

Four of those seven appear nowhere in the guide. "Will my kids be okay" is the single most common reason a family opens a book like this and it is never asked directly in six sessions.

What I would do. Open each session with the family's question, then move to the curriculum's. Session 1 currently opens with the five areas. It could open with what are you actually afraid of here, and reach the five areas as the answer.

WOULD IT HELP THEM

Honestly, yes, and less than it could.

What works

The framework is genuinely good, it is Tom's, and five areas is the right number. Where You Are Starting is now the strongest section in the guide because it is the only one that meets a family where they actually are. The Host Note solves a real problem in three lines. Together is one thing rather than a plan, which is right. And For the Next Generation is the most differentiated thing in the book and probably the reason a G2 reader would keep going.

What does not

The volume. The assumptions. The absence of the family's own questions.

And one more. The guide never tells a family what good looks like. They finish Session 6 with no way to know whether it worked. An advisor has a client outcome. A family has nothing to measure against, and people abandon things they cannot see progress in.

THE SEVEN CHANGES I WOULD MAKE

1. Cut the questions by half. Target fifteen per session, not thirty-one. Reflect drops from seven to four. Going Deeper drops from three passages to one. Learn drops from five prompts to three.

2. Mark the one question that matters in each session. If they answer only that, the session worked.

3. Open each session with the family's question, not the framework's. Reach the framework as the answer rather than the premise.

4. Write the four missing paths. No spouse. Estranged child. Blended family. Modest resources. Each one a short honest paragraph in Where You Are Starting rather than an apologetic aside.

5. Say plainly, early, that most families never do this. Once in the welcome and once in Session 1. It is true, it is the most reassuring sentence available, and it costs nothing.

6. Add a way to see progress. One page at the back. Six lines, one per session, each naming the one thing that changed. Filled in as they go.

7. Name the conversation. Session 1 should ask them to write down the one conversation they have been avoiding, and every session should come back to it. That single thread would do more than any framework in the book.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE OTHER FIVE

Every finding here applies across the series, and two get worse elsewhere.

Alignment is the most technical book and the most likely to lose a family entirely. The question load matters more there, not less.

Next Generation has the most assumptions baked in, because it assumes the next generation is reachable and willing.

The good news is that the fixes are structural rather than a rewrite. Cutting questions, adding paths, and threading one named conversation would change all six books without touching the frameworks, the tools, or the teaching.
