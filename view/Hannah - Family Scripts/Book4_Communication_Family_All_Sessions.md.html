THE BOUNDARY

This is the title most at risk of duplicating the rest of the series, so the line is drawn explicitly and it is stated on camera in Session 1.

Meetings is the event. Communication is everything else. The scheduled gathering, the agenda, who holds the chair, keeping the room on track, and building a rhythm of gatherings all belong to Book 5 and are not repeated here. This book is about the kitchen, the car, the phone call, and the ten minutes after everyone else has gone to bed.

Deliberately left to other books. Ground rules and facilitation mechanics, to Meetings Session 3. Trust as a framework, vain imaginations, Lencioni, and asking forgiveness, to Next Generation Session 3. Open-ended questions and not accepting the first answer, to Clarity Session 2.

What is genuinely this book's own. Understanding rather than agreement. Empathetic listening. The three keys of biblical communication. Triangulation. Conversations with and without money on the table. And communication as an expression of love rather than a transfer of information.

🎬 SESSION 1 — WHY FAMILIES DON'T TALK ABOUT WHAT MATTERS

The Conversations That Never Happen

Source: Coaching Communication script S1 and S2; Workbook Legend S5

BRETT: CENTER

Well, welcome to Family Legacy by Design.

My name is Brett, and I am here with Tom Conway, and we are grateful to be starting this one with you. Tom, thank you for your life and your leadership in this.

We are kicking off a brand new series on family communication. On the conversations that actually matter, and why so many of them never happen.

And let me say up front where this one sits, because if you have been through the series on family meetings, this is going to sound close and it is not the same thing.

A family meeting is an event. It is scheduled, it has a purpose, somebody leads it. That is a different book and it is worth having.

This is everything else. This is the kitchen. This is the car. This is the phone call. This is the ten minutes after everybody else has gone to bed. Because most of what actually gets communicated in a family never happens in a meeting.

BRETT: TURN

So Tom, here is the question underneath this whole series. If these conversations matter so much, why don't families have them?

TOM: CENTER

There are a few consistent barriers, and most of them are not technical. They are relational and emotional.

One of the biggest is discomfort. These conversations touch on money, on expectations, on roles, and sometimes on things further back than that. People do not know how to bring those up in a healthy way, so they avoid them.

And when people do not know how to start, they wait. They assume there will be a better time later.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, that reframes the whole thing.

It is not that families do not care. It is not that they are unwilling. It is that they do not know how to enter the conversation without creating tension, or saying the wrong thing, or opening something they are not sure how to handle.

So instead of stepping into it, they step around it. And over time, that becomes the pattern.

TOM: CENTER

Exactly. And another barrier is fear.

Fear of conflict. Fear of being misunderstood. Fear of saying something that creates tension. So people stay quiet to keep things comfortable.

BRETT: CENTER

And here is what is strange about that. The thing people are trying to preserve, which is comfort, is exactly what keeps the conversation from happening.

And it works for a while. It keeps things smooth on the surface. Underneath, things are building. Unspoken expectations. Different assumptions. Questions nobody feels safe asking.

TOM: CENTER

That is right. And a third barrier is assumption.

People think they already understand each other, so they do not feel the need to talk about it. And assumptions are rarely as accurate as people think.

[TOM: the family where the parents had a clear vision they had never communicated, and the children had their own assumptions, and nobody found out until a decision had to be made. Bring a real one.]

BRETT: CENTER

So everyone is operating with a version of the story, and nobody has checked whether the versions match.

And Tom, the fourth one is the one I think gets the most families.

TOM: CENTER

Timing. People think, we will talk about that later. Especially around anything to do with legacy or transition.

But waiting makes it harder, not easier.

BRETT: CENTER

Because later feels safer. It feels less urgent. It feels like there will be more clarity, more time, more readiness.

And what actually happens is the opposite. The stakes get higher. The decisions get bigger. The emotions get stronger. And now the conversation is harder than it would have been.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, so what is the real risk here? Because most families think the risk is conflict.

TOM: CENTER

The bigger risk is silence.

The issue is usually not disagreement. It is the absence of clarity. And silence does not stay neutral. People fill it.

Scripture. Ephesians 4:25. Speak truth, each one of you, with his neighbor. Written to a church. It applies at a kitchen table.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

SCENARIO ONE. A family where everything looks fine. Structure, planning, progress, no obvious conflict. And underneath there are things that have not been said, assumptions that have not been tested, and expectations nobody has clarified.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

That is most families, and it is worth saying plainly that this is not a broken family. That is a normal one.

But if nobody creates space for those conversations, they just sit there. And eventually they show up in a decision, or in conflict, or in confusion. And by then nobody remembers where it started.

BRETT: CENTER

Let me give you another version of this.

SCENARIO TWO. A family that did try once. Somebody raised something honest, it did not go well, and now there is a shared understanding that we do not talk about that.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

[TOM: a family whose first attempt went badly, and what it took to try again.]

And what I would say to them is that one bad conversation is evidence about that conversation. It is not evidence about your family.

You do not have to solve everything. You do not have to resolve every issue or align every perspective. You are just trying to take one step, from avoiding it to entering it.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, and there is a way to think about this that I think changes it entirely for a family.

TOM: CENTER

Communication is one of the clearest ways a family expresses care for one another.

Taking the time to talk, to listen, to understand. That is how trust gets built over time.

So when communication is missing, it is not just a gap in information. It is a gap in connection.

BRETT: CENTER

To the next generation reading this, one thing.

You may have decided that the silence in your family is your parents' to break, because they are the ones with the information.

Some of it is. But the conversation you are waiting for may be the same one they are waiting for, and one of you is going to have to go first. There is no rule that says it has to be them.

BRETT: CENTER

THIS WEEK. Name one conversation your family has been putting off.

Do not have it yet. Just name it, out loud, to one person. That alone is further than most families get.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, bring us home.

TOM: CENTER

You do not have to remove every barrier at once. You just have to begin moving through them.

Ask a simple question. Create space. And then listen. That is where it starts.

BRETT: CENTER

Next session, the single idea that changes how these conversations go.

We will see you there.

🎬 SESSION 2 — UNDERSTANDING, NOT AGREEMENT

The Goal Is Not to Win

Source: Workbook Legend S5; Coaching Communication script S3

BRETT: CENTER

Welcome back to Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we are grateful you are back.

Last session we looked at why families avoid the conversations that matter most. Discomfort, fear, assumption, and timing. And we said the bigger risk is not conflict. It is silence.

So this session is about the one idea that changes how these conversations actually go. And it is simple enough to say in four words.

Understanding, not agreement.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, unpack that, because I think most families have never separated those two things.

TOM: CENTER

Agreement is transactional. It is a yes or a no. It closes something.

Understanding is relational. And here is the part people miss. Understanding is what actually leads to planning and problem solving.

You cannot solve something with somebody you do not understand. You can force a decision. You cannot solve anything.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, most families think the goal is to get everybody on the same page right away.

TOM: CENTER

That comes later. First, people need to feel heard.

When people feel understood, they become more open. And once there is openness, then you can begin to move toward alignment over time.

But if you start by trying to get agreement, what you usually get is compliance from the people who avoid conflict and resistance from the people who do not. And neither one of those is alignment.

BRETT: CENTER

So Tom, how do you get to understanding?

TOM: CENTER

The way to understanding is empathetic listening.

Not just what did they say. Why did they say it.

Because when somebody makes a statement, there is a reason underneath it, and the reason is almost always more useful than the statement. You have to go after that.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, here is what I have watched happen. Someone says something, and before they have even finished, the other person has already decided what it meant and started forming a response.

So they are not responding to what was said. They are responding to their version of it.

TOM: CENTER

That is right. And there is a well-known idea about this that shows up in families constantly.

The biggest problem in communication is the assumption that it has already taken place.

And a second one right beside it. Most people do not listen to understand. They listen to respond.

BRETT: CENTER

Both of those are in every family I know, including mine.

Because the conversation breaks down not because people do not care. It breaks down because they are not actually hearing each other. They are reacting. They are interpreting. They are getting ready to talk.

Scripture. Proverbs 18:13. He who gives an answer before he hears, it is folly and shame to him. Folly and shame is strong language for something most of us do several times a day.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

SCENARIO ONE. A husband and wife who have had the same disagreement, in roughly the same words, for eleven years. Both of them could recite the other one's position. Neither one could explain why the other one holds it.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

That is a family that has achieved perfect agreement about what they disagree on, and zero understanding.

And the way out is not another round of the same conversation. It is one person asking a question they have never asked. Not what do you think. Why does this matter to you.

Because eleven years in, they have never gotten to the why. They have just gotten faster at the what.

BRETT: CENTER

Let me give you another version of this.

SCENARIO TWO. A family where a decision has to be made, and the parents genuinely want the children's input. So they present the situation and ask what everybody thinks. And what they get back is agreement from all of them, immediately and unanimously.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

Then they did not get input. They got permission.

[TOM: a family where the quick yes turned out to be covering something else entirely.]

And I would tell those parents, unanimous and immediate is not a good sign. It usually means people are being agreeable, not honest.

So you slow it down. You go around the room and you ask each person to say what would concern them about this. Not whether they agree. What concerns them. That question is much harder to answer politely.

BRETT: CENTER

To the next generation reading this, here is where you can move first.

The next time you disagree with someone in your family, ask one question before you respond. Just one.

Not a rhetorical one. A real one, about why they see it the way they do.

You will notice two things. It is much harder than it sounds. And it changes the temperature of the room almost immediately.

BRETT: CENTER

THIS WEEK. In your next disagreement, ask one genuine question before you say anything about your own position.

Then see whether you still hold the position you walked in with.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, bring us home.

TOM: CENTER

The goal is not to get to the right answer. The goal is to create a place where people can say what they actually think.

Because you cannot align a family around something half of them never said out loud.

BRETT: CENTER

Next session, the skill underneath all of this. Listening.

We will see you there.

🎬 SESSION 3 — LEARNING TO LISTEN

What Happens When Someone Finally Feels Heard

Source: Coaching Communication script S3; Workbook Legend S5

BRETT: CENTER

Welcome back to Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett, I am with Tom, and we are glad you have joined us.

Last session we said the goal is understanding, not agreement, and that the way to understanding is empathetic listening.

So this session is about that. Actually listening. Because it is one of those things everybody assumes they already do well, and almost nobody does.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, what makes the difference between a conversation that opens somebody up and one that shuts them down?

TOM: CENTER

It usually comes down to whether people feel safe.

If people do not feel safe, they will not really engage. They may respond. They will not open up.

So the first goal is not to get to the right answer. It is to create the right environment. Because the words matter far less than whether the person believes it is safe to say something true.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, most people think a conversation starts with what you say.

TOM: CENTER

It starts before that. It starts with how it is framed.

If it feels like pressure, or evaluation, or expectation, people hold back. If it feels like an opportunity to share and be heard, they engage.

And that is why so many conversations go wrong. Not because the topic was wrong. Because the environment was.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, so what does it actually look like to start one well?

TOM: CENTER

You start with simple, open-ended questions. Not questions that lead somewhere specific. Questions that invite somebody to say what they think.

[TOM: the family where previous conversations had turned into silence or disagreement, and the one question you opened with instead, and what happened when the first person answered.]

And then you stop and listen. That is the whole move.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, people overcomplicate this. They think they need the perfect question.

TOM: CENTER

It is not about the question. It is about posture. Does the question create pressure, or does it create space?

And then there is the part almost nobody does. You give people silence.

You ask a question, and then you do not fill the space. Most of us are uncomfortable with silence. But that silence is usually where somebody is sorting out what they really think, or getting up the nerve to say something they have never said out loud.

If you move too quickly, you miss it. And you will never know you missed it.

BRETT: CENTER

That silence can feel very long in the moment.

TOM: CENTER

It can. And it is almost always shorter than it feels.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, what about the fact that people are just built differently?

TOM: CENTER

That is a big one, and it wrecks a lot of conversations.

Some people are quick to speak. Others need time to think. Some are direct. Others are reflective.

If you do not account for that, you unintentionally create pressure that shuts people down. You end up hearing from the same voices every time, and missing the others completely.

And the ones you are missing are not disengaged. They are just slower, and the room moved on.

Scripture. James 1:19. Everyone must be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger. In that order. The order is the whole instruction.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

SCENARIO ONE. A family dinner where one person talks and everybody else is polite. Not hostile. Polite. And it has been that way so long that nobody notices anymore, including the person doing the talking.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

The fix is not to tell that person to talk less. That usually goes badly and it does not work anyway.

The fix is to ask someone else a direct question and then wait. Not a question to the table. A question to a person, by name.

And then you have to actually hold the silence, which is the hard part, because the person who usually talks will fill it in about two seconds if you let them.

BRETT: CENTER

Let me give you another version of this.

SCENARIO TWO. A parent who genuinely wants to listen, and every time their adult child starts to say something hard, the parent starts explaining. Not defensively. Helpfully. And the child stops halfway through.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

[TOM: a parent who was listening in order to help, and what changed when they stopped.]

Because when you explain, even kindly, what you are communicating is that you already understand and they can stop now.

So you are listening. You are not interrupting. You are not reacting too quickly. And you are acknowledging what people say even when you do not agree with it.

That is what builds trust. Not agreeing. Acknowledging.

BRETT: CENTER

To the next generation reading this, this session has something in it for you that is easy to miss.

You may have decided your parents do not listen. And you may be right.

But a lot of what looks like not listening is a person who was never listened to either, doing the only thing that was ever modeled for them.

That does not make it your job to fix. It might change how you go into the next conversation.

BRETT: CENTER

THIS WEEK. Ask one person a real question, and then say nothing at all until they are completely finished.

Not nothing until you find a gap. Nothing until they are done.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, bring us home.

TOM: CENTER

You do not have to get everything right. You just have to create the environment where something real can be said.

Because when people feel heard, they become more open. And once there is openness, everything else becomes possible.

BRETT: CENTER

Next session, once you are listening well, how you say the hard thing.

We will see you there.

🎬 SESSION 4 — SAYING IT WELL

Direct, Truthful, and Respectful

Source: Workbook Legend S5 and S11

BRETT: CENTER

Welcome back to Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett and this is Tom, and we are honored you are here.

We have talked about why families avoid these conversations, about understanding rather than agreement, and about listening.

Now the other half. Once you have listened well, at some point you have to say something. And you have to say something true.

And there is a framework we use for this that comes straight out of Scripture. Three keys.

Be direct. Be truthful. Be respectful.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, start with direct, because I think that is the one families struggle with most.

TOM: CENTER

Being direct means saying the thing. Not circling it. Not sending it through somebody else. Not hoping they pick it up from your tone.

Jesus put it about as plainly as it can be put. Let your yes be yes and your no be no.

And a lot of families have gotten very sophisticated at communicating without ever being direct. Hints. Silences. Saying it to one person hoping it reaches another. And then everybody is exhausted and nothing has actually been said.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, direct does not mean harsh.

TOM: CENTER

No. That is the thing people confuse. Some people think being direct gives them permission to be blunt and unkind, and it does not. That is why there are three of these and not one.

BRETT: CENTER

So the second is truthful.

TOM: CENTER

Being truthful means what you say matches what is actually so. Including the parts that do not make you look good.

And this one is harder in families than anywhere else, because we have long histories and we have stories about each other, and it is very easy to say something that is technically accurate and still not true.

BRETT: CENTER

And the third is respectful.

TOM: CENTER

Respectful means the person is still fully a person while you are disagreeing with them. Their dignity is not up for discussion.

And here is the test. You can usually be direct and truthful and still leave somebody feeling like less of a person than when you started. If that happens, you got two out of three, and two out of three is a failure.

Scripture. Matthew 5:37. Let your statement be, 'Yes, yes' or 'No, no.' The whole framework hangs on that being possible in your family.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, now I want to spend real time on something that we flag over and over in this work, and that almost nobody names in their own family.

Triangulation.

TOM: CENTER

Watch out for triangulation.

Here is what it is. You have something with person A. Instead of going to person A, you go to person B and talk about person A.

And it feels reasonable in the moment. You are processing. You are getting perspective. You are checking whether you are crazy.

But what you have actually done is pull a third person into something that is not theirs, and made it harder to ever resolve with the person it belongs to.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, in families this is almost invisible, because it looks exactly like a close relationship.

The mother and the daughter who talk about the son. The two brothers who talk about the third. The spouse who hears about it every night and never gets to say anything.

TOM: CENTER

That is right. And over time it hardens.

Because now person B has an opinion about person A that was formed entirely from one side, and person A has no idea it exists, and no way to answer it.

And the family thinks it is close. It is not close. It is triangulated.

BRETT: CENTER

So what do you do about it?

TOM: CENTER

Two things, and neither one is complicated.

If you are the one carrying something, take it to the person it belongs to. Not perfectly. Just take it there.

And if you are the one being talked to, you can say the most useful sentence in this whole book. Have you talked to him about it?

That one question, asked kindly, ends more dysfunction in families than almost anything else you can do.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

SCENARIO ONE. A family where two people have not been able to be in a room together comfortably for three years. And neither one has ever said a direct sentence to the other about why. But both of them have said a great deal to everybody else.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

[TOM: a family where something had been going around the outside for years, and what happened when it finally went to the person.]

And what I would say is that the reason it has lasted three years is not that it is a hard problem. It is that it has never once been in the room with both of them.

Most of these things are much smaller than they have become. They got big by traveling.

BRETT: CENTER

Let me give you another version of this.

SCENARIO TWO. A parent who has something to say to one adult child, and says it in front of all of them, at a holiday, in a joke.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

That is not direct. That is public, which is a different thing.

Direct means to the person. If it belongs to one person, it goes to one person. And you do not use humor as the delivery vehicle for something you were not brave enough to say plainly, because the person on the receiving end has to smile and cannot answer.

If it was worth saying, it was worth saying to their face and alone.

BRETT: CENTER

To the next generation reading this, here is what I would ask of you.

You are probably in the middle of at least one triangle right now. Most people your age are, and often you did not put yourself there.

You do not have to fix your family's patterns. But you can decline to be the third point. Have you talked to her about it. That is the whole move. And you can say it with a lot of love.

BRETT: CENTER

THIS WEEK. Find one thing you have been saying about someone in your family rather than to them.

Say it to them. Direct, truthful, respectful. All three.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, bring us home.

TOM: CENTER

You are trying to hold all three at once. Direct, truthful, respectful.

Any two of them without the third does damage. Direct and truthful without respect is cruelty. Truthful and respectful without directness never actually gets said. And direct and respectful without truth is just pleasant and useless.

All three. That is the work.

BRETT: CENTER

Next session, the topics everybody is thinking about and nobody says.

We will see you there.

🎬 SESSION 5 — THE HARD CONVERSATIONS

Money, Expectations, and Fairness

Source: Coaching Communication script S4

BRETT: CENTER

Welcome back to Family Legacy by Design. My name is Brett, I am with Tom, and we are grateful you are back.

We have talked about the barriers, about understanding rather than agreement, about listening, and about how to say the thing well.

Now we go where it gets harder. Because at some point every family runs into topics that carry more weight. Money. Expectations. Fairness. Roles. Especially when none of it has ever been talked about before.

And this is where a lot of families hesitate, not because they do not care, but because they can feel the tension before they even start.

BRETT: TURN

So Tom, when a conversation moves from comfortable to difficult, what is actually happening underneath?

TOM: CENTER

It becomes personal.

When you are talking about money, you are not talking about numbers. You are talking about what it means. About responsibility, and fairness, and sometimes about identity.

And people see it very differently. One person sees security. Another sees opportunity. Someone else feels responsibility, or pressure.

So when those perspectives come into the same conversation, it can feel like conflict even when nobody intended conflict.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, that is where it escalates fast. Because people think they are talking about the same thing and they are not.

They are each reacting to what it means to them. And if that has never been surfaced, it feels like it came out of nowhere, when it has been sitting there the whole time.

TOM: CENTER

That is right. And when the differences come out, the instinct is to respond quickly or defend a position.

That usually makes it worse.

Scripture. Proverbs 15:1. A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger. How you respond in that moment matters as much as what you say.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, that is hard, because in real time people do not feel gentle. They feel reactive. They feel misunderstood.

So instead of slowing down, they speed up. And that is usually when it breaks.

TOM: CENTER

Exactly. So part of navigating this is slowing the conversation down.

You make sure people feel heard before you move forward. You shift the focus from being right to understanding each other.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, there is one practical move in this session that I think will help more families than anything else in the book.

TOM: CENTER

Separate the conversation.

There are times when it is helpful to talk without money on the table. Where you are just talking about values, perspectives, and what people hope for.

And then there are times when you bring the financial side into it.

If you combine those too early, it creates pressure that makes it very hard for people to engage honestly.

BRETT: CENTER

That is a big one. Because a lot of families jump straight into the financial conversation. Numbers, structures, who gets what. Without ever building the relational understanding around it.

So now you have got weight without context. And that is when people react.

TOM: CENTER

[TOM: the family where distribution had been decided but never communicated, each child had a different expectation, and what happened when it finally came up. Bring a real one, and keep the figures out.]

And what we did was slow it down. Instead of trying to resolve it, we focused on understanding each perspective. Each person got to explain how they were thinking and why.

At first there was defensiveness. But as people started listening instead of responding, something shifted. They did not agree right away. They began to understand each other.

BRETT: CENTER

And Tom, that is the important part. The win was not agreement.

The win was that the conversation did not break. It stayed intact long enough for understanding to start.

BRETT: TURN

Tom, what about expectations?

TOM: CENTER

A lot of the tension is not about the money at all. It is about what people expected would happen.

And those expectations are almost always unspoken. So when reality does not match the expectation, it feels personal, even when nothing was done to anybody.

BRETT: CENTER

Tom, let me give you a scenario.

SCENARIO ONE. Adult children who have each privately built a picture of how things will go. None of them has ever said it out loud. And every one of the pictures is different.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

They are all going to be disappointed, and none of them will know why, because they will each be measuring against something nobody else ever agreed to.

And the fix is not to correct their expectations. It is to get them said. You cannot address an expectation nobody has stated.

So you ask the question directly. What do you think is going to happen? Not what do you want. What do you actually expect.

That question is uncomfortable and it is the whole ballgame.

BRETT: CENTER

Let me give you another version of this.

SCENARIO TWO. A family where one adult child has carried much more of the load. More time, more responsibility, more of the hard years. And nobody has ever named it, and they have never asked for anything, and it is sitting there.

BRETT: TURN

TOM: CENTER

[TOM: the family where one child felt things were not fair and another felt they had carried more and should be recognized.]

And here is what I would say. That person is not asking to be paid. Most of the time they are asking to be seen.

Families jump straight to whether the distribution should change. And often the thing that actually needed to happen was somebody saying out loud, we know what you did, and we know what it cost you.

Sometimes that is the entire conversation. And it is a lot cheaper than the alternative.
