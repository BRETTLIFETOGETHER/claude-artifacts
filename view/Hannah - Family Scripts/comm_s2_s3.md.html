Day 6

Understanding, Not Agreement

Two different things, and only one is possible today

"He who gives an answer before he hears, it is folly and shame to him." PROVERBS 18:13

Here is the idea that changes how these conversations go, and it is four words long.

Understanding, not agreement.

Agreement is transactional. It is a yes or a no, and it closes something. Understanding is relational, and here is the part people miss. Understanding is what actually leads to planning and problem solving.

You cannot solve anything with somebody you do not understand. You can force a decision. You cannot solve.

Most families walk into a hard conversation aiming at agreement, which means somebody has to move. And because somebody has to move, everybody arrives braced.

Change the target and the whole room changes. If the goal is understanding, nobody has to concede anything today, which means nobody has to defend anything today, which means people can actually say what they think.

Now, families sometimes hear this as a soft option. Just understand each other and never decide anything. That is not it.

Understanding is not the alternative to a decision. It is the precondition for a good one. And in my experience the decision usually gets easier once understanding arrives, because a good deal of what looked like disagreement turns out to have been two people describing different problems.

Here is the practical version. When somebody says something you disagree with, you have two available moves. You can respond to the position, which is what almost everyone does. Or you can ask why they hold it.

The second move costs one question and it changes the temperature immediately.

The proverb calls answering before hearing folly and shame, which is stronger language than we would use. But it names something real.

Answering early feels like competence. It functions as deafness.

So the target for the next five days is not to win anything. It is to be able to describe somebody else's position well enough that they would recognize it.

That is a higher bar than agreement and a much more useful one.

Reflect

1. In your family's recurring disagreement, could you state the other side's position fairly?

2. When did you last aim at understanding rather than agreement?

3. What would change if nobody had to concede anything this month?

4. Where have you answered before hearing?

Day 7

Listening for Understanding

You're understanding why they say that

"Everyone must be quick to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger." JAMES 1:19

You have got to be able to listen, not to judge.

You are listening for understanding. You are understanding why they say that, or why they say this. It is almost what is behind what they are saying.

That last phrase is the one to hold. Behind what they are saying.

Because when somebody in your family says something, there are two things present. There is the statement, and there is the reason the statement exists. Most families respond only to the first, and the first is frequently not the point.

Somebody says they do not want to talk about the house. The statement is about the house. What is behind it may be that the house is where their mother died, or that a conversation about the house went badly in 2014, or that they assume the decision has already been made and they do not want to be told.

If you respond to the house, you have missed it entirely, and the person will not correct you.

So the skill is a specific one. When somebody says something, ask yourself what would have to be true for this to make sense from where they sit.

That question does two things at once. It slows you down, and it puts you in their position rather than in evaluation of it.

There is a version of this that is particularly common between spouses. A man describes what he wants for the family. His wife has an expression. If you ask her, what usually comes out is not disagreement. It is something adjacent that he has never heard, and once he hears it his own answer changes.

He did not know he thought that until she said something else.

James gives the order and the order is the instruction. Quick to hear. Slow to speak. Slow to anger.

Most of us have it exactly reversed and we would defend the reversal as being decisive.

One practical piece. Give people time to answer. Do not move to the next question. The pause after a real question is where the thinking happens, and most of us end it in two seconds because silence is uncomfortable.

Whatever was rising in that moment does not come back.

Day 8

What's Behind What They're Saying

The first answer is the practiced one

"A plan in the heart of a man is like deep water, but a man of understanding draws it out." PROVERBS 20:5

I want to spend a whole day on the mechanics of getting to what is behind something, because it is not mysterious and it is not a talent.

Here is what actually happens in a conversation.

You ask somebody a real question. They give you an answer. That answer is almost always the practiced one, the version they have said before, the one that is ready.

That answer is not dishonest. It is just prepared, and prepared answers are smoother and less useful than the real ones.

The real answer is underneath, and it arrives on the second or third pass, and only if somebody stays in the room long enough to ask again.

So how do you ask again without it feeling like an interrogation?

Three ways, and none of them are clever.

Tell me more about that. Four words. It is not a question, which is why it works. It does not demand anything specific, so the person continues wherever they were actually going.

Why is that important to you? This one goes down a layer rather than sideways. It asks for the reason rather than more detail.

And say nothing. The most effective of the three and the hardest. After somebody finishes, wait. Most people, given four or five seconds of silence, will add something, and the addition is usually the real thing.

I think of it like the old Columbo. He would ask a simple question, come back around, and ask the follow-up. The first question was never the point. It was setting up the second one.

Now a warning about doing this badly, because it can be done badly. If somebody senses that you are running a technique on them, it closes them down faster than not asking at all. The difference between drawing something out and extracting it is entirely in whether you actually want to know.

Proverbs describes a plan in the heart as deep water and says a person of understanding draws it out. The image is a well. There is something down there and it will not come up on its own.

Drawing takes a bucket and it takes somebody willing to lower it more than once.

Day 9

Don't Accept the First Answer

It's almost never the real one

"The first to plead his case seems right, until another comes and examines him." PROVERBS 18:17

Do not accept the first answer.

I say it as a rule because it needs to be a rule. In the moment it always feels rude to ask again, and it is not.

Here is what the first answer usually is.

It is the socially acceptable version. The one that will not cause trouble. The one that fits what the person believes you want to hear, which they worked out in the two seconds before answering.

None of that is deception. It is ordinary social competence, and everybody does it, including you.

Which means that a family that only ever hears first answers has a picture of itself assembled entirely from the smoothest available versions of everyone.

I have watched a father ask each of his children whether they were interested in the business. Every one of them said something reasonable. Every one of those answers was a first answer, and two of them were wrong, and it took a different setting and a second asking to find out.

Now, the objection I hear is that asking twice signals distrust. I understand it and I think it is backwards.

Asking once signals that you were being polite. Asking twice signals that you actually wanted to know, and people can tell the difference immediately.

Where families get this wrong is in the tone. Asking again as a challenge does communicate distrust. Asking again with interest communicates the opposite.

There is a practical variation worth knowing. The second asking often works better in a different setting. Not at the table with everyone present. In a car. On a walk. On the phone a week later. The change of setting gives somebody permission to give a different answer without contradicting themselves publicly.

The proverb says the first to plead his case seems right until somebody examines him. In a courtroom that is obvious. In a family we skip the examination entirely, because it feels like an accusation.

It is not an accusation. It is the ordinary work of finding out what is actually true.

Day 10

Come Back Around

He'd ask a simple question, then a follow-up

"Let your speech always be with grace, seasoned as it were with salt, so that you will know how you should respond to each person." COLOSSIANS 4:6

This is the last day of the second session, and it is about time rather than technique.

Come back around.

Not in the same conversation. Later. Days later, or weeks later, or in some cases months.

Families treat conversations as events with a beginning and an end. Something is raised, discussed, and concluded, and if it did not resolve, the conclusion is that it cannot be resolved.

The most productive conversations I have been part of were not single conversations. They were the same subject, returned to three or four times, over a stretch, with the person given room in between.

Here is why that works and it is not complicated.

The first time somebody hears a hard question, they answer from wherever they happen to be standing that day. If they are defensive, you get a defended answer. If they are tired, you get a short one.

Given a week, a person thinks. They talk to their spouse. They notice something. And the answer they give the second time is frequently different, and not because they were pressured. Because they had room.

I watched this with a couple where I raised something and the wife said no. I did not press. Months later I raised it again and she said not yet. Later still, she said yes. What she told me afterward was that she had needed the time to be sure of something, and no amount of persuading in the first conversation would have produced it faster.

The instinct in most families is the opposite. If it did not land, press harder, or drop it permanently. Both are the same mistake, which is treating the conversation as a single opportunity.

Paul says let your speech be seasoned with salt so that you know how to respond to each person. Notice the ending. Each person. It assumes different people require different handling and that you are meant to know the difference.

Part of knowing the difference is knowing who needs a week.

That is the second session. Next week is the skill underneath all of it.

Reflect

1. What conversation did you conclude was closed because it did not go well once?

2. Who in your family needs time between askings?

3. When did somebody give you room and it changed your answer?

4. What are you currently pressing that you should let sit?

SESSION 3

Learning to Listen

What happens when someone finally feels heard

Day 11

Give Them Time to Answer

Don't just move to the next question

"Be still before the Lord and wait patiently for Him." PSALM 37:7

Here is where most of us fail, and it is not for lack of caring.

We ask a good question. The person pauses. The pause becomes uncomfortable. And we fill it.

The pause was the whole point. Something was rising and we interrupted it.

I want to describe what is actually happening in that silence, because if you understand it you will find it easier to tolerate.

When somebody is asked a real question, the first thing they retrieve is the prepared answer. That takes about a second. If they do not say it, it is because some part of them knows it is not the true one, and now they are looking for something else.

That search takes four to six seconds, which feels like an eternity in a conversation.

If you fill the space at second three, you have ended the search. The person will accept the rescue gratefully, because being in that silence is uncomfortable for them too, and the conversation moves on with the real answer never having surfaced.

So the discipline is simply to wait longer than is comfortable. Not as a technique. Because that is where the answer is.

There is a version of the same failure that looks different. Some people do not fill the silence with words. They fill it by moving to the next question, which is worse, because it signals that the first one was a formality.

If you have five questions and you get through all five, you probably learned nothing. If you ask one and stay with it, you might.

Now, a word for people who find silence genuinely difficult. It is not a character flaw and it is usually learned. Households where silence meant something was wrong produce adults who cannot leave a gap.

If that is you, the practice is to count. Literally count to six in your head after somebody stops talking. It feels absurd and it works.

The psalm says be still before the Lord and wait patiently. Stillness and waiting are named as things requiring instruction, which suggests they do not come naturally to anybody.

If they have to be commanded before God, they certainly have to be practiced with your family.

Day 12

Listen Empathetically

You actively listen to what they say

"Rejoice with those who rejoice, and weep with those who weep." ROMANS 12:15

There is listening and there is listening empathetically, and families frequently do the first while believing they are doing the second.

The difference is not attention. Plenty of people listen with total attention and are still not listening empathetically, because attention can be entirely evaluative.

Empathetic listening is listening from inside somebody else's position rather than from your own.

Here is the test. When somebody finishes speaking, what is the first thing in your mind?

If it is an assessment, agreement or disagreement, that is evaluative listening. If it is something closer to so that is what that has been like, that is empathetic.

Both are legitimate and only one of them makes a person feel heard.

Now, the practical difficulty in families is that you have history with these people. You know the whole backstory. You were there. Which means you arrive at every conversation with a fully formed context, and the context is doing the interpreting before the person has finished speaking.

That is precisely why families are harder to listen to than strangers. With a stranger you have no choice but to take in what they say. With your brother you already know what he means.

Except sometimes you do not, and by the time you find out, several years have gone by.

Paul's instruction is to rejoice with those who rejoice and weep with those who weep. It is a strange piece of guidance if you think about it, because it does not tell you to fix anything or to counsel anybody.

It tells you to be in the same emotional place they are.

That is empathetic listening described in a sentence, and notice how little it asks and how rarely it is done. Most of us, when somebody is upset, want to move them out of upset as quickly as possible, and we experience that as compassion.

Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is our own discomfort, dressed up.

Staying in the place somebody is, without moving them, is one of the more generous things available in a family.

Day 13

The Pause Was the Point

That's where people begin to think more deeply

"There is a time to be silent and a time to speak." ECCLESIASTES 3:7

I want to give a whole day to something that sounds small, because in practice it is the difference between a conversation that goes somewhere and one that does not.

The pause was the point.

Not the question. The pause after it.

Here is what I have observed across a lot of family conversations. The questions are usually fine. Families are not failing because nobody asks good questions. They are failing because nobody survives the two seconds after one.

And the reason is that silence in a family carries meaning that silence with a stranger does not.

If a stranger pauses, you assume they are thinking. If your son pauses, you may assume he is upset, or that you overstepped, or that he does not want to answer. And because you assume it, you rescue him, and now the moment is gone.

Nine times out of ten he was thinking.

There is a second thing happening in that silence that is worth understanding. When somebody pauses before answering a family question, they are frequently deciding how honest to be. That decision takes a few seconds and it is genuinely difficult.

What you do in those seconds tells them the answer. If you fill the space, you have said the honest version is not required. If you wait, you have said it is welcome.

That is a lot of communication to happen without words, and it happens either way.

Ecclesiastes names a time to be silent as well as a time to speak. It is usually read as guidance about discretion, which it is.

It is also permission. Silence is a legitimate thing to be doing in a conversation. It is not an absence of the conversation and it is not a failure of it.

Most of us were never taught that. We were taught that a good conversationalist keeps things moving, which is exactly wrong for the conversations that matter in a family.

So here is the whole practice for today, and it is uncomfortable rather than difficult.

Ask something real. Then let the silence be longer than you want it to be.

Day 14

The Opportunity to Speak at Any Time

Keeping the conversation respectful

"So then, my beloved brothers and sisters, let everyone be quick to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger." JAMES 1:19

There are a few simple expectations that make a family conversation work, and they are not a long list.

Listening to understand is one. Giving people the opportunity to speak at any time is a second. Keeping the conversation respectful is the third.

That is it. Three things, and when people are clear about the expectations, they are far more comfortable participating.

I want to take the second one, because it is the one families do worst without noticing.

The opportunity to speak at any time means that nobody has to wait to be invited, and nobody has to compete for an opening.

In most families there is a hierarchy of who speaks, and it was established decades ago, and it has nothing to do with who has something worth saying. The loudest speak. The oldest speak. Whoever is most comfortable interrupting speaks. Everyone else waits, and waiting becomes a habit, and the habit becomes a personality.

I have sat in rooms where a person said nothing for an hour and then, invited directly, said the most useful thing anybody had said all day.

They were not withholding. They were waiting for an opening that a conversation like that does not naturally produce.

So the expectation has to be stated, out loud, at the beginning. You can speak at any time. Not, we would love to hear from everyone, which is a pleasantry. The actual expectation, said plainly.

And then it has to be enforced, gently, by whoever is holding the room. If somebody is talked over, you go back to them. If somebody has not spoken in twenty minutes, you ask them directly, by name.

That last part feels awkward and it is the whole job.

James gives the same instruction to a church that would not have been much different from a family. Quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger.

He addresses it to everyone rather than to leaders, which is worth noticing. It is not a rule for whoever is running the meeting. It is a description of how each person in a room is supposed to behave.

A family where everyone held that would not need anyone holding the room at all.

Day 15

You Intimidate Your Kids

Here's a guy who's totally unaware

"Fathers, do not exasperate your children, so that they will not lose heart." COLOSSIANS 3:21

I want to tell you about a conversation that changed how I think about family meetings.

I had interviewed a man's four adult children separately, which is something I do. And then I sat with him and his wife.

I said, you intimidate your kids.

He said, I do not.

His wife said, you absolutely do.

Here was a man who was totally unaware. Not a tyrant. Not harsh. By every account a good father who loved his children and had provided for them well. And all four of them, independently, had described walking into a room with him and becoming careful.

He had no idea, and the reason he had no idea is that nobody had ever told him, and the reason nobody had ever told him is exactly the thing they were describing.

That is the loop. The people best positioned to tell you are the people least able to.

I raise this in the listening session rather than the meetings session because the implication is about hearing rather than about running a room.

If you carry any authority in your family, and most people reading this do, then what you hear from your family has been filtered. Not deceptively. People adjust what they say based on who is listening, and they do it below the level of decision.

Which means that the picture you have of your own family is systematically incomplete, and the parts missing from it are precisely the parts about you.

There are two ways to find out. Ask somebody who will tell you, which usually means a spouse or a sibling rather than a child. Or ask a child directly, in a setting where the answer is possible, and then receive it without defending yourself.

That second one is hard and it is the only version that fixes anything.

Paul tells fathers not to exasperate their children so they will not lose heart. Losing heart is specific. It is not anger. It is quiet resignation, the conclusion that being honest is not worth what it costs.

That conclusion gets reached slowly and it is almost never announced.

Reflect

2. Who in your family becomes careful around you?

3. Has anyone ever tried to tell you something like this?

4. How did you respond, and what did they learn from that?
