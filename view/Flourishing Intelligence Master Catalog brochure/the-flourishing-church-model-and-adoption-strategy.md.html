The Flourishing Church — model built out parallel to The Purpose Driven Church, plus an adoption strategy grounded in how the original 40 Days of Purpose campaign actually scaled

The Flourishing Church

Built Out Parallel to The Purpose Driven Church — Plus How to Get That Kind of Adoption

Part One: The Flourishing Church Model

The Purpose Driven Church wasn't just a book of ideas — it was a working system: a purpose statement, a way of measuring commitment, a way of moving people through stages, and a monthly health check. The Flourishing Church needs the same four pieces, not just the five purposes restated.

1. The Purpose Statement

Saddleback's statement was built once and repeated everywhere: bring people to Jesus, develop them to Christlike maturity, equip them for ministry, and send them into life mission, to magnify God's name. The Flourishing Church needs its own single-sentence version, memorable enough that a member could repeat it without looking it up:

"We exist to help every person flourish in Christ, and become someone through whom others flourish too."

2. The Five Circles of Flourishing Commitment

Warren's five circles measured commitment from the outside in: Community as the unchurched, Crowd as regular attendees, Congregation as members, Committed as maturing members, and Core as lay ministers. The Flourishing Church keeps the same structure — it's a genuinely good model — but names each ring by what's actually happening to the person in it, not just their attendance pattern:

  • Community — Surviving. Not yet connected, but living with the pressures the campaign speaks to directly: anxiety, isolation, financial stress, exhaustion.
  • Crowd — Curious. Shows up, is drawn by the question "Are you flourishing, or just surviving?"
  • Congregation — Committed to Christ, formally part of the church family, has completed the flagship six-week campaign.
  • Committed — Growing. In a small group, working the personalized pathway from the assessment, visibly changing.
  • Core — Multiplying. Trained as a Flourishing Guide or Coach, helping someone else move through the same circles.

3. The Flourishing Diamond

Warren's baseball diamond moved people through four bases — fellowship at first base through baptism, discipleship at second through small groups, ministry at third through service, and a home run through mission and evangelism — with worship as the pitch that drives the whole game. The Flourishing Church can use the same shape, or the tree image already in the source strategy, but the diamond has one advantage the tree doesn't: it's a literal process with stages, not just a metaphor. Recommended adaptation, keeping Warren's four-class numbering convention:

  • 101 — Flourishing Foundations class: the six-week flagship campaign, first base, membership.
  • 201 — Flourishing Groups: ongoing small group life, second base, discipleship.
  • 301 — Flourishing Service: a gifts/readiness assessment placing people into ministry, third base.
  • 401 — Flourishing Mission: sending people out to help someone else flourish, home base.

4. The Flourishing Snapshot

Saddleback's monthly tracking tool identified who was at each base of the discipleship process and how many people sat in each circle of commitment, making bottlenecks easy to spot — for example, if attendance grows but small group participation doesn't, that gap is visible immediately. The Flourishing Church needs the same monthly discipline: a one-page report a pastoral staff reviews together, showing movement (or stalling) through the five circles and four bases. This is the piece most campaigns skip, and it's the piece that turns a six-week event into an actual church health system.

5. The Flourishing Assessment as S.H.A.P.E.

Saddleback's S.H.A.P.E. method placed members into ministry based on their spiritual gifts, heart, abilities, personality, and experience. The Flourishing Faith Assessment already in your source strategy (spiritual vitality, joy, health, relationships, character, purpose, work, finances, generosity, community, resilience, legacy) can serve the same placement function — not just a diagnostic, but the mechanism that tells the church exactly which small group, coach, or ministry a specific person should be routed to next.

Part Two: How to Get That Kind of Adoption

This is the part most movements skip. A good framework doesn't spread on its own — 40 Days of Purpose spread because of specific, repeatable mechanics. Here's what actually drove it, and what the equivalent move is for Flourishing Faith.

1. Ship a complete kit, not a concept

Churches didn't adopt a philosophy — they received sermon outlines keyed to each week's reading and full small group curricula, so the pastor's message, the kitchen-table conversation, and the Tuesday-night group were all orbiting the same idea on the same day. Pastors will not build their own campaign from a framework, however good. The Flourishing Church needs the full kit shipped complete on day one: six sermon outlines, six small-group videos, participant guide, daily readings, assessment, family guide, kids/student editions, host recruitment tools, communications package, testimony videos, celebration weekend — exactly as your source strategy already specifies. Nothing launches until all of it exists.

2. Let a flagship book create bottom-up demand

The book did work the campaign kit alone couldn't: The Purpose-Driven Life sold 15 million copies in seventeen months, outselling The Da Vinci Code two to one. Individuals bought it before their church ever ran the campaign, then asked their pastor to do it. That's a pull strategy, not a push strategy — the book recruits the congregation, and the congregation recruits the pastor. Flourishing Faith needs its own consumer-facing book first, written to be read alone on a nightstand, not just distributed inside a campaign kit.

3. Make the theology cross-denominational on purpose

40 Days of Purpose ran in thousands of evangelical and Baptist churches, but also more than a thousand Reformed, Presbyterian, Lutheran, and Episcopal congregations — groups that disagreed bitterly on baptism, women in ministry, and church governance, all running the same forty days. That breadth wasn't an accident; it required the content to sit above denominational lines. Flourishing Faith's five-purposes foundation already has this built in — the discipline is resisting the temptation to add anything more theologically specific than that as the campaign scales.

4. Organize regional launches, not just individual church launches

Warren personally recruited 300 pastors across the Greater New York area to launch a region-wide campaign together, arguing that a synchronized citywide effort creates a synergistic impact no single church gets alone. The same pattern repeated internationally — more than 532 churches in South Africa alone ran the campaign. A pastor is far more likely to say yes when twenty other pastors in the same city are saying yes at the same time. Build the "Flourishing [City]" model from your own source strategy early, not as a phase-two idea — city coalitions are themselves the recruitment mechanism, not just an outcome.

5. Train leaders in person, at scale

More than 400,000 church leaders were directly trained across 22 languages through Purpose Driven conferences — this is likely the single most underrated driver of adoption. A kit tells a pastor what to do; a conference gives them the confidence and the peer network to actually do it. The Flourishing Guides/Coaches certification tiers already in your strategy need an in-person (or livestreamed) training conference as the delivery mechanism, not just a self-serve online course.

6. Report results a pastor can repeat to the next pastor

The first campaign's numbers were tracked and published: average attendance rose 22%, average giving rose 20%, and small group participation rose 102%. Those specific, believable, non-exaggerated numbers are what one pastor tells another pastor over coffee. Build the Flourishing Snapshot (above) to produce exactly this kind of reportable, aggregable result from every participating church, from campaign one — and publish the aggregate numbers, the way Warren did.

7. Recognize adopting churches publicly

Purpose Driven ran an annual Church Health Award recognizing congregations from dozens of states and countries for excellence in adapting the model to their own context. This does two things at once: it rewards early adopters with visibility, and it signals to hesitant pastors that adopting the model is a mark of health, not a loss of independence. Build a "Flourishing Church Award" into year one, not year five.

8. Sequence the follow-on campaign before momentum drops

Purpose Driven followed its flagship with a second campaign, 40 Days of Community, explicitly positioned as the next step in spiritual growth for congregations that had already completed the first forty days. The six-week flagship campaign in your source strategy needs its sequel already built — not written after demand appears, but ready to ship the moment a church finishes week six, so the Flourishing Pathway's four quarters aren't a someday-plan but the actual next email in the pastor's inbox.

9. Start with a small number of undeniable pilot churches

Movements spread through trust, not marketing. The strategy should recruit a small number of respected senior pastors and well-known, credible churches first — and refuse to go wide until those pilots produce results real enough to be worth talking about. National visibility is the result of pilot-church success, never the starting move.

The Short Version

Purpose Driven didn't scale because the five purposes were a good idea — plenty of good theological ideas never leave the seminary. It scaled because Warren built a complete kit, wrote a book that created demand before he ever pitched a pastor, kept the theology ecumenical enough to cross denominational lines, organized cities instead of just churches, trained hundreds of thousands of leaders in person, measured and published believable results, publicly rewarded adopters, sequenced the next campaign before momentum died, and proved it with a handful of undeniable pilot churches before going national. The Flourishing Church needs all nine, not just the framework.
