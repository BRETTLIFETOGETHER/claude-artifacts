SESSION 1

The Advisor as Conversation Guide

Shifting from Expert to Facilitator

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

Brett: Welcome to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, and I'm here with Tom Conway, who has sat with more families in this space than just about anyone over the last thirty years. We're starting a new series on communication, on equipping families to have the conversations that actually matter.

Here's why this one sits at the center of everything. You can have a great plan, you can have everything organized, you can even have alignment on paper. But if the conversation doesn't actually happen, nothing changes in the life of the family. So Tom, let me start you here. When an advisor moves from being the expert in the room to being a guide, what are you really helping them understand?

Tom: You're helping them shift from providing answers to guiding a process. Most advisors are trained to analyze and recommend. That's where their confidence comes from. But when you're working with families, especially around legacy, the real work happens through conversation. So your role becomes creating space, asking the right questions, and helping people think more clearly together.

The advisor needs to see themselves as a discipler, not just a deal-maker. A spiritual planner, not just a financial planner.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's a big shift, and it's not just semantic. If you still see yourself primarily as the expert, you're going to default to answers. You're going to try to move things forward by solving. But if you see yourself as a guide, everything changes. The way you ask questions changes, the way you listen changes, even your sense of success in the conversation changes.

You're still leading. You're just not leading by directing. And I think that's where people feel the tension, because it feels like you're doing less when you're actually doing something much more important.

Tom: That's exactly right. It's a posture of listening and guiding rather than directing. You're still bringing expertise, but you're not leading with answers. You're helping the family discover what matters and how to move forward. And that starts with listening. Really listening.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's harder than it sounds, because most of us are wired to fill the space. We hear something and we immediately want to respond, fix it, move it forward. But here, the space actually matters.

Tom: It does. In this context, the conversation itself becomes the work. It's not just a step toward the solution. It's where understanding develops, where alignment begins, and where people start to think differently.

Families don't talk about what matters. And there are a whole bunch of reasons why they don't. If those conversations don't happen, the deeper issues never really get addressed.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's the reality a lot of advisors walk into. On the surface everything looks fine. There's structure, there's planning, there's progress. But underneath that, there are things that haven't been said, assumptions that haven't been tested, expectations nobody has clarified. And if no one creates space for those conversations, they just sit there until they show up in a decision, or in conflict, or in confusion. So the issue usually isn't a lack of information. It's a lack of conversation.

Tom: That's right. And part of your role is to create space for participation. You're helping each person engage, asking questions, making sure people feel heard. Because when people feel heard, they become more open, and that's when real conversation begins.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's where you start to see what's actually happening in the room. You notice who speaks easily and who hesitates, who feels confident and who holds back, who has been thinking about these things and who is hearing them for the first time. You're not forcing anything. You're creating an environment where people can step in at their own pace and still feel invited.

Tom: That's right, and it requires patience. You ask a question, and then you give space. You don't rush to fill it. Most people are uncomfortable with silence, but that space is often where people begin to think more deeply.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That silence can feel long in the moment, but it's actually where something is forming. It's where people are sorting through what they really think, or something they've never said out loud. And if you move too quickly, you miss it.

Tom: Exactly. And as the conversation develops, you can begin to provide perspective. Not by telling people what to do, but by offering alternatives. You might say, here are a couple of ways families approach this, or here are some options to consider. You're expanding their thinking without taking ownership of the decision.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's a key distinction. You're still bringing your experience, you're still bringing insight, but you're not replacing their thinking. You're helping them think more clearly rather than thinking for them.

Tom: Exactly. And in many cases you're helping them step back and consider a deeper question. What does God want you to do? Because these decisions aren't just financial. They're connected to purpose, values, and stewardship. Part of your role is helping them reflect on that in a way that's meaningful.

[CUE] CASE STUDY

Brett: Tom, let me make this practical. Give me a picture of what it looks like when an advisor stays in the expert seat.

Tom: You've got a family that has everything in place. Good documents, good structure, a competent team around them. The advisor has done exactly what he was trained to do, and he has done it well.

Then a question comes up about the future. And instead of opening it up, he answers it. He gives them his recommendation, because that's what he's paid for and that's what feels helpful in the moment.

Brett: And the family accepts it.

Tom: They accept it, because he's the expert. But nobody in that room said what they actually thought. The wife had a view she never voiced. One of the children had a concern he never raised. And the decision gets made without any of that surfacing.

Brett: So the plan moves forward.

Tom: The plan moves forward, and the family doesn't. And two years later when something has to change, none of the understanding is there, because it was never built. The advisor solved the problem and missed the opportunity.

[CUE] SCENARIOS

Brett: Before we wrap this session, Tom, let me walk you through a couple of scenarios that advisors are going to run into.

First one. An advisor is sitting with a family and asks a good question. Nobody answers. The silence goes about eight seconds and it feels like a minute. What does he do?

Tom: Nothing. That's the answer. He waits. Eight seconds feels like forever to the person who asked, and it feels like about two seconds to the people thinking about it. If he fills that silence, he has just taught the family that they don't have to answer his questions, and he'll spend the rest of the engagement doing all the talking.

Brett: Second one. The advisor asks the question, and one person in the room answers every single time. Same voice, every question.

Tom: He needs to redirect by name, and he needs to do it kindly. Something like, I'd like to hear how you're thinking about this. Because if he doesn't, he's going to walk out of there believing he understands the family when what he actually understands is one person. And the people who stayed quiet will have learned that this is not a room where they speak.

[CUE] BOTH CENTER

Tom: You don't have to do this perfectly. You just have to begin creating space for better conversations.

Brett: Tom, this is where so much of your work lives, and it's the part that doesn't show up on a statement anywhere.

Here's the question to sit with. When you step into a conversation, are you trying to lead with answers, or are you creating space for something meaningful to emerge? Because the quality of the conversation will often determine the quality of the outcome.

We've got additional resources online to help you put this to work with the families you serve. In the next session, we're going to look at why so many families avoid these conversations altogether.

SESSION 2

Understanding Communication Barriers

Why Families Avoid Important Conversations

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

Brett: Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, I'm with Tom, and we're glad you're here.

In the last session we talked about the advisor's role as a guide. But before we go further there's something we have to acknowledge. Even when everything is in place, even when the opportunity is right there, a lot of families still don't have these conversations. So Tom, what are you actually seeing? Why is it that families so often don't talk about what matters most?

Tom: There are a few consistent barriers, and most of them are not technical. They're relational and emotional.

One of the biggest is discomfort. These conversations touch on money, expectations, roles, and sometimes deeper issues like trust or past experiences. People don't always know how to bring those things up in a healthy way, so they avoid them. And when people don't know how to start, they tend to wait. They assume there will be a better time later.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That reframes the issue, because it's not that families don't care and it's not that they're unwilling. It's that they don't know how to enter the conversation without creating tension, or saying the wrong thing, or opening something they're not sure how to handle. So instead of stepping into it, they step around it. And over time, that becomes the pattern.

Tom: Exactly. And another barrier is fear. Fear of conflict, fear of being misunderstood, fear of saying something that creates tension. So people stay quiet to keep things comfortable.

There's a principle in Ephesians 4:25. Speak truthfully to your neighbor. That applies within families as well. But speaking truth requires both courage and the right environment.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's where it gets interesting, because the thing people are trying to preserve is comfort, and comfort is exactly what keeps the conversation from happening. It works for a while. It keeps things smooth on the surface. But underneath, things are building. Unspoken expectations, different assumptions, questions nobody feels safe asking.

Tom: That's right. Avoiding the conversation may feel easier in the moment, but it usually creates more difficulty later.

I worked with a family where this became very clear. The parents had strong values and a clear vision for the long term, but they had never really communicated it to their children. They assumed the children understood. The children had their own assumptions. They weren't sure what their parents expected, and they didn't feel comfortable asking. So everyone was operating with a different understanding, and nobody was talking about it.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And from the outside that family probably looked aligned. Things were moving forward, there wasn't obvious conflict. But underneath there were completely different interpretations of what was happening, and nobody knew it because nobody had said it out loud.

Tom: Exactly. It stayed hidden until a decision had to be made. At that point the lack of communication became very visible, and it created tension that could have been avoided if the conversation had happened earlier.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: So the issue wasn't disagreement. It was the absence of clarity. And that's an important distinction, because a lot of families assume the risk is conflict. The bigger risk is silence.

Tom: That's right. Another barrier is assumption. People think they already understand each other, so they don't feel the need to talk about it. But assumptions are rarely as accurate as people think.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: So everyone is operating with a version of the story, and nobody has checked whether those versions match. Over time those differences start to show up in decisions, in reactions, in how people interpret things.

Tom: Exactly. And there's the issue of timing. People think, we'll talk about that later, especially when it comes to legacy or transition. But waiting usually makes it harder rather than easier.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: Because later feels safer. It feels less urgent. It feels like there will be more clarity, more time, more readiness. What actually happens is the opposite. The stakes get higher, the decisions get bigger, the emotions get stronger, and now the conversation is harder than it would have been.

Tom: That's exactly right. And this is where your role as a guide becomes important. You're helping families move past those barriers, creating a space where it feels safe to talk, and helping them take the first step.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And you're not trying to fix everything in one conversation. You're not trying to resolve every issue or align every perspective. You're helping them take a step, from avoiding the conversation to entering into it.

Tom: Exactly. And at the heart of this, communication is one of the clearest ways a family expresses care for one another. Taking the time to talk, to listen, to understand, that's how trust gets built. So when communication is missing, it's not just a gap in information. It's a gap in connection.

[CUE] CASE STUDY

Brett: Tom, give me one where the barrier wasn't fear or conflict. Something quieter.

Tom: I sat with a couple where the husband had been very clear with me about what he wanted. He had thought it through carefully over a number of years. He had never told his wife any of it.

Brett: Not out of secrecy.

Tom: Not at all. He assumed she knew. They had been married almost forty years, and he figured after that much time it was understood. And when we finally put it on the table, she had a completely different picture in her mind of what they were going to do.

Brett: So what happened in the room?

Tom: She wasn't angry. She was surprised. And then she said something that I've thought about ever since. She said, I wish you had told me that twenty years ago, because I would have made some different decisions.

Brett: That's the cost right there.

Tom: That's the cost. Not a fight. Twenty years of a woman making decisions inside a picture that wasn't accurate. And it was not because he was hiding anything. It was because nobody ever created the moment where he would say it out loud.

[CUE] SCENARIOS

Brett: Two scenarios before we wrap, Tom.

An advisor raises one of these topics with a client and the client deflects. Changes the subject, says now isn't a good time. What does he do?

Tom: He lets it go, and he writes down the date. Pushing in that moment costs him the relationship and gains him nothing. But he comes back to it in six months, and if he has to, he comes back again after that. Most of the significant things I've seen a family decide came on the second or third asking, not the first.

Brett: Second one. The advisor is sitting with parents and adult children, and it's clear the parents believe the children understand something they clearly do not understand.

Tom: He should not correct anybody in that room. What he does is ask the question that surfaces it without assigning blame. Something like, let's make sure we're all working from the same understanding. Can we walk through it? Now the gap comes out as information rather than as somebody's mistake, and nobody has to defend anything.

[CUE] BOTH CENTER

Tom: You don't have to remove every barrier at once. You just have to begin moving through them.

Brett: Tom, that picture of a family looking completely aligned on the outside while everyone is operating from a different story is one that a lot of advisors are going to recognize immediately.

Here's the question to sit with. Are there conversations in your family that haven't happened, not because they don't matter, but because something has been getting in the way? And if so, what would it look like to take the first step?

In the next session we're going to talk about how to actually start those conversations in a way that creates safety and trust.

SESSION 3

Starting the Right Conversations

Creating Safety and Trust

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

Brett: Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we're grateful you're with us.

Last session we talked about why families avoid these conversations. At some point you have to move from understanding the barriers to actually stepping into one, and that's where it stops being theoretical. So Tom, what makes the difference between a conversation that opens people up and one that shuts them down?

Tom: It usually comes down to safety and trust. If people don't feel safe, they won't really engage. They may respond, but they won't open up.

So the first goal is not to get to the right answer. It's to create the right environment.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's a shift, because most people think the conversation starts with what you say. What you're saying is it starts before that. It starts with how it feels. And I think that's where a lot of conversations go off track. Not because the topic is wrong, but because the environment isn't right.

Tom: Exactly. It starts with how the conversation is framed. If it feels like pressure, evaluation, or expectation, people hold back. If it feels like an opportunity to share and be heard, they engage.

There's a principle in Proverbs 18:13. To answer before listening, that is folly. That applies here. You're not starting with answers. You're starting with listening.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's hard, because most people walk into a conversation already thinking about what they want to say or where they want it to go. Here you're almost doing the opposite. You're creating space for something to emerge instead of trying to control it.

Tom: That's right. You're not trying to drive the conversation to an outcome. You're trying to create space for understanding. And that changes how you begin. It starts with simple, open-ended questions. Not questions that lead to a specific answer, but questions that invite someone to share their perspective.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's where people overcomplicate it. They think they need the perfect question or the perfect setup, when really it's about posture. It's about whether the question creates pressure or creates space.

Tom: Exactly. I worked with a family where this was very clear. There had been tension between the parents and their adult children, and previous conversations had not gone well. They turned into silence or into disagreement.

So when we sat down, instead of starting with structure or decisions, we started with a simple question. What would you like your parents to understand about how you're thinking right now? And then we stopped and listened.

At first there was hesitation. But as one person shared, it created space for the others. And for the first time they began to hear each other without immediately reacting.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's a powerful moment, because nothing changed structurally. There wasn't a new plan, there wasn't a new decision. The only thing that changed was how the conversation started. And that changed everything.

Tom: That's right. The goal in those early moments is not agreement. It's understanding. When people feel understood, they become more open, and once there's openness you can begin to move toward alignment over time.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's a big shift, because most people think the goal is to get everyone on the same page right away. What you're saying is that comes later. First, people need to feel heard.

Tom: Exactly. And part of that is recognizing that people process things differently. Some are quick to speak. Others need time to think. Some are more direct, others more reflective. If you don't account for those differences, you can unintentionally create pressure that shuts people down.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's where you really see the dynamics in a room. You've got one person ready to jump in right away, and another still processing the question. If you move too quickly you end up hearing from the same voices every time and missing the others completely.

Tom: That's right. And this is where your posture matters as much as your words. You're listening. You're not interrupting. You're not reacting too quickly. And you're acknowledging what people say even when you don't agree with it. That's what builds trust.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's where the conversation starts to feel different. It's not about being right. It's not about winning a point. It's about understanding each other even when perspectives are different.

Tom: Exactly. There are a couple of well-known ideas that reinforce this. One is that the biggest problem in communication is the assumption that it has taken place. The other is that people don't listen to understand, they listen to respond. Both of those show up in families all the time, and part of your role is helping shift that.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: Because most conversations break down not because people don't care, but because they're not actually hearing each other. They're reacting, they're interpreting, they're responding. They're not slowing down long enough to understand. And that's where trust starts to break down.

Tom: That's right. A good start creates momentum. You don't have to solve everything in one conversation. You just have to begin in a way that builds safety and trust.

[CUE] CASE STUDY

Brett: Tom, let me have you contrast two starts, because I think advisors will feel the difference immediately.

Tom: First one. An advisor sits a family down and opens with the agenda. Here's what we need to accomplish today, here are the decisions in front of us, here's where we need to land. Reasonable. Efficient. And everyone in that room now knows exactly what the right answer is supposed to look like.

Brett: So they perform.

Tom: They perform. They give the answers they think are expected. And the advisor walks out thinking that went well.

Second family, same situation. He opens differently. He says, before we talk about any decisions, I'd like to understand how each of you is thinking about this. And then he asks one open question and waits.

Brett: What's different?

Tom: The first fifteen minutes are slower and less comfortable. But by the end of that meeting he knows things about that family that the first advisor will never learn. Same amount of time. Completely different opening move.

[CUE] SCENARIOS

Brett: Two scenarios, Tom.

An advisor asks a good opening question and one of the adult children gives an answer that is clearly not what the parents want to hear. The room tightens.

Tom: He thanks him. Genuinely. Something like, I appreciate you saying that, that's helpful. Because every other person in that room is watching to see what happens to the first one who is honest. If that gets punished, even with a look, nobody else says anything real for the rest of the meeting.

Brett: Second one. An advisor has a family where a previous conversation went badly, and now there's real reluctance to try again.

Tom: Start smaller than he thinks he needs to. Not the full family, not the big topic. One person, one question, no agenda. What he's rebuilding is not the conversation. It's the belief that a conversation can go well, and that gets rebuilt one good experience at a time.

[CUE] BOTH CENTER

Tom: You don't have to get everything right. You just have to create the right environment to begin.

Brett: Tom, the family that had never heard each other until somebody asked a different opening question, that's the whole session right there.

Here's the question to sit with. When you start a conversation, are you creating space for people to open up, or are you unintentionally creating pressure that causes them to hold back? Because the way you start often determines everything that follows.

In the next session we're going to look at how to navigate conversations when things get more difficult, when expectations, emotions, and potential conflict start to show up.

SESSION 4

Navigating Difficult Topics

Handling Wealth, Expectations, and Conflict

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

Brett: Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, I'm here with Tom, and we're grateful you've joined us.

Last session we talked about starting the right conversations, creating safety and trust so people are willing to engage. But once those conversations begin, they don't stay easy. Every family eventually hits topics that carry more weight. So Tom, when a conversation moves from comfortable to difficult, what's really happening underneath that?

Tom: It becomes personal. When you're talking about wealth, you're not just talking about numbers. You're talking about meaning. You're talking about responsibility, fairness, and sometimes identity.

And people see it very differently. One person may see it as security. Another sees opportunity. Someone else may feel responsibility, or even pressure. So when those perspectives come into the conversation, it can feel like conflict even when that wasn't anyone's intention.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's where things escalate quickly. Because people think they're talking about the same thing, and they're not. They're reacting to what it means to them. If that hasn't been surfaced before, it can feel like it came out of nowhere, when in reality it's been there the whole time.

Tom: That's right. And when those differences come out, the instinct is to respond quickly or defend a position. That usually makes things worse.

There's a principle in Proverbs 15:1. A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger. How you respond in those moments matters as much as what you say.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's hard, because in real time people don't feel gentle. They feel reactive. They feel misunderstood. They feel like they need to clarify or defend what they meant. So instead of slowing down, they speed up. That's usually when the conversation starts to break.

Tom: Exactly. So part of navigating difficult topics is slowing the conversation down. You make sure people feel heard before moving forward. You help shift the focus from being right to understanding each other.

And sometimes that means separating the conversation. There are times when it's helpful to talk without money on the table, where you're just dealing with values, perspectives, and understanding. Then there are times when you bring the financial side into it. If you combine those too early, it creates pressure that makes it harder for people to engage.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's a big one, because a lot of families jump straight into the financial conversation. Numbers, structures, distribution, without first building the relational understanding around it. So now you've got weight without context. And that's when people react.

Tom: That's right. I worked with a family where this became very clear. There was a question about how wealth would eventually be distributed. The parents had a clear idea and had never communicated it. The children each had their own expectations, and those expectations were different.

When the conversation finally happened, there was tension almost immediately. One of the children felt like things weren't fair. Another felt like he had taken on more responsibility and should be recognized for it. Handled poorly, that could have created long-term division.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's the moment a lot of families are trying to avoid, because they can feel that outcome coming. So instead of stepping into the conversation, they just don't have it. But like we talked about, avoiding it doesn't remove it. It delays it.

Tom: Exactly. So we slowed it down. Instead of trying to resolve the issue right away, we focused on understanding each perspective. Each person had the opportunity to explain how they were thinking and why.

At first there was defensiveness. But as people began to listen rather than just respond, something shifted. They didn't immediately agree. They began to understand each other. And that changed the tone of the conversation.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's an important distinction. The win wasn't agreement. The win was that the conversation didn't break. It stayed intact long enough for understanding to start happening.

Tom: That's right. There's also a principle in James 1:19. Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak. That's especially important in difficult conversations, because if you reverse it, quick to speak and slow to listen, you usually get escalation.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And expectations come into it too. A lot of tension is not about wealth. It's about what people expected would happen. Those expectations are often unspoken, so when reality doesn't match, it feels personal.

Tom: That's right. And this becomes more complex when you add something like a family business. Now you're not just talking about wealth. You're talking about roles, contribution, leadership, and future involvement. Some may want to be involved and others may not. If that hasn't been clarified, it creates confusion and tension very quickly.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: And that's where you see multiple layers at once. Ownership questions, contribution questions, fairness questions, and relational dynamics all mixed together. Without structure it can feel overwhelming.

Tom: Exactly. So your role is to help guide both the content and the pace. You're helping people stay grounded. You might say, let's take a moment and make sure we understand what's being said. Even something that simple can change the direction of the conversation.

[CUE] CASE STUDY

Brett: Tom, give me one where the difficulty wasn't about the money at all.

Tom: Two brothers, both in the family business. One of them had been running it day to day for about twelve years. The other had a role but was not carrying the same load, and everyone in the family knew it without ever saying it.

The conversation on the table was about ownership. And every time it came up, it went sideways within about five minutes.

Brett: What was underneath it?

Tom: The brother running the business did not actually want more stock. What he wanted was for somebody in that family to say out loud that he had been carrying it. Twelve years and nobody had ever said it.

Brett: So the money was the thing they had to fight about.

Tom: The money was available to fight about. It was not the issue. And when his father finally acknowledged what he had been doing, in the room, in front of everybody, the ownership conversation took about fifteen minutes and they landed it without any trouble at all.

Brett: Twelve years for one sentence.

Tom: Twelve years for one sentence. And that is more common than people would think.

[CUE] SCENARIOS

Brett: Two scenarios before we close this one out, Tom.

An advisor is in a family meeting and two people start going at each other. Voices are up. What does he do?

Tom: He does not referee and he does not take a side. What he does is slow it down and put understanding back in the middle. Something like, I want to make sure I'm following. Let me hear what each of you is saying. That does two things. It stops the escalation without anybody losing face, and it converts an argument back into information.

Brett: Second one. A parent says something in the meeting that clearly lands hard on one of the children. Everybody sees it. Nobody says anything.

Tom: Do not let that pass. If it sits there, it becomes the thing that family remembers about the meeting. He can say something like, I want to pause here, because I think that mattered. Then let the person respond if they want to. What he is protecting is not anybody's feelings. He is protecting the possibility of another conversation.

[CUE] BOTH CENTER

Tom: Difficult conversations are not something to avoid. They're something to navigate. And how you navigate them determines whether they create division or deeper understanding.

Brett: Tom, the brother who did not want more stock, he wanted to be seen. That's the kind of thing you only find by staying in the conversation longer than is comfortable.

Here's the question to sit with. When difficult topics come up in your family, especially around wealth, expectations, or roles, do those conversations move toward understanding, or do they shut down and escalate? And if they've been avoided, what would it look like to begin approaching them differently?

In the next session we're going to talk about facilitating these conversations in a group setting, bringing the whole family together in a way that creates clarity and alignment.

SESSION 5

Facilitating Family Conversations

Leading Conversations that Create Alignment

[CUE] BRETT AND TOM CENTER

Brett: Welcome back to Family Legacy Coaching by Design. My name is Brett, this is Tom, and we're glad you're back with us.

We've talked about navigating difficult topics one on one. Now we move into something very practical. How do you actually bring a family together and facilitate a conversation with multiple perspectives in the room? So Tom, when you're facilitating at that level, what are you really trying to accomplish?

Tom: You're helping the family think together. That's the goal.

Not just sharing information, and not just reacting to one another, but creating a setting where people can listen, understand each other, and begin to move toward alignment. Most families don't naturally do that. Conversations tend to happen in smaller groups or around specific issues. When you bring everyone together intentionally, it creates a different kind of dynamic, and that's where clarity begins to develop.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER

Brett: That's a big shift, because most families are used to fragmented conversations. Side conversations, one on one conversations, or conversations that happen in reaction to something. This is different. This is intentional, it's structured, it's bringing everyone into the same space with a shared purpose. That alone changes the tone.

Tom: Exactly, and how that space is created matters a lot. There's a principle in 1 Corinthians 14:40. Everything should be done in a fitting and orderly way. That applies here. Structure creates clarity, and clarity allows people to engage more fully.

[CUE] BRETT CENTER
